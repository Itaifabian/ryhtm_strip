# Ch 51 — Gastrointestinal Bleeding: practice questions

**1.** A 36-year-old man comes to the emergency department after one black stool. Heart rate is 78/min and blood pressure is 128/80 mm Hg. Hemoglobin is 14.2 g/dL and BUN is 14 mg/dL. He has no syncope, liver disease or heart failure. His Glasgow-Blatchford score is 1. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?

A. Admission to the ICU and urgent endoscopy within 6 hours
B. Discharge with outpatient management
C. Transfuse 1 unit of packed red cells
D. Emergent CT angiography
E. Admit for 3 days of IV PPI infusion

**2.** A 64-year-old woman without cardiovascular disease is admitted with melena. After fluids she is hemodynamically stable. Her hemoglobin is 7.6 g/dL. Which of the following is the most appropriate transfusion approach?

A. Transfuse to a hemoglobin target of 10 g/dL
B. Transfuse 2 units now
C. Transfuse to keep hemoglobin above 9 g/dL
D. Transfuse only after endoscopy
E. Withhold transfusion unless hemoglobin falls below 7 g/dL

**3.** A 58-year-old man with melena undergoes endoscopy, which shows a duodenal ulcer with a nonbleeding visible vessel. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?

A. Endoscopic hemostatic therapy followed by high-dose PPI for 3 days
B. Once-daily oral PPI and discharge after endoscopy
C. High-dose PPI alone without endoscopic therapy
D. Immediate surgical referral
E. Octreotide infusion and ceftriaxone

**4.** A 46-year-old woman with hematemesis is hemodynamically stable with no significant comorbidities. Endoscopy within 24 hours shows a clean-based gastric ulcer. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?

A. Endoscopic clip placement and admission for 3 days
B. High-dose PPI infusion for 72 hours
C. Repeat endoscopy in 24 hours
D. Once-daily oral PPI, regular diet, and discharge after endoscopy if no other reason for admission
E. Interventional radiology embolization

**5.** A 55-year-old man with alcoholic cirrhosis presents with hematemesis. Heart rate is 112/min and blood pressure is 98/60 mm Hg. Resuscitation is under way and endoscopy is being arranged. Which medications should be given at presentation?

A. High-dose PPI alone
B. Nonselective β-blocker and nitrate
C. IV ceftriaxone and IV octreotide
D. Tranexamic acid and vitamin K only
E. Erythromycin alone

**6.** A 60-year-old woman with cirrhosis (Child-Pugh class C, score 11) has esophageal varices that are actively bleeding at endoscopy. Bleeding is controlled with band ligation, and she is on octreotide and antibiotics. Which of the following has been shown to reduce rebleeding and mortality in this setting?

A. Balloon tamponade for 48 hours
B. Early TIPS
C. Discontinuing octreotide after 12 hours
D. Adding a high-dose PPI infusion as the only additional measure
E. Starting a nonselective β-blocker immediately in place of other therapy

**7.** A 70-year-old man with a drug-eluting coronary stent placed 2 years ago for myocardial infarction takes low-dose aspirin. He presents with a bleeding gastric ulcer, which is treated endoscopically with secure hemostasis. Which of the following is the most appropriate plan for aspirin?

A. Stop aspirin permanently
B. Hold aspirin for 8 weeks
C. Switch aspirin to an NSAID
D. Replace aspirin with clopidogrel for 1 year, then stop
E. Restart aspirin once hemostasis is confirmed

**8.** A 68-year-old man presents with large-volume maroon blood per rectum, heart rate 124/min, and blood pressure 86/52 mm Hg. After resuscitation begins, which of the following is the most appropriate first diagnostic procedure?

A. Upper endoscopy
B. Colonoscopy after bowel prep
C. Capsule endoscopy
D. Flexible sigmoidoscopy
E. Technetium-labeled RBC scan

**9.** A 79-year-old woman has ongoing brisk hematochezia with persistent hemodynamic instability despite resuscitation. Upper endoscopy is negative, and she can't tolerate bowel preparation. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Unprepped colonoscopy at the bedside
B. Capsule endoscopy
C. Barium enema
D. CT angiography
E. Observation

**10.** A 72-year-old man has iron-deficiency anemia and intermittent melena. Upper endoscopy and colonoscopy are unrevealing. He is hemodynamically stable with no suspected stricture. Which of the following is generally the next test?

A. Exploratory laparotomy
B. Repeat colonoscopy in 3 years
C. Video capsule endoscopy
D. Meckel scan
E. Barium enema

**11.** An 80-year-old man has recurrent bleeding from small-intestinal and colonic vascular ectasias. He also has severe aortic stenosis. Which intervention appears to reduce his bleeding?

A. Long-term high-dose PPI
B. Aortic valve replacement
C. Daily aspirin
D. Total colectomy
E. Increased dietary iron alone

**12.** A 52-year-old man arrives 1 hour after vomiting large amounts of bright red blood. He is tachycardic and orthostatic, but his hemoglobin is 13.8 g/dL. Which of the following best explains the near-normal hemoglobin?

A. The bleeding has stopped completely
B. A laboratory error
C. Chronic compensated anemia
D. Hemoconcentration from vomiting
E. Plasma and red cells are lost proportionately, and equilibration takes up to 72 hours

**13.** A 76-year-old woman has sudden, painless, large-volume hematochezia. Colonoscopy shows extensive diverticulosis without active bleeding, and the bleeding stops. Which of the following statements about diverticular bleeding is correct?

A. It stops spontaneously in most patients (≥90% in Western series)
B. It typically presents as chronic occult bleeding
C. It usually comes from the rectum
D. It is usually painful
E. It recurs in most patients within 1 year

**14.** Which of the following critically ill ICU patients meets guideline criteria for stress ulcer prophylaxis with a PPI?

A. A patient on mechanical ventilation receiving enteral nutrition, with no other risk factors
B. A patient with sepsis alone
C. A patient with acute kidney injury alone
D. A patient on mechanical ventilation without enteral nutrition
E. A patient with a platelet count of 120,000/µL alone

**15.** A 61-year-old man is hospitalized with hematemesis and melena. He has high-risk clinical features but stabilizes with resuscitation. When should upper endoscopy be performed?

A. Within 2 hours of arrival in all high-risk patients
B. After 72 hours, once hemoglobin has equilibrated
C. Within 24 hours; more urgent endoscopy within 6–12 hours doesn't improve outcomes
D. Only if bleeding recurs
E. After 5 days of PPI therapy

## Answers

**1. B** — A Glasgow-Blatchford score of 0–1 identifies very low risk (~1% need transfusion, intervention or die). Discharge from the ED with outpatient management is suggested.
- A: ICU admission and urgent endoscopy are for higher-risk patients.
- C: Transfusion isn't indicated with normal hemoglobin.
- D: CTA is for severe lower or small-bowel bleeding.
- E: Admission isn't needed at this risk level.
Source: Ch 0051, Initial Risk Assessment; Table 51-1

**2. E** — A restrictive threshold (transfuse below 7 g/dL) reduced rebleeding and death in acute upper GI bleeding compared with a threshold of 9 g/dL.
- A: A target of 10 is liberal and worse.
- B: She's above the threshold.
- C: A threshold of 9 was inferior.
- D: The threshold applies regardless of endoscopy timing.
Source: Ch 0051, Initial Assessment

**3. A** — Active bleeding or a nonbleeding visible vessel warrants endoscopic therapy, followed by high-dose PPI (e.g., 80 mg bolus + 8 mg/h or intermittent dosing) for 3 days. This reduces rebleeding and mortality.
- B: This is low-risk management.
- C: High-risk stigmata need endoscopic therapy.
- D: Surgery is avoided with endoscopic therapy.
- E: Octreotide and ceftriaxone are for variceal or cirrhotic bleeding.
Source: Ch 0051, Peptic Ulcers; Fig 51-1

**4. D** — Clean-based ulcers have rebleeding rates near zero. Stable patients without another reason for admission get standard oral PPI and can be discharged after endoscopy.
- A: Clean-based ulcers don't need endoscopic therapy.
- B: High-dose infusion is for high-risk stigmata.
- C: Second-look endoscopy isn't indicated.
- E: Embolization isn't indicated.
Source: Ch 0051, Peptic Ulcers; Fig 51-1

**5. C** — Cirrhotic patients presenting with UGIB should get an antibiotic (e.g., ceftriaxone) and an IV vasoactive drug (e.g., octreotide) at presentation. Antibiotics reduce infection, rebleeding and mortality, and vasoactive drugs improve early bleeding control.
- A: A PPI doesn't treat variceal bleeding.
- B: β-blockers are for long-term secondary prevention, not the acute bleed.
- D: These aren't the recommended initial therapy.
- E: Erythromycin improves visualization but isn't the key therapy.
Source: Ch 0051, Pre-Endoscopic Medications; Esophageal Varices

**6. B** — TIPS is suggested for acute variceal bleeding in advanced liver disease (Child-Pugh B 8–9 with active bleeding, or C 10–13). Trials show significant reductions in rebleeding and mortality versus standard therapy.
- A: The source doesn't recommend balloon tamponade.
- C: Vasoactive therapy continues 2–5 days.
- D: A PPI doesn't address variceal bleeding.
- E: β-blockers are for long-term prevention, combined with ligation.
Source: Ch 0051, Esophageal Varices

**7. E** — Patients with established cardiovascular disease on aspirin for secondary prevention shouldn't stop it. If it's held, restart once hemostasis is confirmed. Immediate reinstitution lowered 8-week mortality (1% vs 13%).
- A: Stopping is appropriate only for primary prevention.
- B: Delay raised mortality in the trial.
- C: NSAIDs raise ulcer risk.
- D: This isn't supported by the source.
Source: Ch 0051, Peptic Ulcers

**8. A** — Patients with hematochezia and hemodynamic instability should have upper endoscopy to rule out an upper GI source before evaluating the lower tract. A brisk upper bleed can present as hematochezia.
- B: Colonoscopy comes after excluding an upper source.
- C: Capsule isn't for unstable patients.
- D: Sigmoidoscopy is for young patients with minor bleeding.
- E: A tagged RBC scan isn't first-line here.
Source: Ch 0051, Evaluation and Management of LGIB; Fig 51-2

**9. D** — When ongoing, hemodynamically significant hematochezia prevents bowel prep and colonoscopy, CT angiography is suggested. Extravasation leads to angiography and embolization.
- A: Unprepped colonoscopy is unlikely to localize the bleeding.
- B: Capsule is for stable small-bowel evaluation.
- C: Barium is contraindicated in acute bleeding.
- E: Observation is inappropriate with instability.
Source: Ch 0051, Evaluation and Management of LGIB

**10. C** — For suspected small-intestinal bleeding without severe hemorrhage, video capsule endoscopy is generally the next test (yield ~55%).
- A: Surgery is reserved for severe recurrent bleeding with negative tests.
- B: Repeat colonoscopy in 3 years misses the small bowel.
- D: A Meckel scan is mainly for young patients.
- E: Barium enema evaluates the colon, not the small bowel.
Source: Ch 0051, Evaluation and Management of Small-Intestinal or Obscure GIB

**11. B** — Patients with Heyde syndrome (bleeding vascular ectasias with aortic stenosis) appear to benefit from aortic valve replacement.
- A: A PPI doesn't treat ectasias.
- C: Aspirin would worsen bleeding.
- D: Colectomy doesn't address small-bowel lesions or the underlying mechanism.
- E: Iron alone doesn't stop the bleeding.
Source: Ch 0051, Colonic Sources of Bleeding

**12. E** — Hemoglobin doesn't fall immediately in acute GI bleeding because plasma and red cells are lost together. It drops as extravascular fluid restores volume, which can take up to 72 hours. Vital signs are the best initial assessment.
- A: Tachycardia and orthostasis indicate significant bleeding.
- B: A normal value is expected early.
- C: Nothing suggests chronic anemia.
- D: Equilibration is the explanation the source gives.
Source: Ch 0051, Initial Assessment

**13. A** — Diverticular bleeding is abrupt, usually painless, sometimes massive, and often from the right colon. It stops spontaneously in ≥90% of patients in US and European series, with ~15% rebleeding over 4–5 years.
- B: Chronic occult bleeding isn't characteristic.
- C: It's often right-sided.
- D: It's usually painless.
- E: Rebleeding is ~15% over 4–5 years in Western series.
Source: Ch 0051, Colonic Sources of Bleeding

**14. D** — PPI prophylaxis is suggested for critically ill patients at high risk (≥4%): mechanical ventilation without enteral nutrition, portal hypertension, cirrhosis, platelets <50 × 10⁹/L, INR >1.5, or two of ventilation with enteral nutrition, AKI, sepsis or shock.
- A: Ventilation with enteral feeding counts only alongside a second factor.
- B: Sepsis counts only alongside a second factor.
- C: AKI counts only alongside a second factor.
- E: The platelet threshold is <50,000.
Source: Ch 0051, Erosive Disease

**15. C** — Upper endoscopy should be done within 24 hours in most hospitalized UGIB patients. More urgent endoscopy (6–12 hours) doesn't improve outcomes even in high-risk patients and may raise mortality in observational studies.
- A: Ultra-urgent endoscopy isn't beneficial.
- B: Delaying beyond 24 hours isn't recommended.
- D: Endoscopy guides therapy and discharge.
- E: Delaying beyond 24 hours isn't recommended.
Source: Ch 0051, Endoscopy
