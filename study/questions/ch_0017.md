# Ch 17 — Headache: practice questions

**1.** A 44-year-old woman comes to the emergency department 3 hours after a sudden, severe headache began while she was lifting weights. The pain peaked within 1 minute and is the worst of her life. Temperature is 37.0°C (98.6°F), blood pressure is 148/88 mm Hg, and pulse is 90/min. She has neck stiffness. Neurologic examination is nonfocal. A noncontrast CT scan of the head is normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Discharge with an NSAID and follow-up in 1 week
B. Outpatient MRI of the brain in 2 weeks
C. Lumbar puncture
D. Subcutaneous sumatriptan
E. Empiric IV ceftriaxone without further testing

**2.** A 73-year-old woman comes to the office because of 3 weeks of a new, dull headache. Brushing her hair is painful, and her jaw aches after chewing for a few minutes. She has morning stiffness in both shoulders. Temperature is 37.6°C (99.7°F). The left temporal artery is tender. Visual acuity and funduscopic examination are normal. ESR is 88 mm/h. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Begin prednisone 80 mg daily and arrange a temporal artery biopsy
B. Perform temporal artery biopsy and start glucocorticoids only if it is positive
C. Prescribe sumatriptan for acute attacks
D. Obtain MRI of the brain before any treatment
E. Start naproxen and recheck the ESR in 4 weeks

**3.** A 70-year-old man has 2 weeks of a new bitemporal headache, pain in his jaw when chewing, and a tender, nodular right temporal artery. He has no visual symptoms. ESR is 18 mm/h. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Reassure him that the normal ESR excludes giant cell arteritis
B. Start sumatriptan for presumed migraine
C. Repeat the ESR in 1 month
D. Perform a lumbar puncture
E. Start glucocorticoids and arrange a temporal artery biopsy

**4.** A 26-year-old woman had a lumbar puncture 3 days ago for suspected meningitis; the CSF was normal. She now has an occipitofrontal headache that begins within minutes of sitting up and resolves within minutes of lying down. Bed rest and two IV caffeine infusions have not helped. Vital signs and neurologic examination are normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. MRI of the brain with gadolinium
B. Epidural blood patch at the site of the lumbar puncture
C. CT myelography
D. Oral acetazolamide
E. Repeat lumbar puncture to measure opening pressure

**5.** A 41-year-old man has had 3 months of daily headache. It is absent on waking, begins after he has been upright for a while, and improves within minutes of lying down. It started the day he moved heavy furniture. He has had no lumbar puncture, epidural injection, or head trauma. Neurologic examination is normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial imaging study?

A. Noncontrast CT of the head
B. CT angiography of the head and neck
C. Lumbar puncture with opening pressure
D. MRI of the brain with gadolinium
E. 111In-DTPA radionuclide cisternography

**6.** A 39-year-old woman has had 6 weeks of headache that appears after she has been upright and resolves within minutes of lying down. MRI of the brain with gadolinium shows diffuse meningeal enhancement. Bed rest has not helped, and IV caffeine 500 mg in 500 mL of saline over 2 hours is planned. Which of the following should be obtained before the infusion?

A. Electrocardiogram
B. Serum caffeine level
C. Repeat MRI with spinal sequences
D. Urine drug screen
E. Transthoracic echocardiogram

**7.** A 28-year-old woman comes to the office because of 2 months of generalized headache. It is present on awakening, improves as the day goes on, and is worse when she lies down. She reports brief episodes of graying of vision lasting seconds. BMI is 38 kg/m². Blood pressure is 122/78 mm Hg. Funduscopy shows bilateral papilledema. The rest of the neurologic examination is normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Lumbar puncture before any imaging
B. Start acetazolamide without further testing
C. Noncontrast CT of the head, then discharge if normal
D. Sumatriptan for acute attacks
E. MRI of the brain with MR venography

**8.** A 30-year-old woman with a BMI of 36 kg/m² has 6 weeks of headache present on awakening and worse lying down, with transient visual obscurations and bilateral papilledema. MRI and MR venography are normal. Lumbar puncture performed while she has a headache shows an elevated opening pressure, and her headache improves after 25 mL of CSF is removed. CSF composition is normal. Formal visual field testing shows enlarged blind spots. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial treatment?

A. Topiramate
B. Ventriculoperitoneal shunt
C. Acetazolamide and a weight-loss plan
D. Prednisone
E. Intracranial pressure monitoring

**9.** A 45-year-old woman with migraine now has headache on 25 days per month. For the past year, she has taken an over-the-counter combination analgesic almost daily. Propranolol was started 2 months ago without improvement, and she continues the daily analgesic. Neurologic examination is normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Increase the propranolol dose
B. Reduce and eliminate the analgesic, using naproxen for residual pain
C. Add a butalbital-containing product
D. Switch the preventive to topiramate and continue the daily analgesic
E. MRI of the brain

**10.** A 66-year-old woman comes to the emergency department with severe pain around the right eye followed by a prostrating headache, nausea, and vomiting. The right eye is red, and the right pupil is moderately dilated and does not react to light. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Migraine with aura
B. Cluster headache
C. Subarachnoid hemorrhage
D. Acute glaucoma
E. Giant cell arteritis

**11.** A 34-year-old man has had 6 weeks of vomiting on waking. For the past 2 weeks he has also had headaches that worsen with coughing, and he has become unsteady when walking. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Frontal lobe glioma
B. Prolactin-secreting pituitary adenoma
C. Idiopathic intracranial hypertension
D. Medication-overuse headache
E. Posterior fossa tumor

**12.** A 58-year-old woman with metastatic breast cancer has had 2 weeks of a new, persistent headache. She has never had headaches before. Neurologic examination is nonfocal, and funduscopy is normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. MRI of the brain
B. Trial of sumatriptan
C. Reassurance, because most headaches are benign
D. Temporal artery biopsy
E. Start amitriptyline

**13.** A 31-year-old woman has been treated by her primary care physician for headaches thought to be tension-type headache. Two standard therapies, used correctly for an adequate period, have not helped. She has no red flag features, and the neurologic examination is normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Start a daily opioid
B. Add a third agent from the same class
C. Refer her to a headache specialist
D. Reassure her and continue the current therapy
E. Obtain an electroencephalogram

**14.** A 40-year-old woman with a long history of migraine now has headaches on 18 days per month. She uses an analgesic less than twice a week. There are no red flags, and the neurologic examination is normal. She wants a preventive medication but is worried about feeling drowsy in the morning at work. Which of the following is the most appropriate regimen?

A. Daily oral oxycodone
B. Amitriptyline 10 mg taken about 12 hours before her usual waking time, titrated slowly
C. Daily butalbital-containing analgesic
D. Amitriptyline 1 mg/kg taken each morning from the start
E. Nightly zolpidem

## Answers

**1. C** — Sudden headache maximal within 5 minutes, lasting more than 5 minutes, with a stiff neck and no fever suggests subarachnoid hemorrhage. A small hemorrhage can be missed on CT, so LP is required to diagnose it definitively.
- A, B: Discharge or delayed imaging risks missing SAH.
- D: Triptans treat migraine; SAH must be excluded first.
- E: She is afebrile; the presentation suggests hemorrhage, not meningitis.
Source: Ch 0017, Intracranial Hemorrhage

**2. A** — When suspicion for giant cell arteritis is high (age >50, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, PMR symptoms, raised ESR), start prednisone 80 mg daily immediately alongside the temporal artery biopsy. Untreated, about half of patients develop blindness. [verify: guideline may have changed since this edition]
- B: Waiting for the biopsy risks vision loss.
- C: This isn't migraine.
- D: Brain MRI doesn't diagnose GCA and delays treatment.
- E: NSAIDs and waiting don't prevent ischemic optic neuropathy.
Source: Ch 0017, Temporal Arteritis

**3. E** — The ESR is often but not always elevated; a normal ESR does not exclude giant cell arteritis. With jaw claudication and a tender, nodular temporal artery, treat and biopsy.
- A: This is exactly the trap the chapter warns about.
- B: Migraine is common in the elderly, but these features point to GCA.
- C: Waiting a month risks blindness.
- D: LP has no role in GCA.
Source: Ch 0017, Temporal Arteritis

**4. B** — Post-LP headache is the most common low CSF volume headache. When bed rest and IV caffeine fail, an autologous epidural blood patch is effective; for post-LP headache, it's placed empirically at the LP site.
- A, C: Imaging to find the leak is unnecessary when the source is known.
- D: Acetazolamide lowers CSF production and treats raised, not low, pressure.
- E: Another LP creates a new leak.
Source: Ch 0017, Secondary NDPH — Low CSF Volume Headache

**5. D** — For a suspected spontaneous CSF leak, MRI with gadolinium is the initial study of choice. Diffuse meningeal enhancement is so typical that it establishes the diagnosis in context (Fig 17-1).
- A: Noncontrast CT doesn't show meningeal enhancement.
- B: CTA evaluates vessels, not CSF leaks.
- C: LP could worsen a leak and isn't the initial study.
- E: Radionuclide cisternography is now rarely used.
Source: Ch 0017, Low CSF Volume Headache; Figure 17-1

**6. A** — An ECG to screen for arrhythmia should be performed before IV caffeine. At least two infusions are reasonable before further tests to find the leak.
- B: There's no baseline caffeine level requirement.
- C: Spinal imaging is for localization if caffeine fails.
- D, E: Neither is part of the pre-infusion check.
Source: Ch 0017, Low CSF Volume Headache (treatment)

**7. E** — Suspected raised CSF pressure (an obese young woman with morning headache worse when lying down, visual obscurations and papilledema) requires brain imaging first. MRI with MR venogram is the most efficient initial study, since venous sinus obstruction occurs in some cases.
- A: Image before LP.
- B: Treating without confirming the diagnosis skips the workup.
- C: Noncontrast CT doesn't assess the venous sinuses.
- D: A triptan doesn't address raised intracranial pressure.
Source: Ch 0017, Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension

**8. C** — Initial treatment of idiopathic intracranial hypertension is acetazolamide (250–500 mg bid) plus a weight-loss plan when needed.
- A: Topiramate is the next choice if acetazolamide fails.
- B, E: Shunting and ICP monitoring are for severely disabled patients who don't respond to medical treatment.
- D: Glucocorticoids aren't part of the described treatment.
Source: Ch 0017, Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension

**9. B** — Analgesic overuse causes medication-overuse headache and markedly impairs preventives. Continued regular analgesic use is a common cause of a preventive "failing." Reduce the analgesic (e.g., by 10% every 1–2 weeks) or stop it, with naproxen 500 mg bid for residual pain.
- A, D: Changing the preventive while daily analgesics continue repeats the failure.
- C: Butalbital is another overused agent; withdrawal is harder after barbiturates.
- E: There are no red flags.
Source: Ch 0017, Medication-Related and Medication-Overuse Headache

**10. D** — Glaucoma can present with a prostrating headache, nausea and vomiting, typically starting with severe eye pain. The eye is red with a fixed, moderately dilated pupil.
- A: Migraine doesn't fix and dilate the pupil.
- B: Cluster causes cranial autonomic features (tearing, rhinorrhea, ptosis), not a fixed mid-dilated pupil.
- C: SAH presents as a thunderclap headache with a stiff neck.
- E: GCA causes scalp tenderness and jaw claudication.
Source: Ch 0017, Glaucoma

**11. E** — Vomiting that precedes the headache by weeks is highly characteristic of posterior fossa brain tumors. Pain brought on by coughing or bending also points to a posterior fossa mass.
- A: A frontal tumor wouldn't produce this vomiting pattern or ataxia.
- B: A prolactinoma presents with amenorrhea or galactorrhea.
- C: IIH typically affects obese women and causes visual obscurations.
- D: There's no analgesic overuse history.
Source: Ch 0017, Brain Tumor; Table 17-2

**12. A** — New headache in a patient with known malignancy suggests cerebral metastases or carcinomatous meningitis. Recent-onset headache warrants CT or MRI.
- B: A triptan trial skips evaluation of a red-flag headache.
- C: "Known systemic illness" is a red flag; reassurance is inappropriate.
- D: She lacks GCA features.
- E: Preventive therapy before excluding a secondary cause is wrong.
Source: Ch 0017, Brain Tumor; Clinical Evaluation of Acute, New-Onset Headache

**13. C** — Refer patients who lack a clear diagnosis, have a primary headache other than migraine or tension-type, or haven't responded to two or more standard therapies.
- A: Opioids drive medication-overuse headache.
- B: Adding more of the same doesn't address treatment failure.
- D: Continuing ineffective therapy isn't appropriate.
- E: EEG has no role in headache diagnosis here.
Source: Ch 0017, Primary Care and Headache Management

**14. B** — For chronic daily headache arising from migraine, tricyclics are very useful preventives. Start low (10–25 mg) and give about 12 hours before expected waking to avoid morning sleepiness; doses go up to 1 mg/kg.
- A, C: Opioids and butalbital promote medication-overuse headache.
- D: Starting at full dose in the morning maximizes daytime drowsiness.
- E: A hypnotic isn't a headache preventive.
Source: Ch 0017, Approach to the Patient: Chronic Daily Headache
