# Ch 16 — Abdominal Pain: practice questions

**1.** A 74-year-old man is brought to the emergency department because of 2 days of diffuse, crampy abdominal pain that has become severe over the past 6 hours. He has atrial fibrillation and does not take anticoagulation. Temperature is 37.4°C (99.3°F), pulse is 104/min and irregularly irregular, and blood pressure is 128/76 mm Hg. The abdomen is soft and nondistended, with only minimal tenderness and no guarding; bowel sounds are normal. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Acute pancreatitis
B. Small-bowel obstruction
C. Perforated peptic ulcer
D. Superior mesenteric artery occlusion
E. Irritable bowel syndrome

**2.** A 71-year-old man with hypertension collapses at home after sudden, severe abdominal pain radiating to the left flank and groin. In the emergency department, blood pressure is 74/40 mm Hg and pulse is 128/min. He is pale and diaphoretic. The abdomen is diffusely tender. Two large-bore IV lines are placed and fluids are started. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Immediate transfer to the operating room
B. CT angiography of the abdomen
C. Formal abdominal ultrasound in the radiology department
D. Serial abdominal examinations over the next 2 hours
E. Upright and supine abdominal radiographs

**3.** A 38-year-old woman comes to the emergency department with 12 hours of lower abdominal pain and two episodes of vomiting. Temperature is 37.9°C (100.2°F), pulse is 96/min, and blood pressure is 124/78 mm Hg. She has mild lower abdominal tenderness without rigidity. Pelvic examination shows mild nonspecific tenderness. Urine pregnancy test is negative. The surgeon is uncertain whether an operation is needed. Which of the following is the most appropriate imaging study?

A. Upright and supine abdominal radiographs
B. Upper gastrointestinal barium study
C. Diagnostic peritoneal lavage
D. Hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid (HIDA) scan
E. Low-dose CT of the abdomen and pelvis

**4.** A 29-year-old man comes to the emergency department with 10 hours of severe right lower quadrant pain. He rates the pain 9/10. Temperature is 38.0°C (100.4°F) and pulse is 102/min. There is focal right lower quadrant tenderness. A surgical consultation has been requested and is expected within 1 hour. The nurse asks whether pain medication should be held until the surgeon examines him. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Withhold all analgesia until the surgeon examines him
B. Administer IV opioid analgesia now
C. Give only a low dose of acetaminophen to avoid masking signs
D. Withhold analgesia until a CT scan is completed
E. Give an antispasmodic only

**5.** A 63-year-old man with diabetes and hypertension comes to the emergency department because of 1 hour of epigastric pain with nausea and sweating. Pulse is 98/min and blood pressure is 150/90 mm Hg. The lungs are clear. The abdomen is soft with mild epigastric tenderness. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Upright abdominal radiograph
B. Serum amylase
C. 12-lead electrocardiogram
D. Upper endoscopy
E. Trial of an oral proton pump inhibitor and discharge

**6.** A 70-year-old woman has 3 days of progressive abdominal distention and crampy lower abdominal pain radiating to the lower back. She has not passed flatus for 24 hours. Vital signs are stable. The abdomen is distended and tympanic, without peritoneal signs. Abdominal radiographs show a dilated colon down to the sigmoid with no free air. Which of the following is the most appropriate study to confirm the diagnosis?

A. Oral barium small-bowel follow-through
B. Diagnostic peritoneal lavage
C. HIDA scan
D. Upper endoscopy
E. Contrast enema

**7.** A 19-year-old woman with type 1 diabetes is admitted with diabetic ketoacidosis (glucose 480 mg/dL, pH 7.18, anion gap 24) and diffuse abdominal pain. After 8 hours of IV fluids and insulin, the anion gap has closed and the pH is 7.36. However, her pain is now localized to the right lower quadrant, with focal tenderness and a temperature of 38.2°C (100.8°F). Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Imaging and surgical evaluation for an underlying intraabdominal cause
B. Continue the insulin infusion and reassure her that the pain will resolve
C. Discharge home with outpatient follow-up
D. Double the rate of IV fluids
E. Start an IV proton pump inhibitor

**8.** A 34-year-old man with suspected early peritonitis is being examined by a medical student. The student plans to test for rebound tenderness by pressing deeply into the right lower quadrant and releasing suddenly. Which of the following is the most appropriate way to assess for peritoneal irritation?

A. Deep palpation with sudden release in each quadrant
B. Auscultation of bowel sounds for 5 minutes
C. Firm palpation of all four quadrants
D. Gentle percussion of the abdomen or asking the patient to cough
E. Repeating the rebound maneuver several times to confirm consistency

**9.** A 52-year-old man with acute myeloid leukemia is on day 10 after induction chemotherapy. He develops fever to 38.9°C (102°F) and right lower quadrant abdominal pain. Absolute neutrophil count is 100/μL. The right lower quadrant is tender. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Acute calculous cholecystitis
B. Neutropenic enterocolitis (typhlitis)
C. Splenic abscess
D. Distal ureteral stone
E. Familial Mediterranean fever

**10.** A 44-year-old man has sudden severe left flank pain radiating to the left testicle and inner thigh. He cannot lie still and keeps changing position. Temperature is normal. The testes are normal and nontender. Urinalysis shows 20 red blood cells per high-power field. Which of the following is the most likely site of disease?

A. Ureteropelvic junction
B. Sigmoid colon
C. Intravesical (distal) portion of the ureter
D. Left testis
E. Parietal peritoneum

**11.** A 68-year-old woman has 3 days of burning, lancinating pain in a band across the left upper abdomen that wraps around from the back. Light touch over the area is painful. The pain is unrelated to meals and worsens when she twists her spine. Deep palpation of the abdomen does not increase the pain, and there is no rigidity. No rash is present. Lipase is normal. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Splenic infarct
B. Gastric ulcer
C. Acute pancreatitis
D. Pyelonephritis
E. Herpes zoster

**12.** A 66-year-old woman who takes warfarin for a mechanical heart valve has had a week of coughing fits from bronchitis. She now has constant lower abdominal pain that worsens when she moves or stands. A tender mass is palpable in the left lower quadrant. INR is 4.1. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Rectus sheath hematoma
B. Diverticulitis
C. Ovarian torsion
D. Incarcerated inguinal hernia
E. Appendiceal abscess

**13.** A 48-year-old woman has 1 day of epigastric and right upper quadrant pain. Serum amylase is mildly elevated. Ultrasound shows gallstones without gallbladder wall thickening or pericholecystic fluid. The team cannot tell whether she has acute cholecystitis or acute pancreatitis. Which of the following studies best distinguishes these conditions?

A. Repeat serum amylase in 6 hours
B. Plain abdominal radiograph
C. Diagnostic peritoneal lavage
D. HIDA scan
E. Upper gastrointestinal barium study

## Answers

**1. D** — Superior mesenteric artery occlusion often starts with mild continuous or crampy diffuse pain for 2–3 days before collapse or peritonitis. The early pain comes from hyperperistalsis, so the exam is benign: "pain out of proportion to physical findings." An elderly patient with untreated atrial fibrillation is at embolic risk.
- A: Nothing localizes to the pancreas; the chapter's classic clue here is the disproportion, not epigastric pain.
- B: Obstruction causes colicky periumbilical pain, usually with distention.
- C: Perforation causes chemical peritonitis with rigidity, not a soft, nontender abdomen.
- E: IBS is a diagnosis of exclusion and wouldn't present acutely in this setting.
Source: Ch 0016, Vascular Disturbances

**2. A** — Abdominal pain radiating to the flank, sacrum, or genitalia signals a rupturing AAA. Exsanguinating intraabdominal hemorrhage is the one situation where the patient goes straight to the operating room after venous access. These patients may die in radiology waiting for unnecessary studies.
- B: CT delays definitive treatment in an unstable patient.
- C: Leaving for radiology risks death en route.
- D: Observation is for stable patients with uncertain diagnoses.
- E: Plain films add nothing here.
Source: Ch 0016, Vascular Disturbances; Approach to the Patient

**3. E** — When the need for surgical or medical intervention is unclear in nontraumatic acute abdominal pain, low-dose CT is preferred over abdominal radiography.
- A: Plain films have limited utility.
- B: Upper GI contrast studies are rarely appropriate.
- C: Outside trauma, lavage has been replaced by CT and laparoscopy.
- D: HIDA is for biliary questions, not undifferentiated lower abdominal pain.
Source: Ch 0016, Approach to the Patient (imaging)

**4. B** — Analgesics should not be withheld until a definitive diagnosis or plan is made. Adequate analgesia is unlikely to obscure the diagnosis.
- A, D: Withholding analgesia is not supported.
- C: Deliberately undertreating pain to "preserve signs" is the misconception the chapter corrects.
- E: An antispasmodic alone doesn't adequately treat severe pain.
Source: Ch 0016, Approach to the Patient

**5. C** — Intrathoracic disease must be considered in every patient with abdominal pain, especially upper abdominal pain. MI is among the intrathoracic diseases that most often masquerade as abdominal emergencies. This patient has risk factors and autonomic symptoms, so evaluate for MI first.
- A: Radiographs don't address the most dangerous possibility.
- B: Amylase is nonspecific and doesn't exclude MI.
- D: Endoscopy before excluding MI is inappropriate.
- E: Discharging without evaluating for MI misses a lethal diagnosis.
Source: Ch 0016, Referred Pain in Abdominal Disease

**6. E** — In suspected colonic obstruction without perforation, a contrast enema may be diagnostic. Oral barium should be avoided when colonic obstruction is possible.
- A: Oral barium is specifically to be avoided here.
- B: Lavage has no role in nontraumatic obstruction.
- C: HIDA evaluates the biliary tree.
- D: Upper endoscopy can't reach the colon.
Source: Ch 0016, Approach to the Patient (imaging)

**7. A** — Appendicitis or obstruction can precipitate DKA. If correcting the metabolic abnormality doesn't promptly resolve the abdominal pain, an underlying organic problem should be suspected. New localized RLQ tenderness and fever demand evaluation.
- B: Reassurance ignores the persistent, now-localized pain.
- C: Discharge is unsafe.
- D: The acidosis is already corrected; more fluid won't explain the pain.
- E: A PPI doesn't address a possible surgical cause.
Source: Ch 0016, Metabolic Abdominal Crises

**8. D** — Gentle percussion ("rebound on a miniature scale") or asking the patient to cough elicits true rebound more precisely and without causing protective spasm. Forceful rebound testing is described as cruel and unnecessary, and it makes later exams unreliable.
- A, E: Forceful and repeated rebound testing is what the chapter advises against.
- B: Auscultation is one of the least revealing parts of the exam.
- C: Aggressive palpation adds voluntary spasm and can hide findings.
Source: Ch 0016, Approach to the Patient (examination)

**9. B** — Neutropenic enterocolitis (typhlitis) is a recognized cause of fever and abdominal pain in patients with chemotherapy-induced marrow suppression. It appears among the RLQ causes in Table 16-3. These patients need serial exams to detect perforation.
- A: The pain is in the RLQ, not the RUQ.
- C: Splenic abscess causes LUQ or left flank pain.
- D: A stone causes colic with hematuria, not neutropenic fever.
- E: FMF causes recurrent diffuse attacks and doesn't fit this setting.
Source: Ch 0016, Immunocompromise; Table 16-3

**10. C** — Acute obstruction of the intravesical ureter causes severe suprapubic and flank pain radiating to the penis, scrotum, or inner upper thigh. Colic makes patients restless, unlike peritonitis.
- A: UPJ obstruction causes pain near the costovertebral angle.
- B: Colonic disease doesn't cause hematuria with testicular radiation.
- D: Pain from the testis is accentuated by pressure on it; his testes are nontender.
- E: Peritonitis makes patients lie still.
Source: Ch 0016, Obstruction of Hollow Viscera

**11. E** — Spinal nerve root pain (e.g., herpes zoster before the rash) is lancinating and dermatomal, with common hyperesthesia. It is worse with spine movement and unrelated to food. Palpation doesn't accentuate it, and there's no rigidity.
- A, B, C: These are LUQ visceral causes; they'd be expected to cause tenderness, and pancreatitis is unlikely with a normal lipase.
- D: Pyelonephritis causes CVA tenderness and fever, not cutaneous hyperesthesia.
Source: Ch 0016, Neurogenic Causes

**12. A** — Rectus sheath hematoma, now most often seen with anticoagulation, causes constant abdominal wall pain worsened by movement and standing, sometimes with a lower-quadrant mass. Coughing fits plus a supratherapeutic INR fit.
- B: Diverticulitis typically causes fever and LLQ tenderness without movement-dependent wall pain.
- C: Ovarian torsion causes acute pelvic pain, not a wall mass.
- D: An inguinal hernia sits in the groin.
- E: Appendiceal abscess is right-sided.
Source: Ch 0016, Abdominal Wall

**13. D** — HIDA scans may help differentiate acute cholecystitis or biliary colic from acute pancreatitis.
- A: Amylase rises in cholecystitis as well as pancreatitis, so repeating it won't separate them.
- B: Plain films have limited utility.
- C: Lavage has no role here.
- E: Upper GI contrast studies are rarely appropriate.
Source: Ch 0016, Approach to the Patient (imaging, laboratory)
