# Ch 23 — Syncope: practice questions

**1.** A 68-year-old man is brought to the emergency department after losing consciousness while sitting at dinner. He had palpitations immediately beforehand and recovered within a minute without confusion. ECG shows sinus rhythm with right bundle branch block and left anterior fascicular block (QRS 136 ms). Blood pressure is 128/76 mm Hg with no orthostatic change. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Discharge with outpatient Holter monitoring
B. Tilt-table testing
C. EEG
D. Hospital admission with continuous ECG monitoring
E. Reassurance and increased salt intake

**2.** A 17-year-old girl fainted while having blood drawn. She felt warm, nauseated and lightheaded, became pale and sweaty, and then lost consciousness for about 20 seconds with a few brief jerks of her arms. She was fully oriented immediately on waking. Examination, orthostatic vital signs and ECG are normal. She has had two similar episodes in stressful situations. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?

A. Reassurance, education, trigger avoidance, increased fluid and salt intake, and counterpressure maneuvers
B. Start a β-blocker
C. Start levetiracetam
D. Refer for dual-chamber pacemaker placement
E. Implantable loop recorder

**3.** A 59-year-old woman has had near-daily episodes of brief lightheadedness with two syncopal events over the past 3 weeks. Examination, orthostatic vital signs, echocardiogram and resting ECG are normal. She has no high-risk features. Which of the following is the most appropriate test?

A. Implantable loop recorder
B. Invasive electrophysiologic study
C. Continuous ambulatory (Holter) ECG monitoring
D. Coronary angiography
E. Brain MRI

**4.** A 76-year-old man with benign prostatic hyperplasia started terazosin 2 weeks ago. Since then he has had lightheadedness on standing and fainted once after getting out of bed at night. Blood pressure is 138/80 mm Hg supine and 112/68 mm Hg after 3 minutes standing. ECG is normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate first step?

A. Start fludrocortisone
B. Start midodrine
C. Order tilt-table testing
D. Admit for telemetry
E. Discontinue terazosin

**5.** A 24-year-old woman has recurrent vasovagal syncope that has continued despite reassurance, trigger avoidance, increased fluid and salt intake, and correctly performed leg-crossing and muscle-tensing maneuvers. Examination and ECG are normal. Which of the following medications has the best randomized-trial evidence for preventing her episodes?

A. Fludrocortisone
B. Midodrine
C. Metoprolol
D. Sertraline
E. Hydrochlorothiazide

**6.** A 72-year-old man has had three episodes of unexplained syncope over a year. ECG, echocardiogram and Holter monitoring are unrevealing. Carotid sinus massage is being considered. On examination, a bruit is heard over the right carotid artery. Which of the following is the most appropriate action?

A. Avoid carotid sinus massage
B. Perform carotid sinus massage on the left side only
C. Perform carotid sinus massage without monitoring
D. Perform bilateral simultaneous carotid sinus massage
E. Perform carotid sinus massage after a dose of atropine

**7.** A 19-year-old college basketball player collapsed during practice and regained consciousness within seconds. He has no prodrome and no prior episodes. A systolic murmur is heard at the left sternal border that increases with Valsalva. ECG shows left ventricular hypertrophy. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Tilt-table testing
B. Reassurance and return to play
C. Increase fluid and salt intake
D. Echocardiography
E. EEG

**8.** A 35-year-old woman lost consciousness while standing in a hot, crowded room. A witness describes pallor and sweating beforehand, eyes open and turned upward, a few irregular jerks lasting about 10 seconds, and urinary incontinence. She was fully oriented within a minute. Which of the following features would most strongly suggest a seizure rather than syncope?

A. Eyes open during the event
B. Urinary incontinence
C. Prolonged disorientation lasting many minutes after the event
D. Brief multifocal myoclonic jerks
E. Pallor and diaphoresis before the event

**9.** A 64-year-old man with Parkinson disease has disabling lightheadedness on standing. Blood pressure falls from 132/80 mm Hg supine to 96/60 mm Hg after 3 minutes standing, with no change in heart rate. He has stopped all vasoactive medications, rises in stages, increased his fluid and salt intake, and uses counterpressure maneuvers, but symptoms persist. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Start a β-blocker
B. Start hydrochlorothiazide
C. Implant a pacemaker
D. Start tamsulosin
E. Start midodrine

**10.** A 55-year-old man has had two syncopal episodes 7 months apart, each preceded by sudden palpitations. ECG, echocardiogram, exercise test and a 48-hour Holter monitor are normal. He has no high-risk features. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Implantable loop recorder
B. Repeat 48-hour Holter monitoring
C. Invasive electrophysiologic study
D. Tilt-table testing
E. Reassurance without further testing

**11.** A 70-year-old woman has lightheadedness on standing. Blood pressure is measured supine and after 3 minutes standing. Which of the following findings meets the definition of orthostatic hypotension?

A. Fall in systolic blood pressure from 130 to 118 mm Hg
B. Fall in systolic blood pressure from 142 to 120 mm Hg
C. Fall in diastolic blood pressure from 80 to 74 mm Hg
D. Fall in systolic blood pressure that begins within 15 seconds of standing and resolves by 45 seconds
E. Rise in heart rate of 15/min without a change in blood pressure

**12.** A 22-year-old man reports sudden episodes in which his knees buckle and he slumps to the floor when he laughs hard. He remains aware throughout, and each episode lasts about a minute. He also has excessive daytime sleepiness. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Vasovagal syncope
B. Absence seizures
C. Orthostatic hypotension
D. Cataplexy
E. Psychogenic pseudosyncope

**13.** A 61-year-old man has progressive orthostatic lightheadedness, erectile dysfunction, urinary urgency, reduced sweating, gait unsteadiness and slurred speech. Tilt-table testing shows a gradual fall in blood pressure with no compensatory rise in heart rate. Which of the following is the most likely cause of his orthostatic hypotension?

A. Vasovagal syncope
B. Carotid sinus hypersensitivity
C. Multiple system atrophy
D. Brugada syndrome
E. Volume depletion

**14.** A 66-year-old woman with pure autonomic failure is treated with fludrocortisone and midodrine for orthostatic hypotension. Her supine blood pressure at night is 182/96 mm Hg, and she reports frequent nighttime urination. Which of the following is the most appropriate nonpharmacologic measure?

A. Sleep flat with legs elevated
B. Raise the head of the bed
C. Drink 500 mL of water at bedtime
D. Eat a large carbohydrate-rich meal in the evening
E. Stop all salt intake

## Answers

**1. D** — Syncope while seated, with palpitations and bifascicular block (QRS ≥120 ms), carries several Table 23-1 high-risk features. It warrants hospital admission and inpatient ECG monitoring for a life-threatening arrhythmia.
- A: Outpatient Holter suits frequent episodes in low-risk patients, not high-risk ones.
- B: Tilt testing evaluates reflex syncope and autonomic failure, not suspected conduction disease.
- C: The history (brief, no postictal confusion) fits syncope, not seizure.
- E: Fluid and salt are for vasovagal or orthostatic syncope.
Source: Ch 0023, Table 23-1 and Cardiac Evaluation

**2. A** — Typical vasovagal syncope with a normal workup is managed with reassurance, education, trigger avoidance, fluid and salt expansion, and isometric counterpressure maneuvers.
- B: β-blockers are used by experts for refractory cases but lack strong evidence, and they aren't first line.
- C: Brief myoclonic jerks occur in up to 90% of syncopal episodes and don't indicate epilepsy.
- D: Pacing is limited to older patients (>40) with a cardioinhibitory tilt response and frequent recurrences.
- E: Loop recorders are for suspected arrhythmia; this history is classic reflex syncope.
Source: Ch 0023, Treatment: Neurally Mediated Syncope

**3. C** — Holter monitoring is recommended for patients with frequent (daily or near-daily) episodes.
- A: Implantable loop recorders are for episodes too infrequent to capture with shorter monitoring.
- B: Electrophysiologic study has low sensitivity and specificity. It is reserved for structural disease with ECG abnormalities after a nondiagnostic noninvasive workup.
- D: There's no suggestion of ischemia.
- E: Neuroimaging doesn't evaluate arrhythmic syncope.
Source: Ch 0023, Cardiac Evaluation

**4. E** — A 26 mm Hg systolic fall on standing after starting an α-blocker is drug-induced orthostatic hypotension. The first step is removing the reversible cause, usually a vasoactive medication.
- A: Fludrocortisone is used only after removing causes and failing nonpharmacologic measures.
- B: Midodrine follows the same rule.
- C: The cause is already evident, so tilt testing adds nothing.
- D: He has no high-risk features requiring telemetry.
Source: Ch 0023, Treatment: Orthostatic Hypotension

**5. B** — Of the drugs used for refractory neurally mediated syncope, only midodrine has been shown effective in international multicenter randomized controlled trials.
- A: Fludrocortisone is widely used but lacks that level of evidence.
- C: β-blockers are widely used but lack that level of evidence.
- D: SSRIs aren't among the agents the source lists for this purpose.
- E: A diuretic would worsen volume depletion.
Source: Ch 0023, Treatment: Neurally Mediated Syncope

**6. A** — Carotid sinus massage should be avoided in patients with carotid bruits or known or possible plaque or stenosis.
- B: A bruit on one side signals vascular disease, so massaging the other side is still unsafe.
- C: When performed, massage needs continuous ECG and blood pressure monitoring.
- D: Bilateral simultaneous massage is never appropriate.
- E: Premedication doesn't remove the embolic risk of plaque.
Source: Ch 0023, Autonomic Nervous System Testing

**7. D** — Exertional syncope with a murmur that increases with Valsalva and LVH suggests hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Echocardiography is indicated for an abnormal exam or ECG, and an exercise test with monitoring should follow exercise-related syncope.
- A: Tilt testing evaluates reflex syncope, not structural heart disease.
- B: Syncope during exercise is a high-risk feature, so return to play is unsafe.
- C: Volume expansion treats reflex syncope.
- E: The event has no seizure features.
Source: Ch 0023, Cardiac Evaluation; Table 23-1

**8. C** — Reorientation is almost immediate after syncope. Prolonged postictal drowsiness and disorientation favor a seizure.
- A: Eyes typically stay open and deviate upward in neurally mediated syncope.
- B: Urinary incontinence occurs with both syncope and seizures.
- D: Brief, arrhythmic multifocal myoclonus occurs in up to 90% of syncopal episodes.
- E: Pallor and diaphoresis are premonitory autonomic features of syncope.
Source: Ch 0023, Differential Diagnosis

**9. E** — Neurogenic orthostatic hypotension (Parkinson disease, no compensatory heart-rate rise) that persists after drug removal and nonpharmacologic measures is treated with fludrocortisone or vasoconstrictors such as midodrine or droxidopa.
- A: A β-blocker would lower blood pressure further.
- B: A diuretic causes volume depletion and worsens orthostatic hypotension.
- C: Pacing doesn't correct vasoconstrictor failure.
- D: An α-blocker is itself a cause of orthostatic hypotension.
Source: Ch 0023, Treatment: Orthostatic Hypotension

**10. A** — Episodes months apart in a low-risk patient with suspected arrhythmia call for an implantable loop recorder. Monitoring should ideally last at least twice the interval between episodes.
- B: A repeat short Holter is unlikely to capture an event that happens every few months.
- C: Electrophysiologic study needs structural disease with ECG abnormalities and has low yield.
- D: The palpitation prodrome points to arrhythmia, not reflex syncope.
- E: Palpitations before syncope are a high-risk feature and should be investigated.
Source: Ch 0023, Cardiac Evaluation

**11. B** — Orthostatic hypotension is a fall of at least 20 mm Hg systolic or at least 10 mm Hg diastolic within 3 minutes of standing. A 22 mm Hg systolic fall qualifies.
- A: A 12 mm Hg systolic fall is below threshold.
- C: A 6 mm Hg diastolic fall is below threshold.
- D: Initial orthostatic hypotension (within 15 s, resolving by 45 s) reflects a transient mismatch, not autonomic failure.
- E: No blood pressure fall means no orthostatic hypotension.
Source: Ch 0023, Orthostatic Hypotension

**12. D** — Cataplexy is abrupt loss of muscle tone triggered by strong emotion (laughter, anger), with consciousness preserved, lasting 30 s to 2 min. It occurs in 60–75% of patients with narcolepsy.
- A: Syncope involves loss of consciousness.
- B: Absence seizures impair awareness and aren't triggered by laughter.
- C: Orthostatic hypotension is triggered by posture and causes presyncope or loss of consciousness.
- E: Psychogenic episodes involve apparent loss of consciousness and aren't tied to daytime sleepiness.
Source: Ch 0023, Differential Diagnosis

**13. C** — Orthostatic hypotension without a compensatory heart-rate rise, plus bladder, sexual and sudomotor dysfunction and cerebellar signs, indicates primary autonomic failure. Multiple system atrophy (Shy-Drager) is a synucleinopathy causing this picture.
- A: Vasovagal syncope requires an intact autonomic nervous system and causes abrupt BP and HR falls.
- B: Carotid sinus hypersensitivity is a reflex syncope triggered by neck stimulation.
- D: Brugada syndrome causes arrhythmic syncope, not progressive autonomic failure.
- E: Volume depletion doesn't explain the progressive multisystem autonomic and cerebellar findings.
Source: Ch 0023, Causes of Neurogenic Orthostatic Hypotension; Table 23-2; Fig 23-3

**14. B** — Raising the head of the bed reduces supine hypertension and nocturnal diuresis in patients with orthostatic hypotension.
- A: Lying flat worsens supine hypertension.
- C: A water bolus gives a short-term pressor response, which is unwanted at night with supine hypertension.
- D: Large carbohydrate meals worsen postprandial hypotension.
- E: Salt and fluid expansion is part of orthostatic hypotension treatment.
Source: Ch 0023, Treatment: Orthostatic Hypotension
