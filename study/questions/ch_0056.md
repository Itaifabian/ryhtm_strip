# Ch 56 — Fluid and Electrolyte Disturbances: practice questions

**1.** A 34-year-old woman is on postoperative day 1 after laparoscopic surgery and has been receiving hypotonic IV fluids. She develops headache and vomiting, then has a generalized seizure. Serum sodium is 119 mEq/L (preoperative 139). Which of the following is the most appropriate immediate treatment?

A. 100 mL bolus of 3% saline, repeated as needed to raise sodium by 4–6 mEq/L
B. Fluid restriction to 800 mL/day
C. Oral tolvaptan
D. Normal saline at 75 mL/h
E. Demeclocycline

**2.** A 66-year-old man with chronic hyponatremia (sodium 112 mEq/L, present for weeks) is treated with normal saline for presumed volume depletion. Eighteen hours later his sodium is 127 mEq/L and he is producing large volumes of dilute urine. Which of the following is the most appropriate step?

A. Continue normal saline
B. Start 3% saline
C. Give desmopressin and D5W to relower the sodium
D. Start tolvaptan
E. No change; neurologic symptoms would have appeared already

**3.** A 63-year-old man with a 50-pack-year smoking history has a sodium of 124 mEq/L. He is clinically euvolemic. Serum osmolality is 258, urine osmolality 560, urine sodium 62 mEq/L, and serum uric acid 2.8 mg/dL. TSH and cortisol are normal. He takes no medications. Chest radiograph is normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Normal saline infusion
B. Brain MRI only
C. Repeat chest radiograph in 6 months
D. Oral salt and observation with no further evaluation
E. Chest CT

**4.** A 70-year-old woman with SIAD has a sodium of 126 mEq/L. Urine sodium is 110 mEq/L and urine potassium 60 mEq/L. Which fluid restriction is most appropriate?

A. <1.5 L/day
B. <500 mL/day
C. 500–700 mL/day
D. <1 L/day
E. No restriction is needed

**5.** A 42-year-old woman has fatigue, weight loss and hyperpigmentation. Blood pressure is 88/56 mm Hg. Sodium is 125 mEq/L, potassium 5.9 mEq/L, and urine sodium 68 mEq/L. Which of the following is the most appropriate diagnostic test?

A. Water deprivation test
B. Chest CT
C. Serum uric acid
D. Cosyntropin stimulation test
E. Plasma renin and aldosterone ratio only

**6.** A 52-year-old man who drinks only beer for several weeks presents with sodium 108 mEq/L, potassium 2.6 mEq/L, urine osmolality 70 mOsm/kg and urine sodium <10 mEq/L. Which statement about his management is correct?

A. He is at very high risk of osmotic demyelination because of hypokalemia, alcoholism, malnutrition and rapid self-correction
B. He should receive tolvaptan
C. He needs strict fluid restriction of <500 mL/day as the main therapy
D. Potassium repletion won't affect his serum sodium
E. Correction to 130 mEq/L within 24 hours is safe

**7.** A 70-kg man has a serum sodium of 160 mEq/L from inadequate water intake. Using total body water of 60% of weight, what is his approximate free water deficit, and how fast should it be replaced?

A. 2 L over 6 hours
B. 3 L over 24 hours without monitoring
C. 6 L over 48–72 hours, lowering sodium by no more than 10 mEq/L per day
D. 10 L over 12 hours
E. 6 L over 6 hours

**8.** A 45-year-old man has sodium 154 mEq/L, polyuria of 6 L/day, and urine osmolality 110 mOsm/kg after pituitary surgery. Which of the following is the most appropriate next diagnostic step?

A. Water deprivation test
B. Hypertonic saline infusion
C. 24-hour urine calcium
D. Renal biopsy
E. Give desmopressin and measure the urine osmolality response (with a plasma AVP or copeptin level)

**9.** A 58-year-old woman with alcohol use disorder has a potassium of 2.8 mEq/L that hasn't improved despite 3 days of oral and IV potassium chloride. Which of the following is the most likely reason?

A. Hypercalcemia
B. Hypomagnesemia
C. Hyperphosphatemia
D. Metabolic acidosis
E. Excessive potassium in IV dextrose

**10.** A 26-year-old man of Asian descent wakes at 3 a.m. with severe weakness of all four limbs. Potassium is 1.9 mEq/L, phosphate and magnesium are low, and TSH is suppressed with high free T4. Which treatment rapidly reverses the hypokalemia without risk of rebound hyperkalemia?

A. IV potassium chloride 40 mEq/h
B. Oral levothyroxine
C. Spironolactone
D. High-dose propranolol
E. Acetazolamide

**11.** A 44-year-old woman has resistant hypertension and spontaneous hypokalemia (3.1 mEq/L). She takes lisinopril and hydrochlorothiazide. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial screening test, after adjusting interfering drugs?

A. Plasma aldosterone-to-renin activity ratio
B. 24-hour urine metanephrines
C. Renal artery Doppler only
D. Adrenal vein sampling
E. Genetic testing for Liddle syndrome

**12.** A 24-year-old woman has chronic hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, normal blood pressure and low magnesium. Urine sodium, potassium and chloride are persistently high, and urinary calcium excretion is very low. She denies diuretic use, and a urine diuretic screen is negative. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Bartter syndrome
B. Surreptitious vomiting
C. Gitelman syndrome
D. Primary hyperaldosteronism
E. Liddle syndrome

**13.** A 67-year-old man on dialysis misses two sessions and presents with potassium 7.4 mEq/L. ECG shows peaked T waves and a widened QRS. Which of the following should be given first?

A. Sodium polystyrene sulfonate
B. IV sodium bicarbonate bolus
C. Furosemide
D. Insulin without glucose
E. IV calcium gluconate (10 mL of 10%) over 2–3 minutes

**14.** A 59-year-old woman with CKD has potassium 6.8 mEq/L. Blood glucose is 110 mg/dL. After IV calcium, which of the following is the most appropriate next step to shift potassium into cells?

A. 50 mL of 50% dextrose alone
B. 10 units of IV regular insulin with 50 mL of 50% dextrose, followed by a dextrose infusion
C. Nebulized albuterol alone, given her CKD
D. Undiluted sodium bicarbonate ampules
E. Oral sodium polystyrene sulfonate as the only treatment

**15.** A 61-year-old woman with essential thrombocythemia (platelets 1,400,000/µL) has serum potassium of 6.3 mEq/L. She has normal renal function, a normal ECG, and takes no medications that affect potassium. Which of the following is the most likely explanation?

A. Hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism
B. Addison disease
C. Tumor lysis syndrome
D. Pseudohyperkalemia from in vitro potassium release by platelets
E. Pseudohypoaldosteronism type II

## Answers

**1. A** — Acute symptomatic hyponatremia is an emergency. A 100 mL bolus of 3% saline is more effective than an infusion and raises sodium by 4–6 mEq/L to relieve severe symptoms, with sodium checked every 2–4 hours.
- B: Fluid restriction is far too slow for seizures.
- C: Vaptans have no approved role in acute hyponatremia.
- D: Isotonic saline won't correct this quickly.
- E: Demeclocycline is for chronic SIAD.
Source: Ch 0056, Treatment: Hyponatremia; Table 56-2

**2. C** — Chronic hyponatremia corrected more than 8–10 mEq/L in 24 hours risks osmotic demyelination. Overcorrection, common when AVP falls after saline for hypovolemia, should be reversed with desmopressin and/or D5W.
- A: Continued saline worsens the overcorrection.
- B: Hypertonic saline worsens the overcorrection.
- D: A vaptan worsens the overcorrection.
- E: ODS appears a day or more later, so absent symptoms aren't reassuring.
Source: Ch 0056, Treatment: Hyponatremia; Clinical Features

**3. E** — Euvolemic hyponatremia with concentrated urine, urine Na >30 and low uric acid indicates SIAD. A radiograph may miss small cell lung cancer, so chest CT should be considered in high-risk smokers.
- A: Saline doesn't treat SIAD and may worsen it.
- B: Brain MRI alone skips the most likely cause.
- C: Delay could miss a tumor.
- D: The cause must be sought.
Source: Ch 0056, Diagnostic Approach to Hyponatremia; Table 56-1

**4. B** — The urine-to-plasma electrolyte ratio is (110 + 60)/126 ≈ 1.35. A ratio >1 means minimal free water excretion, so restrict aggressively to <500 mL/day.
- A: Too liberal for a ratio >1.
- C: 500–700 mL/day is for a ratio of about 1.
- D: <1 L/day is for a ratio <1.
- E: Restriction is needed.
Source: Ch 0056, Treatment: Hyponatremia; Table 56-3

**5. D** — Hyperkalemia and hyponatremia in a hypotensive patient with high urine sodium strongly suggest primary adrenal insufficiency. A cosyntropin stimulation test is needed.
- A: Water deprivation evaluates DI.
- B: Chest CT evaluates SIAD causes.
- C: Uric acid helps with SIAD vs hypovolemia but doesn't diagnose adrenal failure.
- E: A renin-aldosterone ratio alone doesn't confirm adrenal failure.
Source: Ch 0056, Hypovolemic Hyponatremia; Diagnostic Approach

**6. A** — Beer potomania (low solute intake: urine osmolality <100–200, urine Na <10–20) corrects rapidly with saline and diet. These patients are at very high ODS risk from hypokalemia, alcoholism, malnutrition and overcorrection.
- B: Vaptans aren't indicated.
- C: Restriction isn't the main therapy.
- D: Potassium repletion raises serum sodium.
- E: That rate would cause ODS.
Source: Ch 0056, Low Solute Intake; Treatment: Hyponatremia

**7. C** — TBW = 0.6 × 70 = 42 L. Deficit = [(160 − 140)/140] × 42 ≈ 6 L. Give it over 48–72 hours without lowering sodium more than 10 mEq/L per day.
- A: Wrong volume and too fast.
- B: Too fast, and monitoring is required.
- D: Wrong volume and too fast.
- E: Correct volume but far too fast.
Source: Ch 0056, Table 56-3; Treatment: Hypernatremia

**8. E** — In hypernatremia, water deprivation is unnecessary and contraindicated. Separate central from nephrogenic DI by the urine osmolality response to DDAVP plus circulating AVP or copeptin. After pituitary surgery, central DI is likely.
- A: Water deprivation is contraindicated here.
- B: Hypertonic saline–stimulated copeptin is for hypotonic polyuria without hypernatremia.
- C: Calcium is a cause of NDI but doesn't distinguish the types.
- D: Biopsy isn't indicated.
Source: Ch 0056, Diagnostic Approach (hypernatremia)

**9. B** — Magnesium depletion reduces muscle K uptake and increases distal K secretion through ROMK. Hypomagnesemic patients are refractory to K replacement until Mg is repleted.
- A: Hypercalcemia doesn't explain the resistance.
- C: Hyperphosphatemia doesn't explain the resistance.
- D: Acidosis tends to raise K.
- E: Dextrose raises insulin and worsens hypokalemia; KCl should be given in saline, but that doesn't explain days of failure.
Source: Ch 0056, Magnesium Deficiency and Hypokalemia

**10. D** — In thyrotoxic periodic paralysis, β-adrenergic activity drives K into cells. High-dose propranolol (3 mg/kg) rapidly reverses the hypokalemia, hypophosphatemia and paralysis without rebound hyperkalemia.
- A: Aggressive IV KCl risks rebound hyperkalemia.
- B: Levothyroxine worsens thyrotoxicosis.
- C: Spironolactone is for aldosterone excess.
- E: Acetazolamide isn't the source's therapy.
Source: Ch 0056, Redistribution and Hypokalemia; Treatment: Hypokalemia

**11. A** — Random plasma renin activity and aldosterone are a helpful screen. A ratio >50 suggests primary hyperaldosteronism. Drugs that modulate the RAAS should be stopped; verapamil, α-blockers, hydralazine and low-dose amiloride don't interfere.
- B: Metanephrines screen for pheochromocytoma.
- C: Doppler alone doesn't screen for aldosteronism.
- D: Adrenal vein sampling follows a positive screen.
- E: Liddle syndrome has suppressed aldosterone and is a later consideration.
Source: Ch 0056, Renal Loss of Potassium (hyperaldosteronism)

**12. C** — Gitelman syndrome (NCC loss of function) causes hypokalemic alkalosis with hypomagnesemia and marked hypocalciuria. Urine Na, K and Cl stay elevated. Urinary calcium is the key test.
- A: Bartter causes hypercalciuria and polyuria.
- B: Vomiting gives urine Cl <10.
- D: Aldosteronism causes hypertension.
- E: Liddle causes hypertension.
Source: Ch 0056, Renal Loss of Potassium; Diagnostic Approach

**13. E** — ECG changes of hyperkalemia are an emergency. IV calcium stabilizes the myocardium within 1–3 minutes (lasting 30–60 minutes) while potassium is shifted and removed.
- A: SPS is slow and carries necrosis risk.
- B: Bicarbonate has no acute role, and undiluted boluses should be avoided.
- C: Diuretics don't work well in a dialysis patient and act slowly.
- D: Insulin comes after calcium, and alone it needs glucose unless glucose is ≥200–250.
Source: Ch 0056, Treatment: Hyperkalemia

**14. B** — Insulin 10 units IV with 50 mL of D50, then a dextrose infusion, lowers K in 10–20 minutes for 4–6 hours. Glucose is given unless the blood glucose is ≥200–250 mg/dL.
- A: D50 without insulin can worsen hyperkalemia (osmotic effect).
- C: About 20% of ESRD patients don't respond to albuterol, so it shouldn't be used alone.
- D: Undiluted bicarbonate boluses risk hypernatremia and have no acute role.
- E: SPS is slow and is a removal, not a shifting, strategy.
Source: Ch 0056, Treatment: Hyperkalemia

**15. D** — Marked thrombocytosis (or leukocytosis or erythrocytosis) can cause in vitro K efflux and pseudohyperkalemia. A normal ECG and no risk factors support this.
- A: Hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism affects diabetics, the elderly and CKD patients.
- B: Addison disease has hypotension and hyponatremia.
- C: Tumor lysis follows chemotherapy.
- E: PHA-II includes hypertension and acidosis.
Source: Ch 0056, Pseudohyperkalemia; Table 56-5
