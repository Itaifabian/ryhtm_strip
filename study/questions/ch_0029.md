# Ch 29 — Confusion and Delirium: practice questions

**1.** An 82-year-old woman is hospitalized for pneumonia and is receiving appropriate antibiotics. On the second night she becomes agitated and disoriented and repeatedly tries to pull out her IV line. She has no history of alcohol or sedative use. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial measure for her safety?

A. Wrist restraints
B. Scheduled haloperidol every 6 hours
C. Intravenous lorazepam
D. Prophylactic donepezil
E. A personal sitter or bed alarm with frequent reorientation

**2.** An 85-year-old man is evaluated on hospital day 3 because the nurses say he seems different. Using the Confusion Assessment Method, which of the following combinations establishes a diagnosis of delirium?

A. Acute onset and fluctuating course, disorganized thinking, and visual hallucinations
B. Acute onset and fluctuating course, inattention, and an altered level of consciousness
C. Inattention, disorganized thinking, and an altered level of consciousness with gradual onset over 2 years
D. Memory impairment and disorientation to date
E. Hallucinations, agitation, and insomnia

**3.** A 79-year-old woman with mild dementia is brought from her assisted-living facility because of 2 days of new confusion and drowsiness. Her medications haven't changed. Temperature is 37.9°C (100.2°F). She has no focal neurologic deficits or meningismus. CBC, electrolytes, calcium, magnesium, phosphorus, liver and renal function have been sent. Which of the following is the most appropriate additional initial evaluation?

A. Lumbar puncture
B. EEG
C. Paraneoplastic antibody panel
D. Urinalysis with urine culture and chest radiograph
E. Brain MRI with gadolinium as the sole next test

**4.** A 23-year-old man is brought to the emergency department by friends because he is agitated, disoriented and seeing things that are not there. He has no medical history. Vital signs show tachycardia. Which of the following is most appropriate to obtain early in his evaluation?

A. Serum and urine toxicology screen
B. Cortisol level
C. Paraneoplastic antibody panel
D. Vitamin B12 level
E. Antinuclear antibodies

**5.** A 74-year-old man has had 5 days of fluctuating confusion during hospitalization for a hip fracture. There have been periods of staring with subtle eyelid twitching. Medication review, basic laboratory studies, infection screening, and brain MRI are unrevealing. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Start a scheduled antipsychotic
B. Repeat the brain MRI
C. EEG
D. Discharge to a nursing home
E. Start a cholinesterase inhibitor

**6.** An 80-year-old woman becomes acutely confused and inattentive on hospital day 2. The day before, she was started on diphenhydramine at night for sleep and oxybutynin for urinary urgency. Vital signs, examination, and basic laboratory tests are normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Add lorazepam at night
B. Order brain MRI and lumbar puncture
C. Start quetiapine
D. Add donepezil
E. Discontinue diphenhydramine and oxybutynin

**7.** A 66-year-old man is admitted for elective knee replacement. On postoperative day 2 he develops tremor, diaphoresis, tachycardia, hypertension, agitation and visual hallucinations. His wife reports he drinks four glasses of wine every evening. Which of the following is the most appropriate treatment?

A. Low-dose haloperidol alone
B. A benzodiazepine
C. Physical restraints and observation
D. Dexmedetomidine as sole therapy for withdrawal
E. Avoid all sedating medication because benzodiazepines worsen delirium

**8.** A 77-year-old man in the hospital is alert but has tangential speech and loses track of the conversation. When asked to repeat random strings of digits said at one per second, he can correctly repeat only 3 digits. His hearing and language are normal. This finding most directly indicates which of the following?

A. Aphasia
B. Short-term memory loss
C. Executive dysfunction
D. An attentional deficit
E. Visuospatial impairment

**9.** A hospital is designing a delirium-prevention protocol for patients aged 70 years and older admitted to medical wards. Which of the following interventions is supported?

A. Ensure glasses and hearing aids are worn, mobilize patients out of bed for meals, and remove unnecessary catheters and monitoring tethers
B. Give scheduled low-dose haloperidol to all high-risk patients
C. Start a cholinesterase inhibitor on admission
D. Give nighttime benzodiazepines to protect sleep
E. Leave room lights on at night so patients can orient themselves

**10.** A 70-year-old man is mechanically ventilated in the ICU for septic shock and needs continuous sedation. Which of the following strategies is most likely to reduce his risk of delirium?

A. Continuous midazolam infusion without interruption
B. Scheduled prophylactic haloperidol
C. Dexmedetomidine with daily interruption of sedation and reorientation by staff
D. Constant lighting and 24-hour activity in the room to keep him oriented
E. Prophylactic rivastigmine

**11.** A 68-year-old woman is brought in with 1 day of fever to 39.2°C (102.6°F), headache and confusion. On examination she is inattentive and has neck stiffness. There is no papilledema. Which of the following is the most appropriate sequence of evaluation?

A. EEG first, then outpatient MRI
B. Urinalysis only, and observe
C. Toxicology screen, then reassess in 24 hours
D. Thyroid function and cortisol levels, then lumbar puncture if abnormal
E. Brain imaging followed immediately by lumbar puncture

**12.** A previously healthy 64-year-old man developed delirium after an uncomplicated inguinal hernia repair. It resolved within 3 days once his pain medication was adjusted. His wife had noticed no cognitive problems before surgery. Which of the following is the most appropriate follow-up?

A. No follow-up; postoperative delirium is benign and self-limited
B. Outpatient cognitive screening for an unrecognized underlying neurologic disorder
C. Long-term low-dose antipsychotic
D. Avoid all future surgery
E. Start a cholinesterase inhibitor indefinitely

**13.** A 58-year-old man with cirrhosis is confused and lethargic. When he holds his arms outstretched with wrists extended, there are brief, irregular lapses in posture. Multifocal myoclonic jerks are also seen. These motor findings most suggest which category of delirium?

A. Focal ischemic stroke
B. Primary psychiatric disorder
C. Nonconvulsive status epilepticus
D. A metabolic or toxic etiology
E. Dementia with Lewy bodies

## Answers

**1. E** — Bed alarms and personal sitters are more effective and much less disorienting than physical restraints. Supportive reorientation is the cornerstone of delirium management.
- A: Physical restraints are themselves a risk factor for delirium.
- B: Antipsychotics don't prevent or treat delirium, raise mortality in the elderly, and are a last resort given only as needed.
- C: Benzodiazepines worsen confusion and are for alcohol or sedative withdrawal.
- D: Cholinesterase inhibitors haven't been shown to prevent or treat delirium.
Source: Ch 0029, Treatment: Delirium

**2. B** — The CAM requires (1) acute onset and fluctuating course and (2) inattention, plus either (3) disorganized thinking or (4) an altered level of consciousness.
- A: Inattention, the required core feature, is missing.
- C: Gradual onset over years suggests dementia rather than delirium.
- D: Memory loss and disorientation alone don't meet the criteria.
- E: Hallucinations and agitation are associated features, not CAM criteria.
Source: Ch 0029, Approach to the Patient: Delirium

**3. D** — Besides the basic labs, screening for systemic infection (urinalysis and culture, chest radiograph, possibly blood cultures) is important in elderly patients with delirium. UTI in a patient with dementia is a classic scenario.
- A: LP is for suspected CNS infection or a negative workup. She has no meningismus, and LP should follow imaging.
- B: EEG is for suspected seizures or an unexplained cause after the initial workup.
- C: Autoimmune and paraneoplastic testing is reserved for an unclear diagnosis after initial testing.
- E: Imaging is often unhelpful in delirium and shouldn't replace the infection screen.
Source: Ch 0029, Table 29-2; Laboratory and Diagnostic Evaluation

**4. A** — Illicit drugs and toxins are common causes of delirium in younger patients, so serum and urine toxicology screening belongs earlier in their workup.
- B: Endocrine tests are second-tier.
- C: Paraneoplastic testing is second-tier.
- D: Vitamin testing is second-tier.
- E: Autoimmune serologies are second-tier, reserved for an unclear diagnosis.
Source: Ch 0029, Etiology; Table 29-2

**5. C** — Nonconvulsive status epilepticus and recurrent seizures can cause fluctuating delirium. EEG is essential and should be done whenever the cause remains unclear after the initial workup, immediately if suspicion is high.
- A: Antipsychotics don't treat delirium and would miss the cause.
- B: MRI was just unrevealing.
- D: A treatable cause hasn't been excluded.
- E: Cholinesterase inhibitors aren't effective for delirium.
Source: Ch 0029, Etiology (seizures); Table 29-2

**6. E** — Anticholinergic drugs are common precipitants, and medications cause about one-third of delirium in the elderly. With a clear precipitant, the offending drugs should be stopped, and further testing may not be required.
- A: Benzodiazepines worsen confusion.
- B: Extensive testing isn't needed when a clear precipitant explains the delirium.
- C: Antipsychotics don't treat delirium.
- D: Cholinesterase inhibitors haven't been proven for delirium.
Source: Ch 0029, Etiology; Laboratory and Diagnostic Evaluation; Treatment

**7. B** — This is hyperactive delirium from alcohol withdrawal. Even a few daily drinks can cause significant withdrawal on admission. Benzodiazepine use in delirium is appropriate only when it is caused by alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal.
- A: Antipsychotics don't treat the underlying withdrawal.
- C: Restraints don't treat the cause and worsen delirium.
- D: The source discusses dexmedetomidine as an ICU sedative less likely to cause delirium, not as withdrawal treatment.
- E: Withdrawal is the exception to the rule against benzodiazepines.
Source: Ch 0029, Etiology; Treatment: Delirium

**8. D** — Forward digit span is a quick, fairly sensitive bedside test of attention. Healthy adults repeat 5–7 digits. A span of 4 or fewer indicates an attentional deficit, the neuropsychological hallmark of delirium.
- A: Aphasia is a language disorder, and his language is normal.
- B: Digit span forward tests attention, not memory storage.
- C: Executive function isn't what this test measures.
- E: Visuospatial impairment isn't tested by digit repetition.
Source: Ch 0029, Physical Examination

**9. A** — Prevention protocols target sensory impairment, immobility, sleep and dehydration. Glasses and hearing aids, getting out of bed for meals, and removing unnecessary tethers (Foley, telemetry, nasal cannula, continuous oximetry) are part of the delirium-reduction checklist.
- B: Antipsychotics haven't prevented delirium in trials.
- C: Cholinesterase inhibitors failed in prevention trials.
- D: Benzodiazepines worsen confusion.
- E: Nights should be dark and quiet to preserve the sleep-wake cycle.
Source: Ch 0029, Prevention; Fig 29-1

**10. C** — ICU strategies include sedatives less likely to cause delirium, such as dexmedetomidine, and protocols for daily sedation interruption with reorientation.
- A: Benzodiazepines promote delirium.
- B: Antipsychotics don't prevent delirium.
- D: Constant 24-hour activity commonly provokes delirium.
- E: Cholinesterase inhibitors haven't been shown to prevent delirium.
Source: Ch 0029, Prevention

**11. E** — When CNS infection is suspected, lumbar puncture must be done immediately after neuroimaging.
- A: Delaying CSF analysis in suspected meningitis is dangerous.
- B: Meningismus and fever require CNS evaluation.
- C: Observation delays treatment of a possible CNS infection.
- D: Endocrine testing is second-tier and must not delay LP.
Source: Ch 0029, Laboratory and Diagnostic Evaluation; Table 29-2

**12. B** — Delirium after a minor insult in a previously healthy person may unmask decreased cerebral reserve ("stress test for the brain"). After the episode clears, careful outpatient screening for an underlying condition should follow.
- A: Delirium isn't benign; it carries morbidity and may herald neurodegeneration.
- C: Antipsychotics aren't indicated.
- D: Future surgery isn't contraindicated.
- E: There's no diagnosis to justify a cholinesterase inhibitor.
Source: Ch 0029, Pathogenesis; Risk Factors

**13. D** — Asterixis and multifocal myoclonus are nonspecific but usually indicate a metabolic or toxic cause of delirium, such as hepatic encephalopathy here.
- A: Focal strokes rarely cause delirium and produce focal deficits, not asterixis.
- B: Psychiatric disorders don't cause asterixis.
- C: EEG is needed to diagnose nonconvulsive status, and asterixis doesn't suggest it.
- E: Dementia with Lewy bodies features parkinsonism and visual hallucinations.
Source: Ch 0029, Physical Examination
