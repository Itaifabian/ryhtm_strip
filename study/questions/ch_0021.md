# Ch 21 — Fever and Rash: practice questions

**1.** A 4-year-old boy has had 1 day of fever and a rash that began as pink macules and is rapidly becoming petechial on his trunk and legs. Temperature is 39.6°C (103.3°F), pulse is 150/min, and blood pressure is 82/46 mm Hg. His daycare had an outbreak of echovirus 9 infection last week. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Supportive care for presumed enteroviral infection
B. Obtain enteroviral PCR and wait for the result before treating
C. Treat as bacterial sepsis with empiric antibiotics
D. Skin biopsy of a petechial lesion before any treatment
E. Discharge with close follow-up

**2.** A 34-year-old man from Virginia went hiking 6 days ago and removed a tick from his leg. He now has fever, severe headache, myalgias, and abdominal pain. Temperature is 39.4°C (102.9°F). Small erythematous macules, some becoming petechial, are present on his wrists and ankles and are beginning to spread to his arms and legs. His palms and soles are spared. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Begin treatment for Rocky Mountain spotted fever now
B. Wait for Rickettsia serology before treatment
C. Withhold treatment because the palms and soles are not involved
D. Treat for Lyme disease and reassess in 2 weeks
E. Observe, because tick-borne illness is unlikely without an eschar

**3.** A 29-year-old woman was started on trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for a urinary tract infection 9 days ago. She now has an intensely itchy, bright-red, symmetric eruption of macules and papules on her trunk and extremities. Temperature is 37.9°C (100.2°F). She feels otherwise well. There are no mucosal lesions, blisters, or facial swelling. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Continue the antibiotic and add an antihistamine
B. Measles serology
C. Skin biopsy
D. Discontinue trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole
E. Admit to a burn unit

**4.** A 41-year-old man started carbamazepine 4 weeks ago. He now has fever, a widespread maculopapular rash, marked facial swelling, and tender lymphadenopathy. Laboratory studies show eosinophils 1800/μL, atypical lymphocytes, and ALT 410 U/L. There are no mucosal erosions. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Toxic epidermal necrolysis
B. Infectious mononucleosis
C. Measles
D. Drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS)
E. Staphylococcal scalded-skin syndrome

**5.** A 58-year-old woman has 1 week of fever and multiple tender, edematous red plaques on her face, neck, and arms that look almost blistered. CBC shows a WBC of 14,000/μL with neutrophilia, a hemoglobin of 9.1 g/dL, and a platelet count of 88,000/μL. Skin biopsy shows a dense dermal neutrophilic infiltrate without infection. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Reassurance only
B. Evaluate for an underlying hematologic malignancy
C. Start empiric antifungal therapy
D. Patch testing for contact allergens
E. Treat for disseminated gonococcal infection

**6.** A 62-year-old man with alcohol-related cirrhosis ate raw oysters 2 days ago. He now has fever and painful hemorrhagic bullae on both legs that are evolving into necrotic ulcers. Blood pressure is 78/40 mm Hg. Which of the following is the most likely cause?

A. Group A Streptococcus
B. Pseudomonas aeruginosa
C. Staphylococcus aureus
D. Neisseria meningitidis
E. Vibrio vulnificus

**7.** A 47-year-old woman receiving chemotherapy for leukemia has an absolute neutrophil count of 50/μL. She develops fever and hypotension and an indurated plaque in the groin that becomes a hemorrhagic bulla and then a black eschar with a red halo. Which organism is most likely responsible?

A. Vibrio vulnificus
B. Bacillus anthracis
C. Pseudomonas aeruginosa
D. Rickettsia akari
E. Francisella tularensis

**8.** A 3-year-old girl has fever, irritability, and diffuse red skin that is exquisitely painful to touch. Light lateral pressure on the skin causes the upper layer to wrinkle and slough. There is crusting around the nose but no oral, ocular, or genital erosions. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Staphylococcal scalded-skin syndrome
B. Toxic epidermal necrolysis
C. Scarlet fever
D. Kawasaki disease
E. Stevens-Johnson syndrome

**9.** A 22-year-old woman has a sudden fever of 39.8°C (103.6°F), vomiting, watery diarrhea, and dizziness on day 3 of her menstrual period. Blood pressure is 78/44 mm Hg. She has diffuse erythema including her palms, red conjunctivae, and a red oral mucosa. There is no wound, soft-tissue infection, or pneumonia. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Streptococcal toxic shock syndrome
B. Scarlet fever
C. Acute meningococcemia
D. Staphylococcal toxic shock syndrome
E. Rocky Mountain spotted fever

**10.** A 36-year-old man has 2 weeks of fever, joint pain, and itchy, burning red wheals on his trunk and thighs. Individual lesions last 3–4 days and leave bruise-like marks; they do not fully blanch. ALT is 1200 U/L. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Begin a nonsedating antihistamine and reassure him
B. Test for acute hepatitis B
C. Allergen skin-prick testing
D. Stool ova and parasite examination only
E. Measure serum tryptase

**11.** A 26-year-old woman has 2 weeks of a scaly, copper-colored papular rash on her trunk, palms, and soles, with low-grade fever and malaise. She is sexually active with new partners. Her physician initially suspected pityriasis rosea. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Topical glucocorticoids and reassurance
B. Skin biopsy for fungal culture
C. Antinuclear antibody testing
D. Measles IgM
E. Serologic testing for syphilis

**12.** A 19-year-old college student is hospitalized for the second time in 3 years with meningococcal sepsis. She has no history of splenectomy. Which of the following is the most appropriate test to evaluate the underlying cause of her recurrent infection?

A. HIV antibody test
B. Serum immunoglobulin levels only
C. Terminal complement (C5–C8) assessment
D. Peripheral smear for Howell-Jolly bodies only
E. Sweat chloride test

**13.** A pregnant nurse at 10 weeks' gestation works on a unit where a 25-year-old patient has been diagnosed with rubella (fever, a rash spreading down from the hairline, postauricular adenopathy, and arthritis). Which of the following is the most appropriate recommendation for the nurse?

A. Avoid contact with the patient
B. Continue routine care with a surgical mask
C. Terminate the pregnancy
D. Begin oral acyclovir prophylaxis
E. No precautions are needed because rubella is mild in adults

**14.** Several members of a swim team develop itchy red follicular papules and pustules on the buttocks, hips, and lower abdomen, in the areas covered by their swimsuits, 2 days after using a hotel hot tub. A 20-year-old team member has no fever and feels well. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?

A. Oral ciprofloxacin for 14 days
B. IV antibiotics
C. Oral acyclovir
D. Reassurance, because the condition is generally self-limited
E. Isolate him as a suspected varicella case

**15.** A 6-year-old boy has a lacy, reticular rash on his arms and trunk that appeared after his fever resolved. Two days earlier he had bright red cheeks with pallor around the mouth. His mother is 18 weeks pregnant and has had no rash. Which of the following complications is the mother's fetus at greatest risk for if she becomes infected?

A. Congenital cataracts
B. Fetal hydrops
C. Microcephaly
D. Chorioretinitis
E. Limb hypoplasia

## Answers

**1. C** — Echovirus 9 infection can mimic acute meningococcemia, and prompt differentiation may be impossible. Patients should be treated as if they have bacterial sepsis.
- A, E: The outbreak history doesn't exclude meningococcemia, and he is hypotensive.
- B, D: Delaying treatment for testing risks death from meningococcemia.
Source: Ch 0021, Purpuric Eruptions

**2. A** — In RMSF, lesions start on the wrists and ankles, spread centripetally, and reach the palms and soles only later. Early therapy is critical because mortality is up to 40% if untreated.
- B: Waiting for serology delays treatment.
- C: Palms and soles are involved late, so sparing them doesn't exclude RMSF.
- D: Erythema migrans is an annular lesion, not acral petechiae.
- E: RMSF doesn't typically produce an eschar.
Source: Ch 0021, Peripheral Eruptions; Table 21-1

**3. D** — An exanthematous drug eruption is intensely pruritic and bright red, typically 2–3 weeks after a new drug. A new medication with no prostration favors a drug cause. Stop the drug; the rash may persist up to 2 weeks afterward.
- A: Continuing the culprit drug risks progression.
- B: Measles causes prostration, cough, coryza and conjunctivitis, starting at the hairline.
- C: Biopsy isn't needed for a typical presentation.
- E: There are no SJS/TEN features (mucosal lesions, blisters).
Source: Ch 0021, Centrally Distributed Maculopapular Eruptions; Table 21-1

**4. D** — DRESS (DIHS) follows aromatic anticonvulsants, sulfonamides or minocycline. It causes a rash that mimics a drug exanthem, profound facial edema, lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia, atypical lymphocytes and organ failure (especially hepatic). Mortality is about 10%.
- A: TEN involves mucosa and sloughing of the full epidermis.
- B: Mono doesn't cause eosinophilia after a new anticonvulsant.
- C: Measles has Koplik spots and the 3 Cs.
- E: SSSS affects young children or adults with renal failure and spares mucosa.
Source: Ch 0021, Confluent Desquamative Erythemas; Table 21-1

**5. B** — Sweet syndrome (acute febrile neutrophilic dermatosis) causes edematous plaques that look vesicular. About 20% of cases are associated with malignancy, usually hematologic. Her anemia and thrombocytopenia make that evaluation essential.
- A: Reassurance ignores the malignancy association.
- C: The biopsy shows no infection.
- D: This isn't contact dermatitis.
- E: DGI causes sparse hemorrhagic pustules near joints.
Source: Ch 0021, Nodular Eruptions; Table 21-1

**6. E** — Disseminated *Vibrio vulnificus* infection occurs in patients with cirrhosis, diabetes or renal failure after eating contaminated seafood or exposure to saltwater. Lesions progress from erythema to hemorrhagic bullae to necrotic ulcers, with hypotension and about 50% mortality.
- A: Strep TSS follows a severe GAS soft-tissue infection, not raw oysters.
- B: Ecthyma gangrenosum mainly affects neutropenic patients.
- C, D: Neither fits the seafood exposure in cirrhosis.
Source: Ch 0021, Vesiculobullous or Pustular Eruptions; Table 21-1

**7. C** — Ecthyma gangrenosum (an indurated plaque → hemorrhagic bulla → eschar with a red halo, in the axilla, groin or perianal area) usually affects neutropenic patients. It occurs in up to 28% of patients with *Pseudomonas* bacteremia.
- A: Vibrio follows seafood exposure in cirrhosis.
- B: Anthrax causes a painless ulcer with surrounding vesicles and edema after animal exposure.
- D: Rickettsialpox comes from urban mouse mites and causes a mild illness.
- E: Tularemia causes an ulceroglandular lesion after tick or animal exposure.
Source: Ch 0021, Vesiculobullous or Pustular Eruptions; Table 21-1

**8. A** — Staphylococcal scalded-skin syndrome affects children under 10 (or adults with renal dysfunction). It causes profoundly tender erythema and a positive Nikolsky sign from a superficial epidermal split. Mucosa is spared.
- B, E: SJS/TEN involve mucosal surfaces and full-thickness epidermal necrosis, usually from drugs, and are uncommon in children.
- C: Scarlet fever has a sandpaper rash and strawberry tongue without sloughing.
- D: Kawasaki causes cracked lips and conjunctivitis without Nikolsky-positive sloughing.
Source: Ch 0021, Confluent Desquamative Erythemas; Table 21-1

**9. D** — Staphylococcal toxic shock syndrome causes fever above 39°C, hypotension, diffuse erythema including the palms, mucosal erythema and multiorgan dysfunction. Usually only colonization is found, not a severe infection.
- A: Strep TSS typically occurs with a severe group A streptococcal infection such as necrotizing fasciitis.
- B: Scarlet fever follows pharyngitis without shock.
- C: Meningococcemia is petechial or purpuric.
- E: RMSF follows a tick bite and is petechial, starting at the wrists and ankles.
Source: Ch 0021, Confluent Desquamative Erythemas; Table 21-1

**10. B** — Urticaria-like lesions with fever that last more than 24 hours (up to 5 days) and don't fully blanch suggest urticarial vasculitis. Causes include serum sickness from infection such as acute hepatitis B, which fits his markedly raised ALT.
- A: Classic urticaria lasts under 24 hours without fever; these lesions don't fit.
- C, E: These evaluate allergic urticaria, not vasculitis.
- D: Parasites are a cause, but the hepatitis picture points to hepatitis B first.
Source: Ch 0021, Urticaria-Like Eruptions; Table 21-1

**11. E** — Secondary syphilis causes a copper-colored, scaly papular eruption that is prominent on the palms and soles. It should be considered in the differential of pityriasis rosea, especially in sexually active patients.
- A: Treating as pityriasis misses a treatable STI.
- B: Nothing suggests a fungal cause.
- C: Lupus rash is malar and photosensitive.
- D: Measles spares the palms and soles.
Source: Ch 0021, Peripheral Eruptions; Table 21-1

**12. C** — Acute meningococcemia is most common in children, people with asplenia and those with terminal complement component deficiency (C5–C8). Recurrent neisserial disease points to complement testing.
- A, B: These evaluate other immune defects that the chapter doesn't link to neisserial infection.
- D: She has no asplenia history, and a smear alone would miss complement deficiency.
- E: Sweat chloride tests for cystic fibrosis.
Source: Ch 0021, Purpuric Eruptions; Table 21-1

**13. A** — Rubella causes severe congenital abnormalities, so pregnant women should avoid exposure to ill people.
- B: A mask alone doesn't meet the recommendation to avoid exposure.
- C: Exposure alone isn't infection.
- D: Acyclovir has no activity against rubella.
- E: The danger is to the fetus, not the adult.
Source: Ch 0021, Centrally Distributed Maculopapular Eruptions

**14. D** — *Pseudomonas* hot-tub folliculitis occurs in outbreaks after hot-tub or pool use. It causes pruritic follicular papules and pustules in areas covered by bathing suits and is generally self-limited.
- A, B: Antibiotics aren't needed for this self-limited condition in a well patient.
- C: This isn't herpes.
- E: Varicella lesions appear in crops in all stages, mainly on the trunk, and aren't confined to the swimsuit area.
Source: Ch 0021, Vesiculobullous or Pustular Eruptions; Table 21-1

**15. B** — Erythema infectiosum (parvovirus B19) causes slapped cheeks and then a lacy rash after the fever. In pregnant women, it can be associated with fetal hydrops.
- A: Cataracts are a congenital rubella defect.
- C: Microcephaly is linked to Zika.
- D: Chorioretinitis isn't the parvovirus complication described.
- E: Limb hypoplasia isn't described for parvovirus.
Source: Ch 0021, Centrally Distributed Maculopapular Eruptions; Table 21-1
