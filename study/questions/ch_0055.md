# Ch 55 — Azotemia and Urinary Abnormalities: practice questions

**1.** A 69-year-old man is found to have a serum creatinine of 3.1 mg/dL on routine labs. One year ago it was 1.0 mg/dL. He has no prior records in between. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial evaluation?

A. Renal biopsy
B. Renal angiography
C. 24-hour urine protein only
D. Urinalysis and renal ultrasound
E. CT with IV contrast

**2.** A 78-year-old woman has 4 days of vomiting and diarrhea. Creatinine is 2.4 mg/dL (baseline 0.9). BUN:creatinine ratio is 32:1, urine sodium 12 mEq/L, urine osmolality 640 mosm/kg, and FENa 0.3%. Urine sediment is bland. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?

A. IV volume repletion
B. Renal biopsy
C. Start furosemide
D. Urgent dialysis
E. Start an ACE inhibitor

**3.** A 59-year-old man had prolonged hypotension during emergency surgery 2 days ago and now has rising creatinine and oliguria. Which of the following urine findings is most predictive of acute tubular necrosis?

A. Urine osmolality 620 mosm/kg
B. FENa 0.4%
C. Hyaline casts only
D. Urine sodium 10 mEq/L
E. Muddy brown granular casts and renal tubular epithelial cells

**4.** A 72-year-old man with hypertension, diffuse atherosclerosis and bilateral abdominal bruits starts lisinopril. Two weeks later his creatinine has risen from 1.1 to 2.6 mg/dL. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Double the lisinopril dose
B. Stop lisinopril and evaluate for bilateral renal artery stenosis
C. Add an NSAID for pain
D. Renal biopsy
E. Add an ARB

**5.** A 74-year-old woman develops acute kidney injury 3 weeks after cardiac catheterization. She has livedo reticularis on her legs, painful blue toes and peripheral eosinophilia. Urinalysis is nearly normal. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Contrast-associated ATN
B. Prerenal azotemia
C. Atheroembolic renal disease
D. Acute glomerulonephritis
E. Bilateral ureteral obstruction

**6.** A 54-year-old woman taking omeprazole for 3 months has a creatinine rise from 0.8 to 2.0 mg/dL. Urinalysis shows pyuria, mild proteinuria, and occasional WBC casts. Urine culture is negative. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Order urine eosinophils to confirm the diagnosis
B. Start empiric antibiotics
C. Increase omeprazole
D. Discontinue omeprazole (renal biopsy if the diagnosis remains uncertain)
E. Start an ACE inhibitor

**7.** A 41-year-old man has new hypertension, edema and a creatinine of 1.3 mg/dL. Urinalysis shows dysmorphic red cells, red cell casts and protein of 1.8 g/day. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Serologic evaluation (ANCA, anti-GBM, complement, hepatitis serologies, etc.) and renal biopsy
B. Repeat urinalysis in 6 months
C. Cystoscopy
D. CT urography alone
E. Start empiric antibiotics

**8.** A 23-year-old man has dark urine after an intense workout. Urine dipstick is strongly positive for blood, but microscopy shows 0–1 RBC/HPF. Creatine kinase is 52,000 U/L. Which of the following best explains the dipstick result?

A. Glomerulonephritis
B. Kidney stone
C. Hemolysis from exercise only
D. Laboratory error
E. Myoglobinuria

**9.** A 63-year-old man who smokes has painless microscopic hematuria with 15 RBCs/HPF on three separate urinalyses. RBCs aren't dysmorphic, and there's no proteinuria or casts. Urine culture is negative. Which of the following is the most appropriate evaluation?

A. Reassurance and repeat in 1 year
B. Urine cytology, renal imaging, and cystoscopy
C. Renal biopsy
D. ANCA and anti-GBM antibodies
E. Treat empirically with antibiotics

**10.** A 67-year-old woman with anemia, hypercalcemia and back pain has acute kidney injury. Her urine dipstick is negative for protein, but she is suspected of having multiple myeloma. Which test is most appropriate to detect urinary light chains?

A. Repeat dipstick on a concentrated sample
B. Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio
C. Sulfosalicylic acid precipitation or urine protein electrophoresis
D. Urine eosinophils
E. Urine culture

**11.** A 61-year-old woman with known retroperitoneal fibrosis has acute kidney injury. Renal ultrasound shows no hydronephrosis. Obstruction is still suspected. Which of the following is the most appropriate next test?

A. Furosemide renogram (MAG3)
B. Repeat ultrasound in 6 months
C. Renal biopsy
D. Urine eosinophils
E. No further testing; a normal ultrasound excludes obstruction

**12.** A 35-year-old woman has 4 L/day of urine output and thirst. Urine osmolality is 90 mosm/kg. Blood glucose is normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step to distinguish the cause?

A. Search for a nonabsorbed solute such as glucose or mannitol
B. Renal biopsy
C. Start a thiazide empirically
D. Water deprivation test with desmopressin, or a plasma AVP or copeptin level
E. CT of the abdomen

**13.** A 28-year-old professional bodybuilder has a serum creatinine of 1.6 mg/dL and a creatinine-based eGFR of 58. Urinalysis and blood pressure are normal. Which of the following is the best next step to estimate his GFR?

A. Renal biopsy
B. Cystatin C–based eGFR (or combined creatinine–cystatin C)
C. Repeat creatinine after a meat-rich meal
D. Diagnose stage 3 CKD
E. Start an ACE inhibitor

**14.** A 39-year-old man with nephrotic syndrome (proteinuria 9 g/day, albumin 1.9 g/dL) develops renal vein thrombosis. Which mechanism contributes to his hypercoagulable state?

A. Increased antithrombin III
B. Urinary loss of antithrombin III, low proteins S and C, and hyperfibrinogenemia
C. Thrombocytopenia
D. Low fibrinogen
E. Excess protein C

## Answers

**1. D** — Urinalysis and renal ultrasound help separate acute from chronic disease and identify obstruction, the first branch of the azotemia algorithm.
- A: Biopsy is for specific indications such as RBC casts.
- B: Angiography is for suspected vascular occlusion.
- C: A protein measurement alone doesn't stage the problem.
- E: Contrast adds nephrotoxic risk without first-line benefit.
Source: Ch 0055, Approach to the Patient: Azotemia; Fig 55-1

**2. A** — Volume loss with BUN:Cr >20:1, urine Na <20, urine osmolality >500, FENa <1% and bland sediment indicates prerenal azotemia, which reverses with restored perfusion.
- B: Biopsy isn't needed.
- C: A diuretic worsens volume depletion.
- D: Dialysis is unnecessary.
- E: An ACE inhibitor worsens prerenal AKI.
Source: Ch 0055, Prerenal Failure; Table 55-2

**3. E** — Dark (muddy brown) granular casts and tubular epithelial cells are highly predictive of ATN.
- A: Concentrated urine suggests prerenal azotemia.
- B: A low FENa suggests prerenal azotemia.
- C: Hyaline casts suggest prerenal azotemia.
- D: A low urine sodium suggests prerenal azotemia.
Source: Ch 0055, Prerenal Failure; Table 55-2

**4. B** — Patients with bilateral renal artery stenosis depend on angiotensin II–mediated efferent constriction to maintain filtration and can lose GFR precipitously with ACE inhibitors or ARBs.
- A: More drug worsens the hemodynamic injury.
- C: NSAIDs block prostaglandin afferent dilation and worsen it.
- D: The mechanism is hemodynamic.
- E: An ARB has the same effect.
Source: Ch 0055, Prerenal Failure

**5. C** — Atheroembolic renal failure usually follows aortic instrumentation. Cholesterol emboli cause an eosinophil-rich reaction with livedo reticularis, distal infarcts and eosinophilia. The urinalysis is often normal, and biopsy is often unnecessary when these stigmata are present.
- A: Contrast ATN occurs soon after exposure, without livedo or eosinophilia.
- B: The systemic findings point to emboli.
- D: GN would show an active sediment.
- E: Obstruction isn't suggested.
Source: Ch 0055, Intrinsic Renal Disease

**6. D** — Drug-induced acute interstitial nephritis (antibiotics, NSAIDs, PPIs) shows pyuria, mild proteinuria, hematuria and sometimes WBC casts. Stop the drug. Biopsy distinguishes it from other causes if needed.
- A: Urine eosinophils have no diagnostic utility and are no longer recommended.
- B: The culture is negative.
- C: Continuing the drug worsens the injury.
- E: An ACE inhibitor isn't indicated.
Source: Ch 0055, Intrinsic Renal Disease

**7. A** — Dysmorphic RBCs, RBC casts and proteinuria >500 mg/day are virtually diagnostic of glomerulonephritis. Even without azotemia, serologic evaluation and early renal biopsy are indicated.
- B: Delay risks progression.
- C: The source is glomerular, not urologic.
- D: Imaging alone misses the glomerular cause.
- E: Antibiotics don't treat GN.
Source: Ch 0055, Hematuria, Pyuria, and Casts; Fig 55-2

**8. E** — A positive blood dipstick without RBCs on microscopy can result from myoglobinuria, often with rhabdomyolysis.
- A: GN would show RBCs on microscopy.
- B: A stone would show RBCs on microscopy.
- C: Hemoglobinuria from hemolysis is possible, but the very high CK points to muscle.
- D: The pattern is classic, not an error.
Source: Ch 0055, Hematuria, Pyuria, and Casts

**9. B** — Persistent hematuria (>3 RBC/HPF on three urinalyses) carries a 9.1% rate of significant lesions. Suspicion for urogenital cancer rises with age. Nondysmorphic isolated hematuria calls for cytology, imaging and cystoscopy.
- A: Reassurance is inappropriate for persistent hematuria at this age.
- C: Biopsy is for glomerular features.
- D: Serologies are for glomerular features.
- E: The culture is negative.
Source: Ch 0055, Hematuria, Pyuria, and Casts; Fig 55-2

**10. C** — Dipsticks detect only albumin and miss Bence Jones proteins. Total urine protein needs sulfosalicylic or trichloroacetic acid precipitation; UPEP characterizes light chains.
- A: The dipstick still won't detect light chains.
- B: ACR measures albumin only.
- D: Urine eosinophils aren't relevant.
- E: Culture doesn't detect protein.
Source: Ch 0055, Proteinuria

**11. A** — Ultrasound can be falsely negative early in obstruction or when the ureters can't dilate (retroperitoneal fibrosis, tumor encasement). A furosemide renogram (MAG3) may be needed.
- B: Delay risks irreversible damage.
- C: Biopsy doesn't assess obstruction.
- D: Urine eosinophils aren't helpful.
- E: A normal ultrasound doesn't exclude obstruction in this setting.
Source: Ch 0055, Postrenal Azotemia

**12. D** — Urine output >3 L/day with dilute urine (<250 mosm/L) is a water diuresis: primary polydipsia, central DI or nephrogenic DI. Plasma AVP or copeptin, or water deprivation with desmopressin, separates them.
- A: Solute diuresis has urine osmolality >300.
- B: Biopsy isn't indicated.
- C: Empiric treatment skips the diagnosis.
- E: CT isn't indicated.
Source: Ch 0055, Polyuria; Fig 55-4

**13. B** — Creatinine-based eGFR underestimates GFR in people with high muscle mass. Cystatin C is less affected by muscle mass, and combined equations are more accurate.
- A: Biopsy is premature.
- C: Cooked meat raises creatinine further.
- D: The diagnosis may be wrong.
- E: Treatment isn't indicated yet.
Source: Ch 0055, Assessment of GFR

**14. B** — The hypercoagulable state of nephrotic syndrome comes from urinary loss of antithrombin III, reduced proteins S and C, hyperfibrinogenemia and enhanced platelet aggregation.
- A: Antithrombin III is lost, not increased.
- C: Platelet aggregation is enhanced, not reduced.
- D: Fibrinogen is high.
- E: Protein C is low.
Source: Ch 0055, Proteinuria (nephrotic syndrome)
