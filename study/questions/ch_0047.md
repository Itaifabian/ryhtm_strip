# Ch 47 — Dysphagia: practice questions

**1.** A 72-year-old man had a left hemispheric stroke 5 days ago. Since then he coughs when drinking water, and some liquid has come out of his nose during meals. Which of the following is the most appropriate test?

A. Upper endoscopy
B. Esophageal manometry
C. Videofluoroscopic swallow study
D. Barium esophagram with a tablet
E. Chest CT

**2.** A 28-year-old man with asthma and allergic rhinitis has had intermittent difficulty swallowing solid foods for 3 years. He once needed emergency removal of impacted meat. He has no weight loss. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Barium swallow only
B. Esophageal manometry
C. Empiric PPI without testing
D. Videofluoroscopic swallow study
E. Upper endoscopy with esophageal biopsies

**3.** A 45-year-old woman with unexplained intermittent solid-food dysphagia undergoes upper endoscopy. The esophageal mucosa looks completely normal, with no rings, furrows or strictures. Which of the following is the most appropriate action during the procedure?

A. End the procedure; no further action is needed
B. Perform empiric dilation without biopsies
C. Biopsy only the stomach
D. Plan esophageal manometry without biopsies
E. Obtain esophageal mucosal biopsies

**4.** A 52-year-old woman has 8 months of dysphagia to both solids and liquids from the onset, with regurgitation of undigested food. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial test?

A. Esophageal manometry
B. Upper endoscopy
C. Videofluoroscopic swallow study
D. 24-hour pH monitoring
E. Chest radiograph

**5.** A 49-year-old man has dysphagia to solids and liquids. Upper endoscopy with esophageal biopsies is normal, with no mass at the gastroesophageal junction. Which of the following is the most appropriate next test?

A. Repeat endoscopy in 1 year
B. Videofluoroscopic swallow study
C. CT of the neck
D. High-resolution esophageal manometry
E. Empiric PPI trial

**6.** A 78-year-old man has progressive dysphagia, regurgitation of food he ate many hours earlier, chronic cough at night, and halitosis. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Achalasia
B. Eosinophilic esophagitis
C. Zenker diverticulum
D. Schatzki ring
E. Globus pharyngeus

**7.** A 76-year-old woman has symptomatic Zenker diverticulum confirmed on barium study, with recurrent aspiration. Which of the following treatments addresses the underlying pathophysiology?

A. Cricopharyngeal myotomy (with diverticulum management)
B. Proton pump inhibitor
C. Swallowed topical glucocorticoid
D. Nasogastric tube feeding
E. Botulinum injection of the lower esophageal sphincter

**8.** A 66-year-old man with a long smoking history has 3 months of progressive dysphagia, first for solids and now for soft foods, with a 7-kg weight loss. Over the past 2 weeks he has become hoarse. Which of the following best explains the hoarseness?

A. Primary laryngeal cancer causing the dysphagia
B. Laryngopharyngeal reflux
C. Vocal strain from coughing
D. Myasthenia gravis
E. Recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement by a malignancy

**9.** A 34-year-old man with HIV and a CD4 count of 40/µL has severe pain with swallowing for 1 week. Which of the following is the most likely category of cause?

A. Schatzki ring
B. Achalasia
C. Zenker diverticulum
D. Opportunistic infectious esophagitis (Candida, HSV, or CMV)
E. Cricopharyngeal bar

**10.** A 55-year-old man has intermittent solid-food dysphagia for years. Upper endoscopy shows a Schatzki ring with no other findings, and biopsies are normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate treatment?

A. Surgical myotomy
B. Esophageal dilation
C. Swallowed topical glucocorticoids
D. Botulinum toxin injection
E. Gastrostomy

**11.** A 48-year-old woman with Raynaud phenomenon, sclerodactyly and heartburn has mild difficulty swallowing solid foods. Which motility pattern is most characteristic of her condition?

A. Absent contractility with a severely weak lower esophageal sphincter
B. Absent peristalsis with failed LES relaxation
C. Disordered body contractions with normal LES function
D. Hypercontractile peristalsis with a hypertensive LES
E. Normal manometry

**12.** A 63-year-old man with advanced ALS has severe oropharyngeal dysphagia and weight loss, and a gastrostomy tube is planned. Which of the following should he and his family be told about this intervention?

A. It eliminates the risk of aspiration pneumonia
B. It reverses the swallowing deficit
C. It's contraindicated in neurologic dysphagia
D. It supports nutrition but doesn't protect against aspiration of saliva or refluxed gastric contents
E. It must be combined with cricopharyngeal myotomy

## Answers

**1. C** — Nasal regurgitation and coughing with swallowing are hallmarks of oropharyngeal dysphagia, common after stroke. A videofluoroscopic swallow study by a swallow therapist is the procedure of choice.
- A: Endoscopy is for esophageal dysphagia.
- B: Manometry evaluates esophageal motor disorders.
- D: A barium tablet study evaluates esophageal strictures and motility.
- E: CT isn't a swallowing study.
Source: Ch 0047, Diagnostic Procedures; Oral and Pharyngeal Dysphagia

**2. E** — Episodic solid dysphagia over years with food impaction in a young atopic man suggests eosinophilic esophagitis. Upper endoscopy is the single most useful test, with esophageal biopsies.
- A: Barium can't obtain biopsies.
- B: Manometry is for motor disorders after endoscopy.
- C: Empiric PPI skips a diagnosis that biopsies can make.
- D: This is esophageal, not oropharyngeal, dysphagia.
Source: Ch 0047, History; Diagnostic Procedures

**3. E** — Because eosinophilic esophagitis is a leading cause of dysphagia, esophageal biopsies should be obtained routinely in unexplained dysphagia, even when the mucosa looks normal.
- A: Ending without biopsies could miss EoE.
- B: Empiric dilation without biopsies could miss EoE.
- C: Gastric biopsy alone doesn't sample the esophagus.
- D: Manometry before excluding EoE skips the key step.
Source: Ch 0047, Diagnostic Procedures

**4. B** — Constant dysphagia to solids and liquids suggests a motor disorder such as achalasia. Endoscopy is still the right first test, because neoplastic and inflammatory conditions can mimic achalasia or spasm.
- A: Manometry follows endoscopy.
- C: The swallow study is for oropharyngeal dysphagia.
- D: pH monitoring evaluates reflux.
- E: A radiograph isn't diagnostic.
Source: Ch 0047, History; Diagnostic Procedures

**5. D** — Manometry is done when endoscopy doesn't explain the dysphagia, or to confirm a suspected esophageal motor disorder.
- A: Repeating endoscopy doesn't evaluate motility.
- B: The swallow study evaluates the oropharynx.
- C: Neck CT isn't indicated.
- E: A PPI doesn't address a suspected motor disorder.
Source: Ch 0047, Diagnostic Procedures

**6. C** — Zenker diverticulum, typically in elderly patients, causes dysphagia with regurgitation of food debris, aspiration and halitosis. It's a pulsion diverticulum above the cricopharyngeus (Killian dehiscence).
- A: Achalasia causes solid and liquid dysphagia without the pharyngeal pouch.
- B: EoE affects young atopic patients.
- D: A Schatzki ring causes intermittent solid dysphagia.
- E: Globus doesn't interfere with swallowing.
Source: Ch 0047, Oral and Pharyngeal Dysphagia

**7. A** — Zenker diverticulum results from limited cricopharyngeal opening. Cricopharyngeal myotomy is helpful in specific disorders, including Zenker diverticulum.
- B: Acid suppression doesn't address UES dysfunction.
- C: Topical steroids treat EoE.
- D: Tube feeding doesn't treat the cause.
- E: LES botulinum targets the distal esophagus.
Source: Ch 0047, Treatment

**8. E** — Hoarseness that follows the onset of dysphagia may come from recurrent laryngeal nerve compromise by a malignancy. Progressive dysphagia over weeks to months raises concern for cancer.
- A: When the primary lesion is laryngeal, hoarseness comes before dysphagia.
- B: The progression and weight loss point to malignancy.
- C: Vocal strain doesn't explain the picture.
- D: Myasthenia causes oropharyngeal dysphagia with fatigable weakness.
Source: Ch 0047, History

**9. D** — Odynophagia usually indicates ulceration. In AIDS or other immunocompromised states, consider opportunistic esophagitis (Candida, HSV, CMV) and tumors such as Kaposi sarcoma and lymphoma.
- A: Structural lesions cause dysphagia, not prominent odynophagia.
- B: Achalasia is a motor disorder without odynophagia.
- C: Zenker is structural.
- E: A cricopharyngeal bar is structural.
Source: Ch 0047, History

**10. B** — Most structural causes of esophageal dysphagia are effectively managed with bougie or balloon dilation, which can be done at the time of endoscopy.
- A: Myotomy treats achalasia.
- C: Topical steroids treat EoE, and biopsies are normal.
- D: Botulinum targets motor disorders.
- E: Gastrostomy is for severe oropharyngeal dysphagia.
Source: Ch 0047, Treatment; Diagnostic Procedures

**11. A** — Scleroderma typically shows absent contractility with severe LES weakness, predisposing to peptic stricture. Despite the motor disorder, dysphagia is usually mild and for solids only.
- B: Absent peristalsis with failed LES relaxation defines achalasia.
- C: Disordered contractions with a normal LES define distal esophageal spasm.
- D: This isn't the scleroderma pattern.
- E: Manometry is abnormal in scleroderma.
Source: Ch 0047, Esophageal Dysphagia; History

**12. D** — Nasogastric or gastrostomy feeding can support nutrition in severe neurologic oropharyngeal dysphagia but doesn't protect against aspiration of saliva or refluxed gastric contents.
- A: Aspiration risk persists.
- B: The deficit isn't reversed.
- C: Gastrostomy is an accepted option.
- E: Myotomy is usually unhelpful in neurologic dysphagia.
Source: Ch 0047, Treatment
