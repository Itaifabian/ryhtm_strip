# Ch 40 — Cough: practice questions

**1.** A 63-year-old woman has a dry cough for 4 months. She has taken lisinopril for hypertension for 3 years. She doesn't smoke, and examination and chest radiograph are normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Continue lisinopril, because ACE inhibitor cough begins within weeks of starting the drug
B. Stop lisinopril and switch to an angiotensin receptor blocker
C. Reduce the lisinopril dose
D. Chest CT
E. Start benzonatate and continue lisinopril

**2.** A 34-year-old man has had a nonproductive cough for 10 weeks with no other symptoms. Examination is normal. He takes no medications. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial test?

A. Bronchoscopy
B. 24-hour esophageal pH monitoring
C. Chest CT
D. Sputum cytology
E. Chest radiograph

**3.** A 45-year-old woman has 4 months of chronic cough. Chest radiograph and spirometry are normal, and a methacholine challenge is negative. Induced sputum shows 9% eosinophils, and exhaled nitric oxide is elevated. Which of the following is the most appropriate treatment?

A. Inhaled glucocorticoid
B. Proton pump inhibitor
C. Azithromycin
D. Codeine
E. Gabapentin

**4.** A 29-year-old man has 3 months of cough that starts when he visits a friend with a cat and resolves after he leaves. He has no wheezing or dyspnea. Examination and spirometry are normal. Which of the following tests can exclude asthma with certainty?

A. Chest radiograph
B. Sputum culture
C. Methacholine bronchoprovocation challenge
D. Allergy skin testing
E. Exhaled nitric oxide

**5.** A 58-year-old never-smoker has 6 months of chronic cough. Chest radiograph is normal. He completed adequate trials of therapy for postnasal drainage, reflux and asthma without improvement and takes no ACE inhibitor. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Long-term codeine
B. Repeat chest radiograph in 1 year
C. Empiric macrolide course
D. Chest CT
E. Reassurance without further testing

**6.** A 40-year-old woman has 3 months of chronic cough with a constant sensation of something dripping down the back of her throat, frequent throat clearing, sneezing and rhinorrhea. The posterior pharyngeal wall has a cobblestoned appearance. Chest radiograph is normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial therapy?

A. Inhaled glucocorticoid and albuterol
B. Proton pump inhibitor
C. Benzonatate
D. Chest CT
E. Intranasal glucocorticoid spray

**7.** A 66-year-old man with a chronic dry cough stopped his ACE inhibitor and was switched to an ARB. Six weeks later the cough is unchanged. Which of the following conclusions is most appropriate?

A. The ACE inhibitor remains the most likely cause; wait another 6 months
B. Failure to improve after a month off the drug argues strongly against an ACE inhibitor cause
C. The ARB is now causing the cough
D. ACE inhibitor cough is dose-dependent, so the previous dose was too low to matter
E. The ACE inhibitor should be restarted to confirm the diagnosis

**8.** A 52-year-old woman has had a dry cough with a throat tickle for 3 years, worse with talking and laughing. Chest radiograph, spirometry, methacholine challenge and chest CT are normal. Adequate trials of therapy for postnasal drainage, reflux and asthma failed. She takes no ACE inhibitor. Which of the following is the most appropriate treatment?

A. Gabapentin
B. Long-term azithromycin
C. High-dose inhaled glucocorticoid
D. Long-term codeine
E. Inhaled lidocaine

**9.** A 31-year-old woman had an upper respiratory infection 5 weeks ago. Her other symptoms resolved, but she still has a cough, sometimes in paroxysms. Examination and chest radiograph are normal. Which of the following best classifies her cough and its likely causes?

A. Acute cough due to aspiration
B. Chronic cough due to lung cancer
C. Chronic cough due to cough hypersensitivity syndrome
D. Subacute cough, such as pertussis or postviral tussive syndrome
E. Acute cough due to smoke inhalation

**10.** A patient with severe refractory cough is considered for nebulized lidocaine. Which of the following is the main safety concern?

A. Bronchospasm
B. Dependence
C. Aspiration from oropharyngeal anesthesia
D. Constipation
E. Hypertension

**11.** A 70-year-old man is recovering from an upper abdominal laparotomy. He has a weak, painful cough and retained secretions. Which of the following simple measures most directly improves his cough effectiveness?

A. Cough suppressant
B. Inhaled lidocaine
C. Benzonatate
D. Restrict fluids
E. Splint the abdomen with a tightly held pillow while coughing

## Answers

**1. B** — Any patient with chronic unexplained cough taking an ACE inhibitor should have a trial off the drug, regardless of when the cough began relative to starting it. ARBs don't cause cough.
- A: Onset can occur at any time; timing doesn't exclude it.
- C: ACE inhibitor cough isn't dose-dependent.
- D: Remove the likely cause before advanced imaging.
- E: Suppressing the cough leaves the cause in place.
Source: Ch 0040, Chronic Cough with a Normal Chest Radiograph

**2. E** — Virtually all patients with chronic cough (>8 weeks) need a chest radiograph. Silent serious causes include sarcoidosis, Hodgkin lymphoma, lung cancer and TB.
- A: Bronchoscopy isn't an initial test.
- B: pH monitoring comes after the radiograph, if reflux is suspected.
- C: CT is for failed treatment of common causes or radiograph abnormalities.
- D: Cytology is for mucoid sputum in productive cough.
Source: Ch 0040, Assessment of Chronic Cough

**3. A** — Eosinophilic bronchitis (sputum eosinophils >3% without obstruction or hyperresponsiveness) responds to inhaled glucocorticoids. Raised exhaled NO predicts the response.
- B: There are no reflux features.
- C: Macrolides have inconsistent benefit and aren't indicated.
- D: Narcotic suppressants treat symptoms, not the cause.
- E: Gabapentin is for cough hypersensitivity after causes are excluded.
Source: Ch 0040, Chronic Cough with a Normal Chest Radiograph

**4. C** — A negative bronchoprovocation challenge (e.g., methacholine) excludes asthma with certainty. Variable or reversible obstruction establishes it.
- A: Radiographs don't test airway reactivity.
- B: Culture doesn't test airway reactivity.
- D: Skin tests show sensitization, not asthma.
- E: Exhaled NO detects eosinophilic inflammation but doesn't exclude asthma.
Source: Ch 0040, Chronic Cough with a Normal Chest Radiograph

**5. D** — Patients who don't respond to treatment of the common causes, or in whom they've been excluded, should have chest CT. It can show tumors, early ILD, bronchiectasis and atypical mycobacterial infection missed on radiograph.
- A: Narcotics cause drowsiness, constipation and dependence and skip the evaluation.
- B: A one-year delay could miss a tumor.
- C: Empiric macrolides have inconsistent benefit.
- E: Reassurance is appropriate only after normal CT, lung function and oxygenation.
Source: Ch 0040, Chronic Cough with a Normal Chest Radiograph

**6. E** — Postnasal drip sensation, throat clearing, sneezing, rhinorrhea and cobblestoned mucosa suggest upper airway cough syndrome. Treatment targets the cause and can include nasal glucocorticoid sprays.
- A: Inhaled steroid plus β₂-agonist treats cough-variant asthma.
- B: There are no reflux symptoms.
- C: Benzonatate is a nonspecific suppressant.
- D: CT is for refractory cough.
Source: Ch 0040, Chronic Cough with a Normal Chest Radiograph

**7. B** — Failure to see the cough decrease after 1 month off the ACE inhibitor argues strongly against that cause.
- A: One month is enough to judge.
- C: ARBs don't cause cough.
- D: ACE inhibitor cough isn't dose-dependent.
- E: Rechallenge isn't the recommended approach.
Source: Ch 0040, Chronic Cough with a Normal Chest Radiograph

**8. A** — Cough hypersensitivity syndrome (dry cough with a throat tickle, worse with talking and laughing, more common in women, diagnosed by exclusion) has shown benefit from off-label gabapentin, pregabalin or amitriptyline.
- B: Macrolides have inconsistent benefit in idiopathic cough.
- C: Inhaled steroids have inconsistent benefit in idiopathic cough.
- D: Narcotics are limited for long-term use by drowsiness, constipation and dependence.
- E: Inhaled lidocaine is transient and carries aspiration risk.
Source: Ch 0040, Symptom-Based Treatment of Cough

**9. D** — Cough lasting 3–8 weeks is subacute, commonly a residue of tracheobronchitis such as pertussis or postviral tussive syndrome.
- A: Acute cough is under 3 weeks.
- B: Chronic cough is over 8 weeks.
- C: Chronic cough is over 8 weeks.
- E: Acute cough is under 3 weeks.
Source: Ch 0040, Symptomatic Cough

**10. C** — Inhaled lidocaine gives only transient suppression and causes oropharyngeal anesthesia, raising aspiration risk.
- A: The source doesn't name bronchospasm as the concern.
- B: Dependence is a limitation of narcotics.
- D: Constipation is a limitation of narcotics.
- E: Hypertension isn't a listed concern.
Source: Ch 0040, Symptom-Based Treatment of Cough

**11. E** — Chest wall or abdominal pain is a common cause of impaired cough. Splinting the abdomen with a tightly held pillow reduces postoperative pain while coughing.
- A: Suppressants worsen secretion clearance.
- B: Lidocaine raises aspiration risk.
- C: Benzonatate is also a suppressant.
- D: Fluid restriction doesn't improve cough mechanics.
Source: Ch 0040, Impaired Cough; Table 40-1
