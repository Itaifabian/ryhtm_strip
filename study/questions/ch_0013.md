# Ch 13 — Palliative and End-of-Life Care: practice questions

**1.** A 66-year-old man with metastatic prostate cancer to the spine has been taking short-acting oral morphine 10 mg every 4 hours around the clock (60 mg/day) for 2 days. He needed 4 rescue doses of 10 mg each in the past 24 hours and still rates his pain 7/10 before each scheduled dose. He is alert; respirations 16/min. Which of the following is the most appropriate change in his regimen?

A. Increase the total daily morphine dose to about 100 mg
B. Switch to morphine only as needed
C. Add naloxone to prevent respiratory depression
D. Replace morphine with buprenorphine
E. Add lorazepam

**2.** A 71-year-old woman with pancreatic cancer develops severe epigastric pain radiating to the back that is not controlled with acetaminophen and ibuprofen. She has never taken opioids. Creatinine is normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial opioid strategy?

A. Transdermal fentanyl patch
B. Extended-release morphine twice daily
C. Short-acting oral morphine on a regular schedule with rescue doses
D. Methadone every 8 hours
E. Oral morphine only when pain exceeds 8/10

**3.** A 74-year-old man with lung cancer is starting around-the-clock oxycodone for bone pain. He currently has one soft bowel movement daily. Which of the following is the most appropriate accompanying prescription?

A. No laxative until constipation develops
B. Scheduled senna plus docusate
C. Methylnaltrexone injections daily
D. Loperamide as needed
E. Daily tap-water enemas

**4.** A 58-year-old woman with apical lung cancer invading the brachial plexus describes constant burning, electric-shock-like pain down her left arm. She takes morphine for chest wall pain. Which of the following is the most appropriate addition for her arm pain?

A. Gabapentin
B. Increase morphine only
C. Ibuprofen
D. Lorazepam
E. Buprenorphine

**5.** A 69-year-old man with breast cancer metastatic to a single site in the left femur has severe localized bone pain despite opioids. There is no fracture on imaging. Which of the following is the most appropriate intervention to relieve this pain?

A. Strontium-89
B. External-beam radiation therapy to the lesion
C. Calcitonin alone
D. Hip replacement
E. Increase the opioid dose indefinitely without other measures

**6.** A 63-year-old woman with end-stage lung cancer, at home with hospice, reports worsening breathlessness at rest. She is not anxious. Respirations 24/min; SpO2 94% on room air. There is no wheezing or edema. She takes no opioids. Which of the following is the most appropriate treatment?

A. Supplemental oxygen by nasal cannula
B. Low-dose oral opioid
C. Lorazepam
D. Nebulized morphine
E. CT pulmonary angiography

**7.** A 79-year-old man with metastatic colon cancer is unresponsive and expected to die within hours. His family is distressed by loud gurgling sounds with each breath and asks the nurse to suction him. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?

A. Deep oropharyngeal suctioning every hour
B. Intubation for airway protection
C. Scopolamine and repositioning, with reassurance that he is not choking
D. Supplemental oxygen by face mask
E. Furosemide 40 mg IV

**8.** A 77-year-old woman with metastatic ovarian cancer at home develops day-night reversal, then becomes agitated and pulls at her clothes, with fluctuating attention. Her medications are scheduled morphine and senna. Bladder scan and rectal exam are unremarkable. Which of the following is the most appropriate pharmacotherapy?

A. Haloperidol
B. Lorazepam alone
C. Diphenhydramine
D. Intramuscular chlorpromazine every 4 hours
E. Physical restraints

**9.** An 84-year-old man with end-stage dementia has stopped eating and drinking over the past week and is expected to die within days. His daughter asks for IV fluids and a feeding tube "so he won't starve or die of thirst." Which of the following is the most appropriate response?

A. Place a nasogastric tube for feeding
B. Start IV fluids at maintenance rate
C. Explain that he is not eating because he is dying, that this does not cause suffering, and that fluids may worsen breathing and swelling
D. Start total parenteral nutrition
E. Request an ethics consultation before discussing further

**10.** A 70-year-old woman with metastatic pancreatic cancer, estimated prognosis of 2–4 weeks, has persistent low mood, hopelessness, and anhedonia that is impairing her final time with family. TSH and calcium are normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate pharmacotherapy?

A. Fluoxetine 20 mg daily
B. Methylphenidate
C. Amitriptyline
D. Phenelzine
E. No treatment, because sadness is normal at the end of life

**11.** A 72-year-old man with metastatic lung cancer has declining function and a prognosis of about 3 months. He wants hospice at home but also wants to continue palliative-intent chemotherapy for the cancer. He has Medicare. Which of the following statements is most accurate?

A. Hospice requires that he be expected to die within 2 weeks
B. Hospice will cover the chemotherapy because it is palliative
C. Under the Medicare hospice benefit, therapy directed at the terminal cancer is not covered, although he may revoke hospice later
D. Once enrolled, he can never return to standard Medicare
E. One physician's certification is sufficient for enrollment

**12.** A 64-year-old man with ALS, in a state where physician-assisted suicide is legal, asks his physician to prescribe medication to end his life. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial response?

A. "That is illegal and unethical; I cannot discuss it."
B. "I'll write the prescription today."
C. "What makes you want to consider this option?"
D. Refer him immediately to another physician
E. Increase his opioid dose to hasten death

**13.** A 55-year-old woman tells her oncologist she understands her scan "is probably not good." The oncologist explains that the cancer has spread to the liver. She begins crying silently. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Immediately describe the chemotherapy options
B. Leave the room so she can be alone
C. Acknowledge her emotion and allow time before continuing
D. Ask a family member to take over the conversation
E. Reassure her that everything will be fine

---

## Answers

**1. A** — If pain is uncontrolled after 24 h despite rescue doses, raise the daily opioid dose by the total rescue used (4 × 10 = 40 mg), or by 50–100%. 60 + 40 = 100 mg/day, continued around the clock. Opioids have no ceiling dose.
- B: PRN-only dosing lets pain recur; continuous pain needs scheduled doses.
- C: Respiratory depression is extremely unlikely, and antagonists aren't justified.
- D: Partial agonists have a ceiling and aren't for chronic end-of-life pain.
- E: A benzodiazepine isn't an analgesic.
Source: Ch 0013, Pain — Interventions

**2. C** — Begin short-acting opioids to determine the 24–48 h requirement, given around the clock with rescue doses, then convert to extended-release once pain is controlled.
- A, B: Starting with long-acting preparations is inappropriate.
- D: Methadone is reserved for cases where first-line opioids fail.
- E: Pain should be prevented, not treated only once it is severe.
Source: Ch 0013, Pain — Interventions; Fig 13-2

**3. B** — Nearly all patients on opioids develop constipation, so prevention is preferred. A laxative plus stool softener such as senna and docusate is recommended.
- A: Waiting causes debilitating constipation and nonadherence.
- C: Methylnaltrexone is for refractory cases.
- D: Loperamide worsens constipation.
- E: Enemas aren't first-line prophylaxis.
Source: Ch 0013, Constipation — Intervention; Table 13-5

**4. A** — Burning, electrical pain from brachial plexus invasion is neuropathic. Gabapentin is first-line, started at 100–300 mg bid–tid and titrated.
- B: Opioids alone handle neuropathic pain poorly.
- C: NSAIDs target inflammatory and nociceptive pain.
- D: Not an analgesic.
- E: Not for end-of-life chronic pain.
Source: Ch 0013, Pain — adjuvant analgesics; Fig 13-2

**5. B** — Radiation therapy treats bone pain from a single metastatic lesion. Radiopharmaceuticals are for multiple metastases.
- A: Strontium-89 is for multiple lesions.
- C: Calcitonin has a multiday onset and is adjunctive.
- D: No fracture, so no indication for surgery.
- E: Ignores an effective targeted therapy.
Source: Ch 0013, Pain — Interventions (radiation therapy)

**6. B** — Low-dose opioids relieve the sensation of dyspnea and are first-line. Oxygen hasn't been shown to beat air in end-stage cancer, and benzodiazepines are only for dyspnea with anxiety.
- A: Not better than air.
- C: No anxiety present.
- D: Nebulized opioids aren't supported.
- E: Invasive workup is inappropriate given her goals and prognosis.
Source: Ch 0013, Dyspnea — Interventions; Table 13-6

**7. C** — The "death rattle" comes from oropharyngeal secretions; the patient isn't choking. Reduce secretions with scopolamine, reposition, and don't suction (uncomfortable and usually ineffective).
- A: Suctioning is discouraged.
- B: Intubation contradicts comfort goals.
- D: Oxygen doesn't help and may prolong dying.
- E: This isn't pulmonary edema.
Source: Ch 0013, Table 13-9

**8. A** — Haloperidol is first-line for terminal delirium, given PO, SC or IV. Reversible causes were checked (retention, constipation).
- B: Benzodiazepines alone can worsen delirium and are added only for alcohol or sedative withdrawal.
- C: Anticholinergics cause delirium.
- D: IM injections should be avoided except as the only route.
- E: Restraints are a last resort for violence.
Source: Ch 0013, Delirium — Interventions; Table 13-7

**9. C** — Patients stop eating because they are dying. Dehydration at the end of life doesn't cause suffering. Forced feeding doesn't prolong life, and IV hydration can worsen dyspnea and edema.
- A, B, D: Each offers no benefit and adds burden.
- E: The physician should first provide this reassurance directly.
Source: Ch 0013, Care during the Last Hours; Table 13-9

**10. B** — For depressed patients with a short prognosis, psychostimulants are preferred because they work within days, while SSRIs take up to 4 weeks.
- A: Too slow, and the dose should be halved in seriously ill patients.
- C: TCAs aren't recommended except as pain adjuvants.
- D: MAOIs aren't recommended.
- E: Persistent hopelessness and anhedonia suggest major depression, which should be treated.
Source: Ch 0013, Depression — Interventions

**11. C** — Medicare hospice requires two physicians to certify a prognosis of 6 months or less. It doesn't cover curative-intent therapy for the terminal illness, but patients can revoke and re-enroll. [verify: hospice coverage rules may change]
- A: The threshold is 6 months or less.
- B: Therapy directed at the terminal disease isn't covered.
- D: The benefit can be revoked.
- E: Two physicians must certify.
Source: Ch 0013, Palliative Care Services: How and Where

**12. C** — Clarify the request with empathic, open-ended questions to find its cause (depression, hopelessness, symptoms, burden). Avoid voicing moral judgment, reassure continued care, and treat contributing factors.
- A: Moral condemnation is counterproductive.
- B: Prescribing without exploring skips assessment of treatable distress.
- D: An immediate referral without exploration feels like abandonment.
- E: Using opioids to hasten death is euthanasia.
Source: Ch 0013, Euthanasia and Physician-Assisted Suicide — Practices

**13. C** — In P-SPIKES, the E step: acknowledge strong emotions as normal, empathize, and give time to respond before moving to summary and planning.
- A: Rushing to options ignores the emotional response.
- B: Leaving signals abandonment.
- D: Unnecessary delegation.
- E: False reassurance undermines trust.
Source: Ch 0013, Table 13-2
