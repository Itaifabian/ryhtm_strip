# Ch 75 — Prevention and Early Detection of Cancer: practice questions

**1.** A 22-year-old sexually active woman with no symptoms asks about cervical cancer screening. She has never been screened. Which of the following is recommended?

A. HPV testing alone every 5 years
B. Co-testing with cytology and HPV every 5 years
C. Cervical cytology every 3 years
D. Annual cytology
E. No screening until age 30

**2.** A 67-year-old woman has had regular cervical screening with consistently normal results for decades and no history of high-grade lesions. Which of the following is the most appropriate recommendation?

A. Stop cervical cancer screening
B. Continue co-testing every 5 years
C. Annual cytology
D. HPV testing every 3 years
E. Colposcopy

**3.** A 62-year-old woman smoked 1.5 packs daily for 20 years and quit 8 years ago. She is healthy and would be a surgical candidate. Which lung cancer screening approach is recommended?

A. Annual chest radiograph
B. Sputum cytology
C. No screening because she has quit
D. One-time low-dose CT
E. Annual low-dose CT

**4.** A 70-year-old man has a 40 pack-year history but quit smoking 16 years ago. He asks whether he should have lung cancer screening. Which of the following is correct?

A. Annual low-dose CT is recommended
B. Screening isn't recommended because he quit more than 15 years ago
C. Annual chest radiograph is recommended
D. CT every 3 years is recommended
E. PET-CT is recommended

**5.** A 46-year-old man at average risk for colorectal cancer has never been screened. Which of the following is the most appropriate recommendation?

A. Wait until age 50
B. Annual digital rectal exam with in-office stool guaiac
C. Serum SEPT9 test as first-line screening
D. Begin colorectal cancer screening now (e.g., annual FIT or colonoscopy every 10 years)
E. Annual CT colonography

**6.** A healthy 72-year-old man asks for a PSA test as part of his annual examination. He has no urinary symptoms or family history. Which of the following is the most appropriate response?

A. Order PSA and digital rectal exam
B. Order multiparametric MRI
C. Recommend against PSA-based screening at this age
D. Order PSA every 2 years
E. Order PSA once, and stop if normal

**7.** A new screening test detects a cancer 3 years earlier than symptoms would, but the date of death is unchanged. Survival measured from diagnosis appears 3 years longer in screened patients. Which bias explains this?

A. Lead-time bias
B. Length-biased sampling
C. Selection bias
D. Recall bias
E. Verification bias

**8.** A cancer with a prevalence of 0.5% is screened with a test that has 95% sensitivity and 90% specificity. Which statement is most accurate?

A. Most positive results will be true positives
B. Sensitivity drives the positive predictive value at low prevalence
C. Predictive values don't depend on prevalence
D. The negative predictive value will be low
E. Most positive results will be false positives because poor specificity strongly lowers PPV at low prevalence

**9.** A 60-year-old postmenopausal woman with an intact uterus has a 5-year Gail model breast cancer risk of 2.4%. She wants pharmacologic risk reduction but is concerned about endometrial cancer. Which agent is most appropriate?

A. Tamoxifen
B. Raloxifene
C. Finasteride
D. Combined estrogen-progestin therapy
E. β-carotene

**10.** A 58-year-old man with a 35 pack-year smoking history asks whether β-carotene supplements could lower his lung cancer risk. What should you tell him?

A. β-carotene lowers lung cancer risk by about 30%
B. β-carotene is neutral and safe to take
C. α-tocopherol is preferred because it lowers lung cancer mortality
D. β-carotene increased lung cancer incidence and mortality in smokers in randomized trials
E. β-carotene should be combined with isotretinoin

**11.** A 52-year-old woman with no family history of ovarian or breast cancer asks for annual CA-125 and transvaginal ultrasound screening. Which of the following is the most appropriate response?

A. Recommend against routine ovarian cancer screening
B. Order both tests annually
C. Order CA-125 every 6 months
D. Order transvaginal ultrasound alone annually
E. Recommend annual adnexal palpation instead

**12.** An 11-year-old girl's parents ask about HPV vaccination. Which schedule is recommended in the United States?

A. Delay until age 15
B. Single dose at age 26
C. Three doses now
D. No vaccination until sexual activity begins
E. Two doses

**13.** A 57-year-old man undergoes screening flexible sigmoidoscopy, which finds a 7-mm tubular adenoma in the sigmoid colon. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Colonoscopy to evaluate the entire colon
B. Repeat sigmoidoscopy in 5 years
C. Annual FIT only
D. CT colonography in 10 years
E. No further evaluation

**14.** Which study design and endpoint provide the strongest evidence that a cancer screening test is beneficial?

A. Observational study showing longer 5-year survival in screened patients
B. Randomized controlled trial showing lower cause-specific mortality in the screened group
C. Case series showing a shift to earlier-stage diagnosis
D. Cohort study showing more cancers detected
E. Randomized trial showing higher detection of early-stage disease

## Answers

**1. C** — Women 21–29 should be screened with cytology alone every 3 years. HPV testing isn't used under 30.
- A: No HPV testing under 30.
- B: No HPV testing under 30.
- D: More frequent screening adds harm without benefit.
- E: Screening starts at 21.
Source: Ch 0075, Cervical Cancer; Table 75-3

**2. A** — Women over 65 with adequate, normal prior screening shouldn't continue screening (USPSTF D; ACS no screening).
- B: Continued screening isn't recommended.
- C: Continued screening isn't recommended.
- D: Continued screening isn't recommended.
- E: Colposcopy isn't indicated.
Source: Ch 0075, Table 75-3

**3. E** — USPSTF recommends annual low-dose CT for adults 50–80 with ≥20 pack-years (she has 30) who smoke or quit within the past 15 years.
- A: Chest radiography didn't reduce mortality in PLCO.
- B: Sputum cytology isn't recommended.
- C: Quitting within 15 years still qualifies.
- D: Screening is annual.
Source: Ch 0075, Lung Cancer; Table 75-3

**4. B** — Screening stops once a person has gone 15 years without smoking.
- A: He's past the 15-year cutoff.
- C: CXR isn't effective.
- D: That interval isn't recommended.
- E: PET-CT isn't a screening test.
Source: Ch 0075, Table 75-3

**5. D** — USPSTF gives a B recommendation for screening adults 45–49 and an A for 50–75, using options such as annual FIT, colonoscopy every 10 years, sigmoidoscopy every 5 years, or CT colonography every 5 years.
- A: Screening now starts at 45.
- B: Office DRE stool testing isn't an endorsed strategy.
- C: SEPT9 has lower sensitivity and specificity than FIT and isn't first-line.
- E: CT colonography is every 5 years.
Source: Ch 0075, Colorectal Cancer; Table 75-3

**6. C** — USPSTF recommends against PSA-based screening in men 70 and older (D). For 55–69 it's an individual decision.
- A: Screening isn't recommended at this age.
- B: Screening isn't recommended at this age.
- D: Screening isn't recommended at this age.
- E: Screening isn't recommended at this age.
Source: Ch 0075, Prostate Cancer; Table 75-3

**7. A** — Lead-time bias: earlier diagnosis increases apparent survival from diagnosis even if life isn't prolonged.
- B: Length bias reflects detecting slower-growing tumors.
- C: Selection bias reflects healthier volunteers.
- D: Recall bias isn't relevant here.
- E: Verification bias isn't relevant here.
Source: Ch 0075, Potential Biases of Screening Tests

**8. E** — Predictive values depend strongly on prevalence. For low-prevalence diseases such as cancer, poor specificity dramatically lowers PPV, so only a small fraction of positives are true. Specificity is at least as important as sensitivity.
- A: Most positives will be false.
- B: Specificity, not sensitivity, drives PPV here.
- C: Predictive values depend on prevalence.
- D: NPV will be high at low prevalence.
Source: Ch 0075, The Accuracy of Screening; Table 75-2

**9. B** — Raloxifene (postmenopausal women only) is comparable to tamoxifen for prevention but without the endometrial cancer risk, and with fewer thromboembolic events. Both are approved for a 5-year Gail risk ≥1.66%.
- A: Tamoxifen increases endometrial cancer risk.
- C: Finasteride is a prostate drug.
- D: Estrogen-progestin raises breast cancer risk.
- E: β-carotene raises lung cancer risk.
Source: Ch 0075, Chemoprevention of Breast Cancer

**10. D** — In the ATBC and CARET trials, β-carotene increased lung cancer incidence and mortality in smokers, contradicting observational studies.
- A: It increased risk.
- B: It isn't safe in smokers.
- C: α-Tocopherol didn't change lung cancer mortality and increased hemorrhagic stroke.
- E: Isotretinoin isn't effective at tolerable doses.
Source: Ch 0075, Chemoprevention of Cancers of the Upper Aerodigestive Tract

**11. A** — Annual TVUS and CA-125 don't reduce ovarian cancer deaths in average-risk women. False positives lead to surgery (~20 surgeries per screen-detected cancer). USPSTF gives a D, and the FDA advises against screening tests.
- B: These tests aren't recommended.
- C: These tests aren't recommended.
- D: These tests aren't recommended.
- E: Adnexal palpation detected no additional cancers.
Source: Ch 0075, Ovarian Cancer; Table 75-3

**12. E** — A two-dose schedule is recommended for those aged 9–14. Three doses are recommended if the series starts at 15–26. The vaccine works best before infection.
- A: Waiting until 15 would require three doses.
- B: Waiting until 26 misses the effective window.
- C: Three doses are for ages 15–26.
- D: Vaccination should precede exposure.
Source: Ch 0075, Vaccines and Cancer Prevention

**13. A** — Adenomatous polyps found on sigmoidoscopy should lead to evaluation of the entire colon with colonoscopy.
- B: Synchronous proximal lesions must be excluded first.
- C: Synchronous proximal lesions must be excluded first.
- D: Synchronous proximal lesions must be excluded first.
- E: Further evaluation is needed.
Source: Ch 0075, Optical Testing

**14. B** — An RCT with cause-specific mortality as the endpoint gives the strongest support. Survival, stage shift and detection rates are subject to lead-time, length and overdiagnosis biases.
- A: Survival is distorted by these biases.
- C: Stage shift is distorted by these biases.
- D: Detection rates are distorted by these biases.
- E: Detection rates are distorted by these biases.
Source: Ch 0075, Assessment of Screening Tests
