# Ch 39 — Dyspnea: practice questions

**1.** A 61-year-old woman has 4 months of gradually worsening shortness of breath on exertion. History and examination don't reveal a clear cause. Walking oximetry shows no desaturation, and peak flow is normal for her. Which of the following is the most appropriate next set of tests?

A. Cardiopulmonary exercise testing
B. Right heart catheterization
C. Chest CT angiography and DLCO
D. Chest radiograph, spirometry, ECG, CBC, and basic metabolic panel
E. Bronchoscopy

**2.** A 27-year-old woman has intermittent episodes of chest tightness and dyspnea, especially with exercise in cold air. Examination, chest radiograph and spirometry (including after bronchodilator) are normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Start long-term inhaled corticosteroid and LABA without testing
B. Methacholine bronchoprovocation challenge
C. Chest CT
D. Echocardiogram
E. Cardiopulmonary exercise testing

**3.** A 70-year-old man with severe COPD on optimal inhaler therapy has a resting oxygen saturation of 87% on room air on two clinic visits, at baseline without an exacerbation. Which of the following interventions has been shown to improve mortality in this patient?

A. Low-dose extended-release morphine
B. Daily benzodiazepine
C. Oral glucocorticoids
D. Pulmonary rehabilitation alone
E. Long-term supplemental oxygen

**4.** A 66-year-old man with known COPD and ischemic cardiomyopathy (LVEF 40%) has persistent exertional dyspnea despite optimal treatment of both conditions. It's unclear which system limits his exercise. Which of the following is the most appropriate test?

A. Cardiopulmonary exercise testing
B. Repeat spirometry
C. BNP level
D. Chest radiograph
E. Bronchoprovocation testing

**5.** A 54-year-old man with cirrhosis reports shortness of breath that worsens when he sits or stands up and improves when he lies flat. Which of the following is the most likely cause?

A. Congestive heart failure
B. Asthma triggered by reflux
C. Hepatopulmonary syndrome
D. Bilateral diaphragmatic paralysis
E. Obesity hypoventilation

**6.** A 59-year-old man has progressive dyspnea, mild dysphagia, and fasciculations in both arms. Spirometry suggests a restrictive defect, and the chest radiograph shows low lung volumes with clear parenchyma. Which of the following tests should be obtained to evaluate the cause of his restriction?

A. Bronchoprovocation challenge
B. Bronchoscopy
C. Echocardiogram
D. Maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures
E. Esophageal pH monitoring

**7.** A 68-year-old woman with chronic dyspnea and obesity has a basic metabolic panel showing a serum bicarbonate of 34 mEq/L. She takes no diuretics. Which of the following is the most appropriate next test?

A. Arterial blood gas
B. Serum BNP
C. Hemoglobin A1c
D. Urine electrolytes
E. Thyroid-stimulating hormone

**8.** A 72-year-old woman with moderate COPD has persistent exertional breathlessness despite optimal bronchodilator therapy. Her resting and exertional oxygen saturations are above 92%. Which of the following is the most appropriate additional intervention to improve dyspnea and exercise capacity?

A. Supplemental oxygen at rest
B. Scheduled benzodiazepine
C. Low-dose extended-release morphine
D. Long-term oral glucocorticoids
E. Pulmonary rehabilitation

**9.** A 64-year-old man undergoes cardiopulmonary exercise testing for unexplained dyspnea. At peak exercise his heart rate reaches 92% of predicted maximum, the anaerobic threshold occurs early, blood pressure falls, and the O₂ pulse decreases. Ventilation stays well below predicted maximum without desaturation. These findings most likely indicate which of the following?

A. A pulmonary cause of exercise limitation
B. A cardiovascular cause of exercise limitation
C. Poor effort
D. Exercise-induced bronchospasm
E. Neuromuscular weakness

**10.** A 57-year-old woman with dyspnea is examined supine. During inspiration, her abdomen moves inward. Which of the following is the most likely explanation?

A. Pulmonary edema
B. Emphysema
C. Diaphragmatic weakness
D. Pleural effusion
E. Pneumothorax

**11.** A 49-year-old woman has 6 months of exertional dyspnea and exertional desaturation. Lungs are clear on auscultation, the pulmonic component of S2 is accentuated, and JVP is elevated. Spirometry and lung volumes are normal, but DLCO is reduced. Chest radiograph shows enlarged central pulmonary arteries. Which of the following is the most appropriate next test?

A. Echocardiogram
B. Methacholine challenge
C. Maximal inspiratory pressure
D. Bronchoscopy
E. Esophageal pH monitoring

**12.** A 75-year-old man with COPD says he has to stop to rest after walking about 100 meters on level ground but can dress and leave the house. What is his Modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnea grade?

A. 0
B. 1
C. 2
D. 3
E. 4

## Answers

**1. D** — After history, examination, walking oximetry and peak flow, the stepwise algorithm moves to phase 1: chest radiograph, spirometry, ECG, CBC and basic metabolic panel.
- A: CPET is phase 3, after nondiagnostic earlier testing.
- B: Invasive testing isn't a first step.
- C: CT, CTA and DLCO are phase 2.
- E: Bronchoscopy isn't part of the initial dyspnea workup.
Source: Ch 0039, Fig 39-2; Laboratory Studies

**2. B** — Bronchoprovocation testing or home peak-flow monitoring helps patients with intermittent asthma-like symptoms and a normal exam and spirometry. Up to one-third of clinically diagnosed asthma isn't confirmed on formal testing.
- A: Committing to long-term therapy without objective confirmation risks misdiagnosis.
- C: Chest CT is for parenchymal disease or PE.
- D: Echo is for suspected systolic dysfunction, pulmonary hypertension or valve disease.
- E: CPET is reserved for when the limiting system is unclear after other testing.
Source: Ch 0039, Laboratory Studies

**3. E** — Supplemental oxygen is indicated for resting saturation ≤88%. In hypoxemic COPD it improves mortality.
- A: A recent trial didn't support low-dose ER morphine for dyspnea in COPD.
- B: Anxiolytics haven't shown consistent benefit.
- C: Systemic steroids aren't a dyspnea or mortality therapy here.
- D: Rehabilitation improves dyspnea, exercise capacity and hospitalizations; the mortality benefit cited is for oxygen.
Source: Ch 0039, Treatment: Dyspnea

**4. A** — When a patient has both pulmonary and cardiac disease and it's unclear which drives dyspnea, CPET determines which system limits exercise.
- B: Spirometry doesn't separate cardiac from pulmonary limitation.
- C: BNP is mainly useful in acute dyspnea and rises with RV strain as well.
- D: A radiograph doesn't measure exercise limitation.
- E: Bronchoprovocation tests airway hyperreactivity.
Source: Ch 0039, Distinguishing Cardiovascular from Respiratory System Dyspnea

**5. C** — Platypnea, dyspnea upright relieved supine, suggests hepatopulmonary syndrome or left atrial myxoma.
- A: Heart failure causes orthopnea, worse lying flat.
- B: Reflux-triggered asthma also causes orthopnea.
- D: Diaphragmatic weakness worsens dyspnea supine.
- E: Obesity-related diaphragm restriction worsens supine.
Source: Ch 0039, History

**6. D** — In suspected chest wall or neuromuscular disease (here possible ALS), maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures are reduced and help explain a restrictive defect.
- A: Bronchoprovocation evaluates asthma.
- B: Bronchoscopy has no role in evaluating neuromuscular weakness.
- C: Echo evaluates cardiac causes.
- E: pH monitoring evaluates GERD.
Source: Ch 0039, Table 39-2

**7. A** — An elevated bicarbonate may indicate CO₂ retention from chronic respiratory failure. An ABG gives the additional information.
- B: BNP doesn't explain a raised bicarbonate.
- C: HbA1c doesn't explain a raised bicarbonate.
- D: Without diuretic use, the respiratory question comes first.
- E: Thyroid testing doesn't assess CO₂ retention.
Source: Ch 0039, Laboratory Studies

**8. E** — Pulmonary rehabilitation (including home and community exercise such as yoga and tai chi) improves dyspnea, exercise capacity and hospitalization rates in COPD.
- A: Oxygen is indicated for saturation ≤88%, and hers is normal.
- B: Anxiolytics lack consistent benefit.
- C: A trial didn't support low-dose ER morphine in COPD.
- D: Long-term steroids aren't a dyspnea therapy.
Source: Ch 0039, Treatment: Dyspnea

**9. B** — Heart rate >85% of predicted, an early anaerobic threshold, falling blood pressure and a falling O₂ pulse (a stroke-volume surrogate) indicate a cardiovascular limitation.
- A: Pulmonary limitation shows reaching predicted maximal ventilation, rising dead space or hypoxemia, or bronchospasm.
- C: Reaching 92% of predicted heart rate argues against poor effort.
- D: No bronchospasm is described.
- E: The cardiac pattern explains the findings.
Source: Ch 0039, Distinguishing Cardiovascular from Respiratory System Dyspnea

**10. C** — Paradoxical inward abdominal motion during inspiration when supine is a sign of diaphragmatic weakness.
- A: Abdominal rounding during exhalation suggests pulmonary edema.
- B: Emphysema is suggested by hyperresonance.
- D: Effusion shows dullness to percussion.
- E: Pneumothorax shows hyperresonance.
Source: Ch 0039, Physical Examination

**11. A** — Reduced DLCO with normal spirometry, a loud P₂, raised JVP and enlarged central pulmonary arteries suggest pulmonary hypertension. Echocardiography is indicated, with right heart catheterization to confirm.
- B: Methacholine evaluates asthma.
- C: MIP evaluates neuromuscular weakness.
- D: Bronchoscopy doesn't evaluate pulmonary vascular disease.
- E: pH monitoring evaluates GERD.
Source: Ch 0039, Table 39-2; Chest Imaging

**12. D** — mMRC grade 3 is stopping to rest after walking 100 m or a few minutes on level ground.
- A: Grade 0 is breathless only with strenuous exercise.
- B: Grade 1 is breathless hurrying on level ground or up a slight hill.
- C: Grade 2 is walking slower than peers or stopping at one's own pace on level ground.
- E: Grade 4 is too breathless to leave the house or breathless while dressing.
Source: Ch 0039, Table 39-1
