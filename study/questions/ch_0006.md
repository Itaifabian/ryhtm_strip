# Ch 6 — Screening and Prevention: practice questions

**1.** A 68-year-old man comes for a routine visit. He smoked 1.5 packs/day from age 20 to 46 and has not smoked since. He has hypertension controlled with amlodipine. BP 132/80 mm Hg, pulse 72/min. Physical exam, including abdominal exam, is unremarkable. He has never had any imaging of his abdomen. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Annual low-dose chest CT
B. CT angiography of the abdomen
C. One-time abdominal ultrasound
D. Pulmonary function testing every year
E. No additional screening is indicated

**2.** A 57-year-old woman comes for a health maintenance visit. She has smoked 1 pack/day since age 22 and continues to smoke. She has no cough, hemoptysis, or weight loss. Vital signs are normal. Lung exam is clear. Which of the following is the most appropriate screening test for this patient?

A. Annual chest x-ray
B. Annual low-dose chest CT
C. Sputum cytology every year
D. Spirometry only
E. No lung cancer screening until age 65

**3.** A 35-year-old man comes for a routine visit. His older brother was diagnosed with colon adenocarcinoma at age 42; no other relatives have had cancer. He has no GI symptoms. Exam and CBC are normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate recommendation regarding colorectal cancer screening?

A. Begin screening now
B. Begin screening at age 40
C. Begin screening at age 45
D. Begin screening at age 50
E. Screening is not indicated because his brother is a second-degree relative

**4.** A 23-year-old woman comes for a routine visit. She is sexually active with one male partner and uses oral contraceptives inconsistently with condoms. Her last Pap smear 2 years ago was normal. She has no symptoms. Pelvic exam is normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate test at this visit?

A. Pap smear
B. Pap smear with HPV co-testing
C. HSV type 2 serology
D. Nucleic acid amplification test for chlamydia and gonorrhea
E. No testing is needed today

**5.** A 36-year-old woman has a Pap smear with HPV co-testing at a routine visit. Cytology is normal and HPV testing is negative. She has no history of abnormal Pap results. When should her next cervical cancer screening occur?

A. 6 months
B. 1 year
C. 3 years
D. 5 years
E. No further screening is needed

**6.** A 66-year-old woman comes for a routine visit. She has no history of fractures, takes no medications, and has never had bone density testing. She walks daily. BMI is 23 kg/m². Exam is normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Begin calcium and vitamin D and recheck in 5 years
B. Dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DEXA)
C. Plain radiographs of the lumbar spine
D. Serum alkaline phosphatase
E. No screening because she has no risk factors

**7.** A 34-year-old woman is found to carry a pathogenic BRCA1 mutation after her mother developed ovarian cancer. She has no breast symptoms and a normal breast exam. In addition to genetic counseling, which of the following is the most appropriate breast cancer screening strategy for this patient?

A. Clinical breast exam only until age 50
B. Mammography every 2 years starting at age 50
C. Breast magnetic resonance imaging
D. Breast ultrasound only if a lump is palpated
E. Serum CA-125 every 6 months

**8.** A 55-year-old man asks whether he should have a PSA test. He has no urinary symptoms and no family history of prostate cancer. Digital rectal exam is normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Order PSA because all men older than 50 should be screened
B. Refuse to order PSA because screening has no benefit
C. Discuss the potential benefits and harms of screening and decide together
D. Refer to urology for prostate biopsy
E. Order transrectal ultrasound

**9.** A 28-year-old woman with no medical problems is planning to conceive within the next year. She eats a varied diet. Which of the following preventive interventions is most appropriate now?

A. Folic acid supplementation
B. Low-dose aspirin
C. Vitamin D supplementation
D. Iron supplementation
E. Tamoxifen

**10.** An 83-year-old woman with metastatic pancreatic cancer receiving palliative chemotherapy asks whether she is due for her colonoscopy; her last one was 11 years ago and was normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate response?

A. Schedule a colonoscopy within the next month
B. Order yearly fecal occult blood testing instead
C. Order a CT colonography
D. Explain that further colon cancer screening is unlikely to benefit her and focus on her current goals of care
E. Order flexible sigmoidoscopy

**11.** A new cancer screening test is introduced. Five-year survival after diagnosis rises from 40% to 65% in screened patients, but the age at death from that cancer is unchanged in the population. The apparent improvement in survival is best explained by which of the following?

A. Lead-time bias
B. Recall bias
C. Selection of high-risk patients
D. Improved treatment of advanced disease
E. Reduced false-positive results

**12.** A screening program for a slowly growing tumor detects many small, well-differentiated tumors; tumors that grow rapidly usually appear between scheduled screening rounds. Screened patients appear to have better outcomes than unscreened patients even though mortality is unchanged. Which of the following best explains this finding?

A. Lead-time bias
B. Length-time bias
C. Observer bias
D. Confounding by smoking status
E. Regression to the mean

**13.** A 63-year-old man with a 40-pack-year history quit smoking 18 years ago. He has no symptoms and a normal exam. He asks about lung cancer screening. Which of the following is the most appropriate response?

A. Annual low-dose chest CT
B. Low-dose chest CT every 2 years
C. Chest x-ray every year
D. Lung cancer screening is not recommended for him
E. Sputum cytology

**14.** A 19-year-old man comes for a college sports physical. He drinks alcohol at parties on weekends, rides a motorcycle, and does not smoke. BP 118/72 mm Hg; BMI 23 kg/m². Exam is normal. Which of the following preventive interventions addresses the leading cause of death in his age group?

A. Fasting lipid panel
B. Counseling on helmet and seat belt use and not drinking before driving
C. Resting ECG
D. Hepatitis C antibody testing
E. Testicular ultrasound

**15.** A 52-year-old woman asks what she is most likely to die of, given her age. She has no chronic illnesses. According to U.S. age-specific mortality data, which of the following is the leading cause of death in her age group (45–64 years)?

A. Heart disease
B. Accidents
C. Malignancy
D. Cerebrovascular disease
E. Diabetes mellitus

**16.** A 79-year-old woman who lives alone comes for an annual visit. She tripped on a rug last month but was not injured. She wears glasses that are 6 years old. Her vaccinations are up to date and her DEXA was done last year. Which of the following is the most appropriate additional preventive measure at this visit?

A. Screening colonoscopy
B. Screening for vision and hearing problems and home safety, with a fall-prevention exercise program
C. Annual chest radiograph
D. Abdominal aortic aneurysm ultrasound
E. Serum PSA

**17.** A primary care group wants to prioritize the preventive service with the largest estimated average gain in life expectancy for its population. Which of the following has the largest estimated effect?

A. Mammography for women aged 50–70
B. Regular exercise (30 min, 3 times/week) for a 40-year-old man
C. Getting a 35-year-old smoker to quit
D. Pap smears for women aged 18–65
E. Mammography for women aged 40–50

**18.** A health department is choosing a disease for a new population screening program. Which of the following conditions least satisfies the WHO principles of screening?

A. Colorectal cancer
B. Hypertension
C. Alzheimer's disease
D. Cervical cancer
E. Hyperlipidemia

---

## Answers

**1. C** — Men aged 65–75 who have ever smoked should have a one-time ultrasound screen for abdominal aortic aneurysm. He quit more than 15 years ago, so he no longer qualifies for lung cancer screening.
- A: He quit 22 years ago (more than 15).
- B: CT angiography is not the screening test; it adds contrast and radiation.
- D: Routine yearly PFTs are not a screening recommendation here.
- E: He meets AAA screening criteria.
Source: Ch 0006, Table 6-4 / Table 6-7

**2. B** — Annual low-dose CT is recommended for adults 50–80 with at least 20 pack-years who smoke now or quit within the past 15 years. She has about 35 pack-years and still smokes. [verify: guideline may have changed since this edition]
- A: Chest x-ray detects tumors too late to change outcomes.
- C: Sputum cytology is not a recommended screening test.
- D: Spirometry screens for COPD, not lung cancer.
- E: Screening starts at 50, not 65.
Source: Ch 0006, Basic Principles of Screening; Table 6-4

**3. A** — With a significant family history of colon cancer, screening begins 10 years before the youngest affected relative's age at diagnosis (42 − 10 = 32). He is already 35, so he should start now.
- B: That would be 8 years too late.
- C: Age 45 is the average-risk start.
- D: Age 50 is an older average-risk threshold.
- E: A brother is a first-degree relative.
Source: Ch 0006, Weighing the Benefits and Harms

**4. D** — Sexually active women younger than 25 should be screened for chlamydia and gonorrhea with a NAAT on urine or a cervical swab.
- A: After a normal Pap at 21, the next one is due in 3 years.
- B: HPV co-testing is for women 30–65.
- C: HSV serology is not a recommended screening test.
- E: She is due for STI screening.
Source: Ch 0006, Table 6-4

**5. D** — For women 30–65 with negative HPV co-testing, screening can be repeated every 5 years. [verify]
- A, B: These intervals are too short for a normal co-test.
- C: Three years is the interval for cytology alone.
- E: Screening continues to 65.
Source: Ch 0006, Table 6-4

**6. B** — DEXA screening is recommended for women older than 65, or older than 60 with risk factors. She qualifies by age alone.
- A: Supplements without measuring bone density skip the screening test.
- C: Plain films are insensitive for low bone density.
- D: Alkaline phosphatase is not a screening test for osteoporosis.
- E: Age alone is the indication.
Source: Ch 0006, Table 6-4

**7. C** — BRCA carriers (5–20 times the risk of breast and ovarian cancer) should have genetic counseling and breast MRI. MRI is considered too harmful for average-risk women but is appropriate here.
- A: Exam alone is inadequate for a high-risk carrier.
- B: That is average-risk timing.
- D: Waiting for a lump is not screening.
- E: CA-125 is not a breast cancer screening test.
Source: Ch 0006, introduction (germline genomic data)

**8. C** — Because the benefit/harm balance of PSA screening is uncertain (overdiagnosis, indolent disease, competing mortality), expert groups recommend individualized shared decision-making.
- A: Screening is not automatic.
- B: Refusing outright ignores a reasonable patient choice.
- D: Biopsy is not indicated without an abnormal result.
- E: TRUS is not a screening test.
Source: Ch 0006, Implementing… (shared decision-making paragraph)

**9. A** — Folic acid is recommended for women planning or capable of pregnancy to prevent neural tube defects.
- B: Aspirin chemoprevention applies to ages 40–59 with high CVD risk.
- C: Vitamin D is for people older than 64 at fall risk.
- D: Iron is not a listed routine preventive intervention.
- E: Tamoxifen is for women at high breast cancer risk.
Source: Ch 0006, Table 6-5

**10. D** — Screening benefit accrues only after 5–10 years, and there are few data for most screening past 75. With advanced disease and limited life expectancy, care should shift to interventions that affect quality and length of life.
- A, B, C, E: Any colorectal screening modality offers no meaningful benefit given her prognosis.
Source: Ch 0006, Implementing Disease Prevention and Screening

**11. A** — Lead-time bias: screening moves the date of diagnosis earlier without moving the date of death later. Survival measured from diagnosis increases while mortality is unchanged.
- B: Recall bias concerns retrospective exposure reporting.
- C: Selecting high-risk patients would not leave mortality unchanged in this way.
- D: Better treatment would lower mortality.
- E: False positives do not explain longer survival.
Source: Ch 0006, Methods of Measuring Health Benefits

**12. B** — Length-time bias: periodic screening preferentially detects slowly progressive disease. The screened group is enriched with indolent cases and appears to do better.
- A: Lead-time bias is about earlier diagnosis dates, not tumor growth rate.
- C: Observer bias is measurement bias.
- D: Smoking confounding is not described here.
- E: Regression to the mean applies to repeated measurements.
Source: Ch 0006, Methods of Measuring Health Benefits

**13. D** — Lung cancer screening applies to current smokers or those who quit within the past 15 years. He quit 18 years ago, so he no longer qualifies despite 40 pack-years. [verify]
- A, B: He is outside the eligibility window.
- C: Chest x-ray is not an effective screening test.
- E: Sputum cytology is not recommended.
Source: Ch 0006, Table 6-4

**14. B** — Accidents are the leading cause of death at ages 15–24, followed by homicide and suicide. The corresponding interventions are counseling on seat belts and helmets and on the dangers of alcohol while driving.
- A: Lipid screening isn't targeted at this age group's top causes of death.
- C: An ECG isn't a recommended screening test.
- D: HCV screening is recommended but doesn't address the leading cause of death.
- E: Not a screening test.
Source: Ch 0006, Table 6-7

**15. C** — At ages 45–64 the leading cause of death is malignancy, followed by heart disease and accidents. Heart disease becomes the leading cause at 65 and older.
- A: Heart disease is #2 here and #1 at 65 and older.
- B: Accidents lead at 15–44.
- D, E: These rank lower.
Source: Ch 0006, Table 6-7

**16. B** — For adults 65 and older, Table 6-7 adds screening for visual and hearing problems, home safety issues and elder abuse, and fall-prevention exercise for those at higher risk. She tripped recently and lives alone.
- A: Few data support colorectal screening past 75.
- C: Chest x-ray isn't a screening test.
- D: AAA screening is for men 65–75 who have ever smoked.
- E: PSA doesn't apply to a woman.
Source: Ch 0006, Table 6-7

**17. C** — Getting a 35-year-old smoker to quit adds an estimated 3–5 years. It's the largest gain in Table 6-3.
- A: About 1 month.
- B: 9 months to 2 years; substantial but smaller.
- D: 2–3 months.
- E: 0–5 days.
Source: Ch 0006, Table 6-3; Counseling on Healthy Behaviors

**18. C** — The WHO principles require an accepted treatment and a detectable latent stage where early treatment helps. Alzheimer's disease has no curative treatment and few data that early treatment improves outcomes.
- A: Polypectomy prevents progression to cancer.
- B, E: Early treatment lowers cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risk.
- D: Cytology detects a treatable preinvasive stage.
Source: Ch 0006, Table 6-1; Basic Principles of Screening
