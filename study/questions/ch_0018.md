# Ch 18 — Low Back Pain: practice questions

**1.** A 36-year-old man comes to the office because of 5 days of low back pain that began after he helped a friend move furniture. The pain does not radiate. He has no fever, weight loss, bladder or bowel changes, or history of cancer. Vital signs are normal. There is paraspinal tenderness. Straight leg raising is negative, and strength, sensation, and reflexes are normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. MRI of the lumbar spine
B. Over-the-counter analgesic such as an NSAID, education, and staying active with physical therapy
C. Plain radiographs of the lumbar spine
D. Oxycodone for 2 weeks and bed rest
E. Gabapentin

**2.** A 49-year-old woman has had 5 months of nonradicular low back pain. Physical therapy and a home exercise program have helped only partially. She has no red flags, and neurologic examination is normal. She asks about a daily medication. Which of the following has the best support for her chronic low back pain?

A. Acetaminophen as first-line therapy
B. Gabapentin
C. Long-term opioid therapy
D. Duloxetine
E. Diazepam

**3.** A 52-year-old man has had 2 days of severe low back pain. Today he could not urinate and noticed numbness between his legs. Post-void residual is 700 mL. There is decreased sensation in the perianal area, reduced rectal tone, and bilateral ankle weakness. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Urgent MRI of the lumbar spine with urgent surgical consultation
B. Physical therapy referral
C. Epidural steroid injection
D. Outpatient electromyography
E. Plain radiographs of the lumbar spine

**4.** A 74-year-old man has had 1 year of pain in both buttocks and thighs that comes on after walking two blocks and is relieved by sitting down. He can walk much farther in the grocery store when leaning on the shopping cart. Pedal pulses are normal. He walks with a wide-based gait. Straight leg raising is negative bilaterally. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Herniated lumbar disk
B. Peripheral arterial disease
C. Facet joint arthropathy
D. Sacroiliac joint dysfunction
E. Lumbar spinal stenosis

**5.** A 38-year-old man has 3 weeks of low back pain radiating down the back of his left leg to the foot. Which of the following examination findings is most specific for a lumbar disk herniation?

A. Pain reproduced by raising the left leg
B. Anterior thigh pain on the femoral stretch test
C. Left leg pain reproduced by raising the right leg
D. Paraspinal tenderness
E. A positive Schober test

**6.** A 67-year-old woman has 2 years of right-sided low back pain that is worse in the morning and when standing, and better when she sits. There is right paraspinal tenderness. She has no leg symptoms, and neurologic examination is normal. Two separate fluoroscopically guided anesthetic blocks of the right L4–L5 medial branch nerves each relieved more than half of her pain. Physical therapy and NSAIDs have not provided lasting benefit. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Radiofrequency ablation of the medial branch nerves
B. Lumbar interbody fusion
C. Provocative discography
D. Basivertebral nerve ablation
E. Epidural steroid injection

**7.** A 30-year-old woman fell onto her buttocks while skiing 4 months ago. She now has left-sided pain below the L5 level that sometimes radiates to the groin. The FABER (Patrick) test, Gaenslen test, and compression test are all positive on the left. Hip range of motion is otherwise normal. Which of the following is the reference standard for confirming the source of her pain?

A. MRI of the lumbar spine
B. Provocative discography
C. Lumbar medial branch block
D. Low-volume intraarticular anesthetic injection of the sacroiliac joint
E. Electromyography

**8.** A 78-year-old woman with osteoporosis has an acute T12 compression fracture diagnosed 3 weeks ago after she lifted a laundry basket. Despite oral analgesics and physical therapy, her pain remains severe and she cannot get out of bed without help. Neurologic examination is normal. MRI shows marrow edema at T12 without retropulsion. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Lumbar interbody fusion
B. Continue the current regimen for 6 more months
C. Basivertebral nerve radiofrequency ablation
D. Epidural steroid injection
E. Consider percutaneous vertebral augmentation (vertebroplasty or kyphoplasty)

**9.** A 42-year-old man has an L4–L5 disk herniation compressing the left L5 nerve root. Over 2 weeks, his left ankle dorsiflexion strength has worsened from 4/5 to 2/5 despite NSAIDs and physical therapy. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Continue physical therapy for 6 more weeks
B. Surgical decompression
C. Epidural steroid injection
D. Start gabapentin
E. Repeat MRI in 3 months

**10.** A 35-year-old woman has had 6 weeks of pain radiating from the low back down the back of her right leg to the lateral foot. MRI shows an L5–S1 disk herniation contacting the right S1 root. Strength is normal and stable. NSAIDs and 6 weeks of physical therapy have given little relief. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Lumbar interbody fusion
B. Long-term opioid therapy
C. Epidural steroid injection
D. Pregabalin, as the most effective agent for radicular pain
E. Provocative discography

**11.** A 33-year-old man who injects heroin has 1 week of severe midback pain that is worse at night, with fevers and chills. Temperature is 38.6°C (101.5°F). There is focal tenderness over the lower thoracic spine. Neurologic examination is normal. Which of the following is the most likely underlying cause?

A. Spinal infection
B. Cauda equina syndrome
C. Spondylolysis
D. Spinal epidural hematoma
E. Muscle strain

**12.** A 45-year-old man has low back pain radiating down the back of the left leg. On examination, the left ankle (Achilles) reflex is absent and the right is normal. Which nerve root is most likely involved?

A. L2
B. L3
C. L4
D. S1
E. T12

**13.** A 60-year-old man with chronic low back pain has been taking oxycodone for a recent severe flare. At an urgent care visit, he was also prescribed diazepam for back spasms. He has no red flags, and neurologic examination is normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Continue both medications at the current doses
B. Add gabapentin
C. Increase the oxycodone dose
D. Add scheduled acetaminophen as first-line therapy
E. Stop the diazepam and use a nonbenzodiazepine muscle relaxant if needed

## Answers

**1. B** — Most acute low back pain resolves within 6 weeks. Over-the-counter analgesics plus nonpharmacologic care (targeted physical therapy, core strengthening, education) usually suffice without imaging or prescription analgesics. In acute LBP, only red flags or serious or progressive deficits warrant imaging.
- A, C: There are no red flags or neurologic deficits.
- D: Opioids show no long-term benefit; bed rest isn't recommended.
- E: Evidence for gabapentinoids in back pain is insufficient.
Source: Ch 0018, Imaging; Multimodal and Interdisciplinary Strategies

**2. D** — Pharmacologic options for chronic LBP include NSAIDs, nonbenzodiazepine muscle relaxants, and antidepressants such as duloxetine (an SNRI) or tricyclics.
- A: Acetaminophen is unlikely to help and is no longer recommended first line.
- B: Evidence for gabapentinoids is insufficient.
- C: Opioids haven't shown long-term benefit and carry serious harms.
- E: Benzodiazepines aren't among the recommended relaxants.
Source: Ch 0018, Pharmacotherapy

**3. A** — Saddle anesthesia, urinary retention, reduced rectal tone and bilateral deficits are red flags for cauda equina syndrome. After checking perianal sensation and rectal tone, urgent MRI confirmation may be needed.
- B, C: Conservative or interventional pain treatment delays an emergency.
- D: Electrodiagnostics don't belong in an emergency workup.
- E: Plain films don't show neural compression.
Source: Ch 0018, History and Physical Exam; Table 18-4

**4. E** — Neurogenic claudication (leg pain with walking relieved by sitting) is the hallmark of spinal stenosis. So are relief with leaning forward (the shopping cart sign), a wide-based gait, and a usually negative straight leg raise.
- A: A herniated disk causes dermatomal pain, usually with a positive SLR.
- B: Normal pulses and relief from sitting or flexion favor a neurogenic cause.
- C: Facet pain is axial and is improved, not provoked, by sitting.
- D: SI pain is below L5 and unilateral.
Source: Ch 0018, Spinal Stenosis; Table 18-3

**5. C** — The crossed straight leg raise (pain in the affected leg when the opposite leg is raised) has consistently high specificity (>85%) but low sensitivity.
- A: The ipsilateral SLR is sensitive (~80% for L5/S1) but has variable specificity.
- B: The femoral stretch test probes mid-lumbar roots and is only moderately sensitive.
- D: Paraspinal tenderness is weakly associated with facet pain.
- E: Schober tests lumbar flexion and is nonspecific.
Source: Ch 0018, History and Physical Exam; Table 18-2

**6. A** — Facet joint pain can be established only by diagnostic medial branch blocks. When the blocks give significant relief (e.g., ≥50%), the same nerves can be treated with radiofrequency ablation.
- B: Fusion is for instability or spondylolisthesis, not facet pain.
- C: Discography is for suspected discogenic pain.
- D: Basivertebral ablation targets vertebrogenic (Modic) pain.
- E: ESIs treat radicular pain; she has no leg symptoms.
Source: Ch 0018, Lumbar Medial Branch Nerve Blocks and Ablation; Table 18-3

**7. D** — A low-volume intraarticular SI joint injection is both therapeutic and the reference standard for diagnosis. Three or more positive provocation tests, pain below L5 and groin radiation all raise the likelihood of SI pain.
- A: Imaging is often unremarkable in extraarticular SI pathology and doesn't confirm the pain source.
- B: Discography is for discogenic pain.
- C: Medial branch blocks diagnose facet pain.
- E: EMG evaluates nerve roots and peripheral nerves.
Source: Ch 0018, SI Joint Interventions; Table 18-2

**8. E** — For severe pain or disability from an acute (<6 weeks) compression fracture, vertebroplasty or kyphoplasty can be considered, although evidence is conflicting. [verify: guideline may have changed since this edition]
- A: Fusion isn't used for an uncomplicated osteoporotic fracture.
- B: Most fractures improve within 6–8 weeks, but augmentation is time-limited to acute fractures, and her disability is severe now.
- C: Basivertebral ablation is for chronic pain with Modic changes.
- D: ESI treats radicular pain.
Source: Ch 0018, Vertebral Augmentation and Basivertebral Nerve Ablation

**9. B** — Lumbar nerve root decompression is indicated for severe or progressive neurologic deficits (loss of motor or sensory function or reflexes).
- A, E: Waiting risks a permanent deficit.
- C: ESI is for pain, not a progressive motor deficit.
- D: Gabapentinoids have minimal benefit and don't address compression.
Source: Ch 0018, Surgery

**10. C** — Epidural steroid injections for radicular pain from disk herniation are likely most effective in the acute phase. They may have a weak surgery-sparing effect.
- A: Fusion adds complications without benefit when there's no instability.
- B: Long-term opioids lack benefit.
- D: Meta-analyses suggest gabapentinoids give minimal benefit relative to harms.
- E: Discography is for discogenic axial pain.
Source: Ch 0018, Epidural Steroid Injections; Table 18-3

**11. A** — IV drug use is a red flag for infection, and fever with pain worse at night points to infection or tumor.
- B: Cauda equina requires saddle anesthesia, sphincter dysfunction or progressive deficits.
- C: Spondylolysis is a red flag in patients ≤18.
- D: Hematoma follows anticoagulation, trauma or a spine procedure.
- E: Strain doesn't cause fever or night pain.
Source: Ch 0018, Red Flags; Table 18-4

**12. D** — The Achilles reflex reflects S1. The patellar reflex reflects L4, and sometimes L2 or L3.
- A, B, C: These roots relate to the patellar reflex, not the Achilles.
- E: T12 doesn't contribute to lower-limb reflexes.
Source: Ch 0018, History and Physical Exam (neurologic exam)

**13. E** — Concomitant opioids and benzodiazepines should be avoided because of the increased risk of respiratory depression. Nonbenzodiazepine muscle relaxants are among the recommended agents.
- A: Continuing the combination is unsafe.
- B: Evidence for gabapentinoids is insufficient.
- C: Opioids should be used at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time.
- D: Acetaminophen is no longer first line for back pain.
Source: Ch 0018, Pharmacotherapy
