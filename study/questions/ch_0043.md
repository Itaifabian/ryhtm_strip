# Ch 43 — Edema: practice questions

**1.** A 57-year-old woman has 3 weeks of bilateral leg swelling that pits to the knees and puffiness of her face. She has no dyspnea, orthopnea or known liver disease. Which of the following is the most important initial step in determining the cause of her generalized edema?

A. Lower-extremity venous duplex
B. Lymphoscintigraphy
C. Start furosemide and reassess
D. Measure serum albumin and obtain a urinalysis
E. Thyroid ultrasound

**2.** A 44-year-old man with long-standing type 1 diabetes notices swelling around his eyes that is worst on waking, along with leg edema. Blood pressure is 148/92 mm Hg. Serum albumin is 2.4 g/dL, total cholesterol is 310 mg/dL, and the urine dipstick is strongly positive for protein. JVP is normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Quantify urinary protein excretion
B. Echocardiogram
C. Liver biopsy
D. Paracentesis
E. Lower-extremity venous duplex

**3.** A 68-year-old man has 2 months of progressive leg edema, exertional dyspnea, two-pillow orthopnea and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea. JVP is elevated, an S3 is present, and the extremities are cool. Serum sodium is 131 mEq/L and the BUN-to-creatinine ratio is elevated. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Serum albumin and liver biopsy
B. 24-hour urine protein
C. Lymphoscintigraphy
D. Abdominal ultrasound for ascites
E. Natriuretic peptide level, ECG and echocardiogram

**4.** A 61-year-old woman developed bilateral ankle edema 6 weeks after starting amlodipine for hypertension. JVP is normal, lungs are clear, and albumin, creatinine and urinalysis are normal. Which of the following is the most likely cause, and the most appropriate management?

A. Drug-induced edema from the calcium channel blocker; reduce the dose or switch antihypertensive class
B. Early heart failure; add furosemide and continue amlodipine
C. Nephrotic syndrome; refer for renal biopsy
D. Deep venous thrombosis; start anticoagulation
E. Hypothyroidism; start levothyroxine

**5.** A 55-year-old man with heavy alcohol use has abdominal distention and later leg edema. JVP is low, blood pressure is 102/64 mm Hg, and he has spider angiomata, palmar erythema and gynecomastia. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Echocardiogram as the primary test
B. Liver function tests, serum albumin and evaluation of the ascites
C. 24-hour urine protein as the primary test
D. Lower-extremity venous duplex
E. Lymphoscintigraphy

**6.** A 63-year-old man who smokes has 3 weeks of progressive swelling of the face, neck and both arms. His legs are not swollen. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Serum albumin and urinalysis only
B. Echocardiogram for heart failure
C. Lower-extremity venous duplex
D. Evaluate for superior vena cava obstruction
E. Start furosemide

**7.** A 66-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes has new bilateral leg edema 2 months after a medication change. Her medications are metformin, pioglitazone (newly added), atorvastatin and lisinopril. Cardiac examination, albumin, creatinine and urinalysis are normal. Which medication is the most likely cause?

A. Metformin
B. Pioglitazone
C. Atorvastatin
D. Lisinopril
E. None; drug-induced edema doesn't occur with these classes

**8.** A 38-year-old woman with exophthalmos and a suppressed TSH has firm swelling over both shins. Pressing on it leaves no indentation. Which of the following is the most likely cause?

A. Heart failure
B. Nephrotic syndrome
C. Deep venous thrombosis
D. Cirrhosis
E. Pretibial myxedema of Graves disease

**9.** A 12-year-old boy has edema, cola-colored urine and hypertension 2 weeks after a sore throat. JVP is mildly elevated. Which of the following best explains his edema?

A. Primary renal sodium and water retention, with normal or increased cardiac output
B. Low-output heart failure
C. Hypoalbuminemia from hepatic failure
D. Lymphatic obstruction
E. Increased capillary permeability from sepsis

**10.** A 30-year-old woman recovering from prolonged starvation develops worsening edema after starting an adequate diet. In addition to increased sodium intake, which hormonal mechanism directly contributes?

A. Suppression of aldosterone
B. Increased atrial natriuretic peptide
C. Increased insulin release, which increases tubular sodium reabsorption
D. Decreased vasopressin
E. Increased thyroid hormone

**11.** A 52-year-old woman who had an axillary lymph node dissection for breast cancer 2 years ago has persistent swelling of the ipsilateral arm. Which of the following explains why this type of edema is particularly hard to treat?

A. Hypoalbuminemia
B. Low cardiac output
C. Restricted lymphatic flow raises both capillary pressure and interstitial protein concentration
D. Renal sodium retention from RAAS activation
E. Venous valve incompetence alone

## Answers

**1. D** — After establishing that edema is generalized, determine whether there's serious hypoalbuminemia (<3.0 g/dL). If present, the history, exam and urinalysis point to cirrhosis, malnutrition or nephrotic syndrome.
- A: Duplex addresses localized edema.
- B: Lymphoscintigraphy also addresses localized edema.
- C: Diuretics before a diagnosis skip the evaluation.
- E: Thyroid imaging isn't an initial edema test.
Source: Ch 0043, Approach to the Patient: Edema

**2. A** — Periorbital morning edema, hypoalbuminemia (<3.0 g/dL), hypercholesterolemia and proteinuria in a diabetic patient suggest nephrotic syndrome. It's defined by proteinuria ≥3.5 g/d, so quantification is the next step.
- B: Normal JVP and the lab pattern don't suggest heart failure.
- C: The labs point to the kidney.
- D: There's no ascites.
- E: Edema is generalized and periorbital, not localized.
Source: Ch 0043, Table 43-1; Nephrotic Syndrome

**3. E** — Exertional dyspnea, orthopnea, PND, raised JVP, S3, hyponatremia and a high BUN:creatinine ratio indicate cardiac edema. Natriuretic peptides, ECG and echocardiography establish heart disease.
- A: There are no liver features.
- B: Nothing suggests nephrotic syndrome.
- C: Edema is generalized.
- D: Ascites isn't the leading issue.
Source: Ch 0043, Table 43-1; Heart Failure

**4. A** — Calcium channel blockers (arteriolar dilation) are a recognized cause of drug-induced edema. A normal JVP, lungs, albumin and urinalysis support a drug cause.
- B: No heart-failure findings are present.
- C: A normal urinalysis and albumin argue against nephrotic syndrome.
- D: Bilateral ankle edema after starting a CCB doesn't suggest DVT.
- E: There are no myxedema findings.
Source: Ch 0043, Drug-Induced Edema; Table 43-2; Other Causes of Edema

**5. B** — Ascites with low JVP, lower blood pressure and stigmata of chronic liver disease indicate hepatic edema. Evaluate liver function and albumin.
- A: Low JVP argues against cardiac edema.
- C: The picture is hepatic, not renal.
- D: Edema followed ascites; that's not a localized venous pattern.
- E: There's no lymphatic process.
Source: Ch 0043, Table 43-1; Hepatic Cirrhosis

**6. D** — Edema confined to the face, neck and upper extremities, where venous pressure is higher than in the legs, indicates superior vena cava obstruction.
- A: Hypoalbuminemic edema is generalized.
- B: Heart failure edema is dependent, in the legs.
- C: The legs aren't involved.
- E: Diuresis doesn't address an obstruction.
Source: Ch 0043, Distribution of Edema

**7. B** — Thiazolidinediones are listed among the drugs associated with edema (Table 43-2).
- A: Metformin isn't listed.
- C: Statins aren't listed.
- D: ACE inhibitors aren't listed.
- E: Many drug classes cause edema.
Source: Ch 0043, Table 43-2

**8. E** — Pretibial myxedema of Graves disease is typically nonpitting.
- A: Heart failure causes pitting edema.
- B: Nephrotic edema is pitting.
- C: DVT causes pitting edema.
- D: Cirrhotic edema is pitting.
Source: Ch 0043, Other Causes of Edema

**9. A** — Edema in acute glomerulonephritis comes with hematuria, proteinuria and hypertension. It results from primary renal sodium and water retention, with normal or even increased cardiac output, unlike most heart failure.
- B: Cardiac output isn't reduced in acute GN.
- C: There's no liver disease.
- D: Lymphatic obstruction causes localized edema.
- E: Sepsis isn't present.
Source: Ch 0043, Edema of Renal Disease

**10. C** — Refeeding edema results from increased sodium intake and increased insulin release, which directly increases tubular sodium reabsorption.
- A: Aldosterone suppression would cause natriuresis.
- B: ANP promotes natriuresis.
- D: Less vasopressin would cause water loss.
- E: Thyroid hormone isn't the mechanism described.
Source: Ch 0043, Edema of Nutritional Origin

**11. C** — Lymphedema (here after node resection) is particularly intractable because restricted lymphatic flow raises both intracapillary pressure and interstitial protein, which together worsen fluid retention.
- A: Lymphedema is localized and doesn't depend on albumin.
- B: Lymphedema is localized and doesn't depend on cardiac output.
- D: RAAS-driven retention causes generalized edema.
- E: Venous valve failure is a different mechanism of localized edema.
Source: Ch 0043, Localized Edema
