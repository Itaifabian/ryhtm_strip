# Ch 58 — Acidosis and Alkalosis: practice questions

**1.** A 58-year-old man in septic shock has arterial pH 7.41, PaCO₂ 20 mm Hg and HCO₃⁻ 12 mmol/L. Na is 140 and Cl 106 mEq/L. Which acid-base disturbance is present?

A. Simple metabolic acidosis with appropriate compensation
B. Simple respiratory alkalosis
C. High-anion gap metabolic acidosis with a concurrent respiratory alkalosis
D. Metabolic acidosis with concurrent respiratory acidosis
E. Metabolic alkalosis with respiratory compensation

**2.** A 64-year-old woman with cirrhosis has Na 138, Cl 104, HCO₃⁻ 20 mEq/L and albumin 2.5 g/dL. Which statement about her anion gap is correct?

A. Her AG of 14 is normal
B. Albumin doesn't affect the AG
C. Her corrected AG is 9
D. Hypoalbuminemia raises the AG
E. Her corrected AG is about 19, indicating a high-AG acidosis

**3.** A 45-year-old man with alcohol use disorder has vomited for 3 days and now has abdominal pain. Na 135, Cl 81, HCO₃⁻ 24 mEq/L, pH 7.41, PaCO₂ 39 mm Hg. β-hydroxybutyrate is markedly elevated. Which disorder is present?

A. Mixed high-AG metabolic acidosis and metabolic alkalosis
B. No acid-base disorder; values are normal
C. Simple metabolic alkalosis
D. Simple respiratory alkalosis
E. Non-AG metabolic acidosis

**4.** A 36-year-old man is found confused after drinking an unknown liquid from his garage. pH is 7.16, AG 28, measured osmolality 330 and calculated osmolality 290 mOsm/kg. Urinalysis shows envelope-shaped crystals, and creatinine is rising. Toxic alcohol levels will take 6 hours. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?

A. Wait for ethylene glycol levels before treating
B. Oral activated charcoal only
C. IV sodium bicarbonate alone
D. Fomepizole now, with hemodialysis
E. Oral ethanol as first-line therapy

**5.** A 52-year-old man drank a large amount of rubbing alcohol. He is drowsy and his breath smells fruity. Osmolar gap is 40, AG is 11, glucose 62 mg/dL, and serum acetone is positive. He is hemodynamically stable, and his level is below 400 mg/dL. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?

A. Fomepizole and hemodialysis
B. Supportive care with IV fluids and glucose
C. Sodium bicarbonate infusion
D. Urine alkalinization
E. Methylene blue

**6.** A 39-year-old woman ingested a large amount of aspirin. pH is 7.38, PaCO₂ 25 mm Hg, HCO₃⁻ 15, and AG 22. Renal function is normal. After activated charcoal, which of the following is the most appropriate therapy?

A. Acetazolamide alone
B. Furosemide
C. IV sodium bicarbonate to alkalinize the urine to pH >7.5, with potassium and glucose
D. Hypotonic saline only
E. Immediate hemodialysis regardless of renal function

**7.** A 22-year-old man with type 1 diabetes has DKA. pH is 7.15, HCO₃⁻ 7, glucose 520 mg/dL, and potassium 4.8 mEq/L. He isn't in shock. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?

A. Isotonic saline and IV insulin (0.1 U/kg bolus, then 0.1 U/kg/h) without bicarbonate
B. IV sodium bicarbonate bolus to normalize pH
C. Insulin only after bicarbonate corrects the pH
D. Hypotonic saline with bicarbonate
E. Subcutaneous insulin and discharge

**8.** A 31-year-old woman with Sjögren syndrome has recurrent calcium phosphate stones. Labs show non-AG metabolic acidosis, K 2.7 mEq/L, urine pH 6.6, and a positive urine anion gap. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial management?

A. Start sodium bicarbonate before addressing potassium
B. Potassium-sparing diuretic
C. Fludrocortisone
D. Acetazolamide
E. Correct hypokalemia first, then begin alkali therapy

**9.** A 70-year-old man with diabetic nephropathy (eGFR 38) taking lisinopril has K 5.9 mEq/L, non-AG metabolic acidosis (HCO₃⁻ 18) and urine pH 5.2. Which of the following is the most appropriate step to improve both abnormalities?

A. IV calcium gluconate
B. Sodium bicarbonate alone
C. Increase lisinopril
D. Lower potassium (e.g., patiromer, low-K diet, review K-retaining drugs)
E. Start trimethoprim prophylaxis

**10.** A 27-year-old woman has 5 days of persistent vomiting. BP is 98/60 mm Hg with orthostatic tachycardia. pH 7.54, HCO₃⁻ 38, K 2.9 mEq/L, urine Cl 8 mEq/L. Which of the following is the most appropriate treatment?

A. Acetazolamide
B. IV isotonic saline with potassium chloride
C. Dilute hydrochloric acid infusion
D. Spironolactone
E. Amiloride

**11.** A 74-year-old man with decompensated heart failure on high-dose furosemide has pH 7.52, HCO₃⁻ 36 and K 3.6. He has pulmonary edema, so saline can't be given. His kidney function is adequate. Which of the following is most appropriate to correct the alkalosis?

A. IV normal saline 2 L
B. Stop all diuretics and give albumin
C. IV acetazolamide, with potassium monitoring
D. Dilute HCl infusion
E. Sodium bicarbonate

**12.** A 50-year-old man has chronic watery diarrhea and non-AG metabolic acidosis. Urine Na 30, K 20 and Cl 75 mEq/L. Urine pH is 6.2. What does the urine anion gap suggest?

A. Distal RTA
B. Proximal RTA
C. Type 4 RTA
D. High-AG acidosis
E. Appropriately high urine ammonium, consistent with GI bicarbonate loss

**13.** A 66-year-old woman in cardiogenic shock has lactic acidosis with pH 6.95 and HCO₃⁻ 6. If sodium bicarbonate is given, what is the appropriate goal?

A. Raise arterial pH to no more than about 7.2 (HCO₃⁻ ≤12)
B. Normalize pH to 7.40 quickly
C. Give a large undiluted bolus to HCO₃⁻ 24
D. Bicarbonate is never given in lactic acidosis
E. Target pH 7.50 to counter ongoing lactate production

**14.** A 68-year-old man with severe COPD and chronic hypercapnia (baseline PaCO₂ 62, HCO₃⁻ 36) is intubated for pneumonia. The ventilator is set to bring his PaCO₂ down to 38 within an hour. Which of the following is the most appropriate approach?

A. Continue; rapid normalization of PaCO₂ is ideal
B. Add sodium bicarbonate
C. Give acetazolamide immediately
D. Lower PaCO₂ gradually toward his baseline, providing Cl⁻ and K⁺
E. Increase sedation and stop ventilation

## Answers

**1. C** — AG = 140 − (106 + 12) = 22, a high-AG acidosis. Winter formula predicts PaCO₂ = 1.5 × 12 + 8 ± 2 = 24–28. A PaCO₂ of 20 is lower, so a concurrent respiratory alkalosis is present (common in sepsis).
- A: The PaCO₂ is below the predicted range.
- B: HCO₃⁻ is too low for a simple respiratory alkalosis.
- D: Respiratory acidosis would put PaCO₂ above predicted.
- E: HCO₃⁻ is low, so this isn't a metabolic alkalosis.
Source: Ch 0058, Simple Acid-Base Disorders; Table 58-1; Table 58-2

**2. E** — Uncorrected AG = 138 − (104 + 20) = 14. Add 2.5 for each g/dL of albumin below 4.5: 14 + 2.5 × 2 = 19, a high AG.
- A: Uncorrected, it looks normal.
- B: Albumin is the main unmeasured anion.
- C: The correction adds, not subtracts.
- D: Hypoalbuminemia lowers the measured AG.
Source: Ch 0058, Evaluate the Anion Gap

**3. A** — AG = 135 − (81 + 24) = 30, so ΔAG is 20 but ΔHCO₃⁻ is 0. The incongruity reveals a high-AG ketoacidosis coexisting with metabolic alkalosis from vomiting, despite a normal pH and HCO₃⁻.
- B: The high AG is meaningful even with normal pH and HCO₃⁻.
- C: The elevated AG shows an acidosis too.
- D: PaCO₂ is normal.
- E: The AG is high.
Source: Ch 0058, Approach to the Patient (Δ values); Table 58-2

**4. D** — A high AG plus a high osmolar gap with oxalate crystals means ethylene glycol toxicity. Treatment shouldn't wait for levels. Give fomepizole (15 mg/kg load). Dialysis is indicated for pH <7.3, osmolar gap >20, or end-organ damage.
- A: Delay is the key error.
- B: Charcoal alone doesn't treat it.
- C: Bicarbonate alone doesn't block metabolism.
- E: Ethanol is used only if fomepizole is unavailable.
Source: Ch 0058, Ethylene Glycol; Treatment

**5. B** — Isopropyl alcohol raises the osmolar gap but not the AG (acetone is excreted), and hypoglycemia is common. Treatment is supportive, with dialysis reserved for instability, prolonged coma, or levels >400 mg/dL.
- A: The parent compound, not a metabolite, is toxic, so fomepizole isn't indicated.
- C: There's no acidosis to treat.
- D: Alkalinization is for salicylates.
- E: Methylene blue is for methemoglobinemia.
Source: Ch 0058, Isopropyl Alcohol

**6. C** — Salicylate toxicity causes a high-AG acidosis with respiratory alkalosis. After charcoal, IV bicarbonate to urine pH >7.5 raises clearance about fivefold. Replace potassium and give glucose. Dialysis is for AKI.
- A: Acetazolamide alone can cause systemic acidosis and reduce clearance.
- B: Furosemide isn't the therapy.
- D: Hypotonic saline isn't the therapy.
- E: Dialysis isn't automatic with normal renal function.
Source: Ch 0058, Treatment: Salicylate-Induced Acidosis

**7. A** — Bicarbonate in adult DKA is reserved for pH <7.00 or shock. Treat with isotonic saline (avoiding overexpansion) and IV regular insulin (0.1 U/kg bolus, then 0.1 U/kg/h) until the AG closes.
- B: pH 7.15 doesn't meet the threshold, and normalizing pH is never the goal.
- C: Insulin shouldn't wait.
- D: Bicarbonate isn't indicated.
- E: DKA needs IV therapy.
Source: Ch 0058, Diabetic Ketoacidosis; Treatment: Metabolic Acidosis

**8. E** — Classic distal (type 1) RTA shows hypokalemia, non-AG acidosis, urine pH >5.5, low NH₄⁺ (positive UAG), and stones from hypocitraturia and hypercalciuria. Correct hypokalemia first, then give alkali to HCO₃⁻ 24–26.
- A: Alkali before potassium risks worsening hypokalemia.
- B: Potassium-sparing diuretics aren't the therapy.
- C: Fludrocortisone is for type 4 with hypoaldosteronism.
- D: Acetazolamide causes acidosis.
Source: Ch 0058, Non–Anion Gap Metabolic Acidoses; Treatment

**9. D** — In type 4 RTA, hyperkalemia directly reduces ammoniagenesis. Restoring normokalemia (patiromer, low-K diet, stopping K-retaining drugs) improves net acid excretion and the acidosis.
- A: Calcium is for emergent cardiac toxicity.
- B: Bicarbonate alone doesn't address potassium.
- C: More ACE inhibitor worsens K.
- E: Trimethoprim raises K.
Source: Ch 0058, Treatment: Non–Anion Gap Metabolic Acidoses

**10. B** — Vomiting-related alkalosis is maintained by volume, chloride and potassium depletion (low urine Cl). It's saline-responsive: give isotonic NaCl and KCl.
- A: Acetazolamide is for patients who can't receive saline.
- C: Dilute HCl isn't advised.
- D: Spironolactone is for mineralocorticoid excess.
- E: Amiloride is for mineralocorticoid excess or Liddle.
Source: Ch 0058, Metabolic Alkalosis: Etiology; Treatment

**11. C** — When conditions like heart failure prevent saline, acetazolamide 125–250 mg IV speeds renal bicarbonate loss in patients with adequate kidney function. Monitor for hypokalemia.
- A: Saline would worsen pulmonary edema.
- B: This doesn't address the alkalosis directly.
- D: HCl isn't advised.
- E: Bicarbonate worsens alkalosis.
Source: Ch 0058, Treatment: Metabolic Alkalosis

**12. E** — UAG = (30 + 20) − 75 = −25. A negative UAG suggests appropriately high urine ammonium, consistent with an extrarenal (GI) cause. Urine pH can exceed 6 in diarrhea because of high NH₄⁺.
- A: RTA gives a positive UAG.
- B: RTA gives a positive UAG.
- C: RTA gives a positive UAG.
- D: The AG is normal.
Source: Ch 0058, Non–Anion Gap Metabolic Acidoses

**13. A** — For acute severe acidemia (pH <7.00), give enough bicarbonate to raise pH to no more than 7.2 or HCO₃⁻ to no more than 12. Returning pH to normal is deleterious: it can worsen lactate production, cause volume overload and hypercapnia, and produce overshoot alkalosis.
- B: Normalizing pH is harmful.
- C: Large boluses are harmful.
- D: Bicarbonate is used for pH <7.0.
- E: Overshoot is harmful.
Source: Ch 0058, Approach to the Patient: L-Lactic Acid Acidosis

**14. D** — Rapid correction of chronic hypercapnia can cause arrhythmias, reduced cerebral perfusion and seizures, plus posthypercapnic alkalosis. Lower PaCO₂ gradually toward baseline and give Cl⁻ and K⁺ so the kidneys can excrete HCO₃⁻.
- A: Rapid normalization is harmful.
- B: Bicarbonate is inappropriate with an already high HCO₃⁻.
- C: Acetazolamide may be considered later for posthypercapnic alkalosis but isn't the first step.
- E: Withdrawing ventilation is dangerous.
Source: Ch 0058, Treatment: Respiratory Acidosis
