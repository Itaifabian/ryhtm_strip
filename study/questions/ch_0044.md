# Ch 44 — Approach to the Patient with a Heart Murmur: practice questions

**1.** A 67-year-old man is hospitalized on day 4 after an inferior-posterior myocardial infarction. He develops sudden dyspnea, hypotension and pulmonary edema. A soft early systolic murmur is heard at the apex with no thrill. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Urgent transthoracic echocardiography
B. Repeat auscultation in 24 hours
C. Exercise stress testing
D. Outpatient cardiology referral
E. Start a β-blocker and observe

**2.** A 22-year-old college athlete has a grade 2/6 midsystolic murmur at the left sternal border found on a preparticipation exam. He has no symptoms. The rest of the cardiac exam, including S2 splitting and carotid pulses, is normal, and there are no other signs of heart disease. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Transthoracic echocardiography
B. Cardiac MRI
C. Exercise stress testing
D. No further testing
E. Transesophageal echocardiography

**3.** A 74-year-old woman has a grade 3/6 crescendo-decrescendo midsystolic murmur at the second right intercostal space radiating to the carotids. She denies symptoms. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. No further testing, since she is asymptomatic
B. Transthoracic echocardiography
C. Coronary angiography
D. Repeat examination in 5 years
E. Start a vasodilator

**4.** A 50-year-old man has a soft, high-pitched decrescendo diastolic murmur along the left sternal border, heard only with him leaning forward at end expiration. He has no symptoms. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Reassurance; soft murmurs are benign
B. Exercise stress testing
C. Chest radiograph only
D. Repeat auscultation in 1 year
E. Transthoracic echocardiography

**5.** A 19-year-old man has a systolic murmur between the left lower sternal border and the apex. ECG shows left ventricular hypertrophy, and the carotid upstroke is brisk. Which of the following maneuvers would most likely increase the intensity of this murmur?

A. Squatting
B. Passive leg raising
C. Standing from a squatting position
D. Sustained handgrip
E. Intravenous phenylephrine

**6.** A 24-year-old woman has a grade 3/6 systolic murmur at the left lower sternal border that gets louder with the Valsalva maneuver. ECG shows LVH. She has no click. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step to confirm the diagnosis?

A. Cardiac catheterization
B. Chest radiograph
C. Holter monitor
D. Transthoracic echocardiography
E. Exercise stress test

**7.** A 9-year-old boy has a continuous murmur in the right supraclavicular fossa. It disappears with firm pressure over the right internal jugular vein and when he turns his head. He is well. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?

A. Transthoracic echocardiography
B. Cardiac MRI
C. Referral for ductus closure
D. Reassurance
E. CT angiography of the neck

**8.** A 78-year-old man has a systolic murmur heard at the base and apex. During auscultation he has a premature ventricular contraction, and the murmur is noticeably louder in the beat after it. Which of the following is the most likely cause?

A. Chronic mitral regurgitation
B. Aortic stenosis
C. Ventricular septal defect
D. Tricuspid regurgitation
E. Mitral valve prolapse

**9.** A 35-year-old woman has a grade 2/6 midsystolic murmur at the upper left sternal border and wide splitting of S2 that doesn't change with respiration. She reports mild exertional dyspnea. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Reassurance
B. Repeat examination in 1 year
C. Cardiac catheterization
D. Exercise stress test
E. Transthoracic echocardiography

**10.** A patient develops acute severe mitral regurgitation from papillary muscle rupture after a myocardial infarction. Which of the following statements is correct?

A. A loud holosystolic murmur with a thrill is present in nearly all cases
B. It usually complicates anterior MI within the first 12 hours
C. The posteromedial papillary muscle is involved much more often than the anterolateral
D. The anterolateral papillary muscle is involved 6–10 times more often
E. Right heart catheterization shows an oxygen step-up in the right ventricle

**11.** A 61-year-old man with chronic severe aortic regurgitation has a low-pitched mid-diastolic rumble at the apex with no opening snap. Which response to inhaled amyl nitrite would support an Austin Flint murmur rather than mitral stenosis?

A. The rumble becomes shorter and softer
B. The rumble becomes louder
C. An opening snap appears
D. Presystolic accentuation develops
E. The rumble is unchanged

**12.** A 58-year-old woman with chronic mitral regurgitation has symptoms out of proportion to the moderate regurgitation estimated on transthoracic echo. Which test is most appropriate to quantify regurgitant volume and fraction?

A. Repeat transthoracic echocardiography in 1 year
B. Exercise ECG
C. Coronary CT angiography
D. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging
E. Chest radiograph

**13.** A 29-year-old woman at 30 weeks' gestation has a grade 2/6 midsystolic murmur at the left sternal border. She has no symptoms, and the rest of the exam is normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?

A. Transthoracic echocardiography
B. Reassurance; this is likely a flow murmur from increased blood flow
C. Transesophageal echocardiography
D. Cardiac MRI
E. Start a β-blocker

**14.** A 40-year-old tall man has a decrescendo diastolic murmur heard best along the right sternal border. Which of the following is the most likely mechanism of his aortic regurgitation?

A. Rheumatic leaflet deformity
B. Endocarditis of a bicuspid valve
C. Leaflet prolapse
D. Congenital bicuspid valve without root dilation
E. Aortic root disease such as Marfan syndrome with aneurysm

## Answers

**1. A** — A new murmur after MI calls for TTE. Papillary muscle rupture typically follows an inferior, posterior or lateral MI 2–7 days after presentation, often with hypotension and pulmonary edema. Mechanical complications need aggressive stabilization and prompt surgical referral.
- B: Delay is dangerous with a possible mechanical complication.
- C: Stress testing is contraindicated with acute decompensation.
- D: This is an emergency.
- E: Medication doesn't define the lesion, which needs surgery.
Source: Ch 0044, Early Systolic Murmurs

**2. D** — An isolated grade 1–2 midsystolic murmur without symptoms or signs of heart disease is most often benign. No further evaluation, including TTE, is needed.
- A: TTE isn't recommended for this group.
- B: Advanced imaging adds nothing here.
- C: Stress testing adds nothing here.
- E: TEE adds nothing here.
Source: Ch 0044, Midsystolic Murmurs; Echocardiography

**3. B** — Echocardiography is indicated for midsystolic murmurs of grade 3 or louder. A basal crescendo-decrescendo murmur radiating to the carotids suggests AS.
- A: Grade ≥3 warrants imaging even without symptoms.
- C: Angiography isn't the initial test.
- D: Waiting 5 years is inappropriate for a grade 3 murmur.
- E: There's no indication for a vasodilator.
Source: Ch 0044, Echocardiography

**4. E** — Echocardiography is indicated for any diastolic murmur. This one is typical of chronic AR.
- A: Diastolic murmurs are never assumed benign.
- B: Stress testing isn't the indicated evaluation.
- C: A radiograph doesn't assess valve function.
- D: Delay isn't appropriate.
Source: Ch 0044, Early Diastolic Murmurs; Echocardiography

**5. C** — A murmur with LVH and a brisk carotid upstroke suggests HOCM. Lowering preload (standing, Valsalva) increases outflow obstruction and murmur intensity.
- A: Squatting raises preload and afterload, softening the murmur.
- B: Leg raising raises preload, softening the murmur.
- D: Raising afterload softens it or leaves it unchanged.
- E: A vasopressor raises afterload, softening it or leaving it unchanged.
Source: Ch 0044, Midsystolic Murmurs (HOCM); Changes in Venous Return

**6. D** — The HOCM murmur increases with Valsalva. LVH and no click favor HOCM over MVP, and the diagnosis is confirmed by TTE.
- A: Catheterization isn't needed for diagnosis.
- B: A radiograph doesn't diagnose HOCM.
- C: Holter assesses arrhythmia, not anatomy.
- E: An exercise test doesn't confirm the structural diagnosis.
Source: Ch 0044, Midsystolic Murmurs (HOCM)

**7. D** — A continuous venous hum in healthy children, heard in the right supraclavicular fossa and abolished by jugular pressure or head turning, is benign. Continuous murmurs from a venous hum don't require echocardiography.
- A: Echo isn't needed for a venous hum.
- B: MRI isn't needed.
- C: A PDA murmur is heard lateral to the upper left sternal border and isn't abolished by jugular compression.
- E: Neck imaging isn't indicated.
Source: Ch 0044, Continuous Murmurs; Echocardiography

**8. B** — LV outflow obstruction murmurs such as AS get louder in the beat after a premature beat (more filling plus postextrasystolic potentiation). The MR murmur doesn't change. This helps when an AS murmur radiates to the apex (Gallavardin effect).
- A: Chronic MR intensity stays constant after a PVC.
- C: The post-PVC behavior described is specific to outflow obstruction.
- D: TR is right-sided and louder with inspiration.
- E: MVP changes with preload maneuvers, not after a PVC.
Source: Ch 0044, Post-Premature Ventricular Contraction

**9. E** — Fixed S2 splitting with a grade 2–3 midsystolic murmur at the mid or upper left sternal border indicates an ASD. TTE is indicated for a grade 2–3 murmur with other signs of heart disease.
- A: Fixed splitting is abnormal.
- B: Delay isn't appropriate.
- C: Catheterization isn't the initial test.
- D: Stress testing doesn't define a shunt.
Source: Ch 0044, Midsystolic Murmurs (ASD); Dynamic Auscultation

**10. C** — The posteromedial papillary muscle is involved 6–10 times more often than the anterolateral. Rupture usually follows inferior, posterior or lateral MI at 2–7 days, and a murmur may be absent in up to 50%.
- A: A thrill in nearly all cases describes ventricular septal rupture.
- B: Rupture typically occurs at 2–7 days, mainly after nonanterior MI.
- D: It's the posteromedial muscle that predominates.
- E: An O2 step-up indicates septal rupture; MR shows a tall v wave.
Source: Ch 0044, Early Systolic Murmurs

**11. A** — Lowering afterload with amyl nitrite shortens and softens the Austin Flint murmur of severe AR. The MS rumble stays the same or gets louder because of increased cardiac output and mitral flow.
- B: Getting louder fits MS.
- C: Austin Flint lacks an opening snap; the drug doesn't create one.
- D: Presystolic accentuation is an MS feature in sinus rhythm.
- E: Staying unchanged fits MS.
Source: Ch 0044, Early Diastolic Murmurs; Alterations of Systemic Vascular Resistance

**12. D** — Cardiac MRI quantifies regurgitant fraction and volume. It has largely replaced catheterization when clinical and echo findings disagree in regurgitant valve disease.
- A: Delay leaves the discrepancy unresolved.
- B: An exercise ECG doesn't quantify regurgitation.
- C: Coronary CTA assesses coronaries before surgery.
- E: A radiograph doesn't quantify regurgitation.
Source: Ch 0044, Other Cardiac Testing

**13. B** — A grade 1–2 midsystolic murmur at the left sternal border is common in pregnancy from accelerated flow. Without symptoms or signs of heart disease, no echocardiography is needed.
- A: TTE isn't needed for an isolated benign murmur.
- C: TEE isn't needed.
- D: MRI isn't needed.
- E: There's no indication for a β-blocker.
Source: Ch 0044, Midsystolic Murmurs; Echocardiography

**14. E** — When AR comes from aortic root disease (Marfan syndrome with aneurysm, annuloaortic ectasia, ankylosing spondylitis, dissection), the diastolic murmur may radiate along the right sternal border.
- A: Primary valve disease radiates along the left sternal border.
- B: Endocarditis is also primary valve disease.
- C: Prolapse is also primary valve disease.
- D: A bicuspid valve is also primary valve disease.
Source: Ch 0044, Early Diastolic Murmurs
