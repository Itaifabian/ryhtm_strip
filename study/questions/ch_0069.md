# Ch 69 — Bleeding and Thrombosis: practice questions

**1.** A 19-year-old woman has had heavy menstrual periods since menarche, changing pads more than hourly and passing large clots. She had prolonged bleeding after a wisdom tooth extraction and frequent gum bleeding after dental cleanings. Platelet count and PT are normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next evaluation?

A. Von Willebrand factor assays (with factor VIII)
B. Factor XIII clot solubility test only
C. Bleeding time
D. Thrombophilia panel
E. Bone marrow biopsy

**2.** A 45-year-old man is scheduled for elective hernia repair. He has had several prior surgeries and dental extractions without excess bleeding and no family history of bleeding. Which preoperative coagulation testing is supported?

A. aPTT and PT
B. Bleeding time
C. No routine aPTT testing based on a negative bleeding history
D. PFA-100
E. Factor VIII and IX levels

**3.** A 52-year-old woman has an isolated prolonged aPTT found before surgery. She has never had abnormal bleeding despite childbirth and a hysterectomy, and has no history of thrombosis or pregnancy loss. Mixing corrects the aPTT. Which deficiency is most likely?

A. Factor VIII
B. Factor IX
C. Factor VII
D. Fibrinogen
E. Factor XII

**4.** A 31-year-old woman with three first-trimester pregnancy losses has an isolated prolonged aPTT that doesn't correct with 1:1 mixing. She has no bleeding symptoms. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Factor VIII inhibitor (Bethesda) titer and recombinant factor VIIa
B. Confirm a lupus anticoagulant (e.g., dilute Russell viper venom time with phospholipid correction) and test for antiphospholipid antibodies
C. Factor XII level
D. Vitamin K
E. Fresh frozen plasma

**5.** A 74-year-old man with no prior bleeding history develops extensive bruising and a large thigh hematoma. The aPTT is prolonged. On mixing with normal plasma, the aPTT corrects immediately but becomes prolonged again after incubation at 37°C. What is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Lupus anticoagulant
B. Mild hemophilia A
C. Factor XII deficiency
D. Acquired factor VIII inhibitor
E. Vitamin K deficiency

**6.** A 68-year-old man with poor oral intake has received broad-spectrum antibiotics for 10 days in the ICU. His PT is prolonged and the aPTT is normal. Which of the following is the most likely explanation?

A. Early vitamin K deficiency
B. Hemophilia B
C. Lupus anticoagulant
D. Factor XIII deficiency
E. Von Willebrand disease

**7.** A hospitalized patient's coagulation sample, drawn from a central line kept open with heparin flushes, shows a markedly prolonged thrombin time with a normal reptilase time. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Fibrinogen concentrate
B. Diagnose dysfibrinogenemia
C. Redraw the sample peripherally to exclude heparin contamination
D. Start cryoprecipitate
E. Order a lupus anticoagulant panel

**8.** A 38-year-old man completes 6 months of anticoagulation for an unprovoked DVT. His physician is considering a thrombophilia evaluation that would influence the decision to extend therapy. When should functional assays (antithrombin, protein C, protein S) be drawn?

A. During the acute thrombosis, before anticoagulation
B. While on warfarin
C. While on heparin
D. Within 24 hours of stopping a DOAC
E. In steady state, at least 3 weeks after stopping anticoagulation

**9.** A 64-year-old woman with chronic ITP has a stable platelet count of 62,000/µL and no bleeding history. She needs an elective colonoscopy with possible polypectomy. Regarding her platelet count, which statement is most accurate?

A. Most procedures can be performed at a platelet count of 50,000/µL or greater
B. Platelet transfusion to >150,000/µL is required
C. Spontaneous bleeding is likely at this count
D. The procedure must be canceled
E. Bleeding time should guide the decision

**10.** A 3-year-old boy has recurrent painful swollen knees and ankles after minor falls, and a large muscle hematoma after an immunization. His maternal uncle had similar problems. The aPTT is prolonged; PT and platelets are normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next test?

A. Factor VII level
B. Platelet aggregometry
C. Bleeding time
D. Factor VIII and IX assays
E. Thrombin time

**11.** A 60-year-old man takes daily aspirin for primary prevention and is scheduled for a procedure with high bleeding risk. His surgeon asks how long the antiplatelet effect lasts. Which statement is correct?

A. Aspirin's effect lasts only 6 hours
B. Aspirin irreversibly inhibits platelets, but platelet function returns to near-normal 2–3 days after the last dose
C. Aspirin effect lasts 30 days
D. Aspirin has no effect on primary hemostasis
E. Aspirin's effect is reversed when the drug is removed, like other NSAIDs

**12.** A 55-year-old man with polycythemia (hematocrit 63%) has a prolonged PT and aPTT on routine labs but no bleeding history. Which of the following best explains the results?

A. Disseminated intravascular coagulation
B. Liver failure
C. Artifact from a reduced plasma-to-citrate ratio at hematocrit >55%
D. Factor X deficiency
E. Lupus anticoagulant

**13.** A child has lifelong mucocutaneous bleeding with a normal platelet count. Platelet aggregation studies show absent aggregation to all agonists except ristocetin. Which defect is most likely?

A. Glycoprotein Ib-IX-V deficiency (Bernard-Soulier)
B. Von Willebrand factor deficiency
C. Factor VIII deficiency
D. Storage pool disease from aspirin
E. Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa deficiency (Glanzmann thrombasthenia)

## Answers

**1. A** — Mucosal bleeding (heavy menses from menarche, dental bleeding, gum bleeding) suggests a primary hemostatic disorder, most commonly VWD. Evaluate with VWF assays.
- B: Factor XIII deficiency doesn't explain the mucosal pattern as well.
- C: Bleeding time doesn't predict bleeding and isn't recommended.
- D: Thrombophilia testing is for clotting.
- E: Marrow isn't indicated with a normal platelet count.
Source: Ch 0069, History of Bleeding; Measures of Platelet Function

**2. C** — A bleeding history is the most important predictor of bleeding risk. Studies haven't confirmed the usefulness of a preoperative aPTT in patients with a negative bleeding history.
- A: Routine aPTT isn't supported.
- B: Bleeding time isn't recommended.
- D: PFA-100 isn't validated to predict bleeding.
- E: Factor assays aren't indicated without a suspicious history.
Source: Ch 0069, Laboratory Evaluation

**3. E** — An isolated prolonged aPTT that corrects with mixing, without clinical bleeding, fits deficiency of factor XII, prekallikrein or HMWK. These don't cause bleeding.
- A: Factor VIII deficiency causes bleeding.
- B: Factor IX deficiency causes bleeding.
- C: Factor VII prolongs the PT.
- D: Fibrinogen deficiency prolongs both PT and aPTT.
Source: Ch 0069, Table 69-3

**4. B** — A prolonged aPTT that doesn't correct on mixing, with pregnancy loss and no bleeding, suggests a lupus anticoagulant. Confirmation requires a prolonged phospholipid-dependent test, no correction on mixing, and correction with added phospholipid, plus antiphospholipid antibody testing.
- A: An inhibitor work-up is for bleeding patients.
- C: Factor deficiencies correct on mixing.
- D: Vitamin K doesn't apply.
- E: FFP isn't indicated.
Source: Ch 0069, Mixing Studies; Testing for Antiphospholipid Antibodies

**5. D** — Acquired neutralizing factor VIII antibodies may correct immediately on mixing but prolong with incubation at 37°C. Muscle and soft tissue bleeding is common in acquired factor VIII deficiency.
- A: Lupus anticoagulant shows no correction and doesn't cause bleeding.
- B: Mild hemophilia A would correct and stay corrected, and would be lifelong.
- C: Factor XII deficiency causes no bleeding.
- E: Vitamin K deficiency prolongs the PT.
Source: Ch 0069, Mixing Studies; History of Bleeding

**6. A** — An isolated prolonged PT is seen with factor VII deficiency, early vitamin K deficiency, or warfarin. Poor intake plus antibiotics favors vitamin K deficiency, because factor VII has the shortest half-life.
- B: Hemophilia B prolongs the aPTT.
- C: Lupus anticoagulant usually prolongs the aPTT.
- D: Factor XIII deficiency has a normal PT and aPTT.
- E: VWD has a normal PT.
Source: Ch 0069, Table 69-3; Underlying Systemic Diseases

**7. C** — The thrombin time, but not the reptilase time, is prolonged by heparin. A sample drawn from a heparinized line should be redrawn to exclude contamination.
- A: Low fibrinogen prolongs both tests.
- B: Dysfibrinogenemia prolongs both tests.
- D: Cryoprecipitate isn't indicated.
- E: A lupus anticoagulant panel isn't indicated.
Source: Ch 0069, Other Coagulation Tests

**8. E** — Thrombophilia testing should be done in steady state, remote from the acute event. Acute thrombosis and heparin lower antithrombin, warfarin lowers proteins C and S, and DOACs interfere with functional assays. Stop after 3–6 months of treatment and test at least 3 weeks later.
- A: Acute thrombosis distorts the results.
- B: Warfarin distorts the results.
- C: Heparin distorts the results.
- D: 24 hours off a DOAC is too soon.
Source: Ch 0069, Laboratory Testing for Thrombophilia

**9. A** — Bleeding rarely occurs with isolated thrombocytopenia above 50,000/µL, and most procedures can be performed at ≥50,000/µL.
- B: Transfusion to normal isn't needed.
- C: Spontaneous bleeding usually needs counts <10,000–20,000.
- D: The procedure doesn't need to be canceled.
- E: Bleeding time isn't predictive.
Source: Ch 0069, Underlying Systemic Diseases (platelets)

**10. D** — Spontaneous hemarthroses and muscle hematomas are the hallmark of moderate–severe factor VIII or IX deficiency. With an X-linked pattern and isolated aPTT prolongation, specific factor assays are needed.
- A: Factor VII prolongs the PT.
- B: Platelet defects cause mucosal bleeding.
- C: Bleeding time isn't recommended.
- E: Thrombin time tests fibrinogen.
Source: Ch 0069, History of Bleeding; Specific Factor Assays

**11. B** — Aspirin's effect lasts for the life of the platelet, but with typical turnover, function returns to near-normal within 2–3 days. Other NSAIDs are reversible.
- A: The effect lasts much longer.
- C: The effect resolves much sooner.
- D: Aspirin impairs primary hemostasis.
- E: That reversible pattern describes other NSAIDs.
Source: Ch 0069, Prohemorrhagic Effects of Medications

**12. C** — A hematocrit above 55% lowers the plasma-to-anticoagulant ratio and can falsely prolong clotting times. Abnormal results without a bleeding history often normalize with proper sampling.
- A: Nothing suggests DIC.
- B: Nothing suggests liver failure.
- D: An isolated factor deficiency isn't suggested.
- E: Lupus anticoagulant wouldn't explain this pattern as well.
Source: Ch 0069, Laboratory Evaluation

**13. E** — Glanzmann thrombasthenia (absent or dysfunctional GpIIb/IIIa) is a platelet aggregation defect. Aggregation is absent to all agonists except ristocetin, which depends on GpIb and VWF.
- A: Bernard-Soulier is an adhesion defect (GpIb-IX-V).
- B: VWD is also an adhesion defect.
- C: Factor VIII affects secondary hemostasis.
- D: Aspirin doesn't abolish aggregation to all agonists.
Source: Ch 0069, Table 69-1; Platelet Plug Formation
