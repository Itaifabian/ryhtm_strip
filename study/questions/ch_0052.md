# Ch 52 — Jaundice: practice questions

**1.** A 24-year-old man notices yellow eyes after 3 days of fasting during a religious holiday. Total bilirubin is 3.2 mg/dL with direct bilirubin 0.3 mg/dL. CBC, reticulocyte count, LDH, ALT, AST and alkaline phosphatase are normal. He takes no medications. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?

A. Liver biopsy
B. Reassurance; no treatment is needed
C. MRCP
D. Start phenobarbital
E. Hemolysis workup with Coombs test and haptoglobin, then splenectomy referral

**2.** A 62-year-old woman has jaundice and pruritus. Total bilirubin is 6.8 mg/dL (mostly direct), alkaline phosphatase is 690 U/L, and ALT is 110 U/L. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Liver biopsy
B. ERCP
C. Right upper quadrant ultrasound
D. Hepatitis serologies only
E. Antimitochondrial antibody only

**3.** A 70-year-old man has painless jaundice. Ultrasound shows dilated intra- and extrahepatic bile ducts, but the distal common bile duct isn't seen well because of bowel gas. No stone or mass is identified. Which of the following is the most appropriate next diagnostic test in most cases?

A. MRCP
B. Repeat ultrasound in 3 months
C. Liver biopsy
D. HIDA scan
E. Antimitochondrial antibody

**4.** A 29-year-old woman has 5 days of nausea, malaise and jaundice. ALT is 2,400 U/L, AST 1,900 U/L, alkaline phosphatase 160 U/L, and bilirubin 7 mg/dL. Which set of tests is most appropriate for acute viral hepatitis?

A. Hepatitis C antibody alone
B. Hepatitis B surface antibody and hepatitis A IgG
C. Antimitochondrial antibody and MRCP
D. Hepatitis A IgM, hepatitis B surface antigen and core IgM, hepatitis C RNA, and an acetaminophen level
E. Liver biopsy

**5.** A 48-year-old man who drinks heavily has jaundice, fever and tender hepatomegaly. AST is 260 U/L, ALT is 105 U/L, and bilirubin is 9 mg/dL. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Acute hepatitis A
B. Acetaminophen hepatotoxicity
C. Alcoholic hepatitis
D. Ischemic hepatitis
E. Acute hepatitis C

**6.** A 54-year-old woman has 1 year of fatigue and pruritus, and is now jaundiced. Alkaline phosphatase is 520 U/L with modestly elevated aminotransferases. Ultrasound shows no biliary dilation. She takes no medications. Which of the following is the most appropriate next test?

A. ERCP
B. Antimitochondrial antibody
C. CT angiography
D. Ceruloplasmin
E. Serum protein electrophoresis alone

**7.** A 32-year-old man with ulcerative colitis has a persistently elevated alkaline phosphatase and new mild jaundice. Ultrasound shows no ductal dilation. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Antimitochondrial antibody
B. Repeat ultrasound
C. Liver biopsy as the first test
D. Ceruloplasmin
E. MRCP

**8.** A 66-year-old man with jaundice has a prothrombin time of 22 seconds. After parenteral vitamin K, the PT doesn't change. What does this most likely indicate?

A. Severe hepatocellular injury
B. Vitamin K malabsorption from prolonged cholestasis
C. Laboratory error
D. Warfarin ingestion
E. Hemolysis

**9.** A 35-year-old woman with no prior liver disease took a large amount of acetaminophen 3 days ago. She is now jaundiced and confused with asterixis, and her INR is 4.1. Which of the following is the most appropriate step?

A. Outpatient follow-up in 1 week
B. MRCP
C. Liver biopsy before any other step
D. Urgent liver transplant evaluation
E. Start glucocorticoids

**10.** A 50-year-old man is recovering from an episode of obstructive jaundice that was relieved by stone extraction. His bilirubin is falling much more slowly than expected, and his urine is now negative for bilirubin even though the serum direct fraction is still high. What best explains these findings?

A. New hemolysis
B. Gilbert syndrome
C. Dubin-Johnson syndrome
D. Recurrent stone obstruction
E. Delta bilirubin (conjugated bilirubin covalently bound to albumin)

**11.** A 30-year-old vegetarian woman with diabetes notices yellow palms and soles. Her sclerae are white, and bilirubin and liver tests are normal. Which of the following is the most likely cause?

A. Gilbert syndrome
B. Carotenoderma
C. Early hepatitis
D. Hemolysis
E. Dubin-Johnson syndrome

**12.** A 27-year-old woman with sickle cell disease and a usual bilirubin of 3 mg/dL presents with RUQ pain and a bilirubin of 9 mg/dL, now mostly direct, with an elevated alkaline phosphatase. Which of the following is the most important diagnosis to evaluate?

A. Gilbert syndrome
B. Crigler-Najjar syndrome
C. Rotor syndrome
D. Worsening hemolysis alone
E. Choledocholithiasis from pigment gallstones

**13.** A 22-year-old man has lifelong jaundice with unconjugated bilirubin levels of 12–18 mg/dL, normal liver enzymes, and no hemolysis. Genetic testing shows a UDPGT mutation with markedly reduced activity. Which medication can lower his bilirubin?

A. Phenobarbital
B. Rifampin
C. Probenecid
D. Ursodeoxycholic acid
E. Prednisone

## Answers

**1. B** — Mild indirect hyperbilirubinemia (<6 mg/dL) triggered by fasting, with no hemolysis and normal liver tests, is typical of Gilbert syndrome. It affects 3–7% of the population and is of no clinical consequence.
- A: Biopsy is unnecessary in a benign condition.
- C: MRCP is unnecessary in a benign condition.
- D: Phenobarbital is used in Crigler-Najjar type II.
- E: Hemolysis markers are normal.
Source: Ch 0052, Unconjugated Hyperbilirubinemia; Table 52-1

**2. C** — With a cholestatic pattern (ALP out of proportion to aminotransferases), the first step is to separate intra- from extrahepatic cholestasis by ultrasound, which detects biliary dilation with high sensitivity and specificity.
- A: Biopsy is for intrahepatic cholestasis after serology.
- B: ERCP follows imaging that suggests obstruction.
- D: Serologies alone skip the key imaging step.
- E: AMA alone skips the key imaging step.
Source: Ch 0052, Cholestatic Conditions; Fig 52-1

**3. A** — Ultrasound rarely identifies the site or cause of obstruction, and the distal CBD is hard to see. MRCP has replaced ERCP as the initial diagnostic test in most cases; CT and EUS are alternatives.
- B: Delay risks missing a malignancy.
- C: Biopsy is for intrahepatic cholestasis.
- D: HIDA assesses cholecystitis.
- E: AMA is for intrahepatic cholestasis (PBC).
Source: Ch 0052, Cholestatic Conditions

**4. D** — For hepatocellular jaundice, test for acute viral hepatitis with HAV IgM, HBsAg and anti-HBc IgM, and HCV RNA. HCV antibody can take up to 6 weeks to appear. Add a toxicology screen with acetaminophen level.
- A: HCV antibody may be negative early.
- B: These markers reflect immunity, not acute infection.
- C: These are cholestatic tests.
- E: Serologic testing comes first.
Source: Ch 0052, Hepatocellular Conditions; Fig 52-1

**5. C** — Alcoholic hepatitis typically shows an AST:ALT ratio of at least 2:1, with AST rarely above 300 U/L.
- A: Jaundiced viral hepatitis usually has aminotransferases >500 with ALT ≥ AST.
- B: Toxic injury causing jaundice shows aminotransferases >500.
- D: Ischemic hepatitis causes dramatic aminotransferase elevation.
- E: Same pattern as A.
Source: Ch 0052, Hepatocellular Conditions

**6. B** — Intrahepatic cholestasis (no ductal dilation) in a middle-aged woman suggests primary biliary cholangitis, diagnosed by antimitochondrial antibody (positive in 95%).
- A: ERCP is for extrahepatic obstruction.
- C: CT angiography isn't indicated.
- D: Ceruloplasmin screens for Wilson disease in younger patients with hepatocellular injury.
- E: SPEP alone isn't diagnostic.
Source: Ch 0052, Cholestatic Conditions

**7. E** — PSC is diagnosed by cholangiography (MRCP) showing pathognomonic segmental strictures. About 75% of PSC patients have IBD. Ultrasound may miss it because scarring prevents duct dilation.
- A: AMA tests for PBC.
- B: Repeat ultrasound can be falsely negative in PSC.
- C: Cholangiography is the diagnostic test.
- D: Ceruloplasmin tests for Wilson disease.
Source: Ch 0052, Cholestatic Conditions

**8. A** — A prolonged PT reflects vitamin K deficiency or hepatocellular dysfunction. Failure to correct with parenteral vitamin K indicates severe hepatocellular injury.
- B: Malabsorption-related deficiency would correct with parenteral vitamin K.
- C: Nothing suggests a lab error.
- D: Warfarin effect would also correct with vitamin K.
- E: Hemolysis doesn't prolong the PT.
Source: Ch 0052, Laboratory Tests

**9. D** — Encephalopathy and coagulopathy in a jaundiced patient without preexisting liver disease signify acute liver failure, which warrants urgent transplant evaluation.
- A: Delay is dangerous.
- B: MRCP doesn't address hepatocellular failure.
- C: Biopsy doesn't take priority.
- E: Steroids aren't indicated for acetaminophen toxicity.
Source: Ch 0052, Global Considerations; Table 52-2

**10. E** — Delta bilirubin is conjugated bilirubin covalently bound to albumin. Its half-life matches albumin's (18–20 days), so levels fall slowly, and it isn't filtered by the glomerulus, so urine bilirubin can be negative.
- A: Hemolysis raises indirect bilirubin.
- B: Gilbert causes unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia.
- C: Dubin-Johnson is inherited and lifelong.
- D: Recurrent obstruction would raise enzymes and bilirubin again.
Source: Ch 0052, Measurement of Serum Bilirubin; Measurement of Urine Bilirubin

**11. B** — Carotenoderma concentrates on the palms, soles, forehead and nasolabial folds and spares the sclerae. It's associated with high carotene intake, diabetes, hypothyroidism and anorexia.
- A: Gilbert raises bilirubin and causes scleral icterus.
- C: Hepatitis raises bilirubin and causes scleral icterus.
- D: Hemolysis raises bilirubin and causes scleral icterus.
- E: Dubin-Johnson raises bilirubin and causes scleral icterus.
Source: Ch 0052, introduction

**12. E** — Chronic hemolysis rarely raises bilirubin above 5 mg/dL, and pigment gallstones are common. A rise in direct bilirubin with ALP elevation and pain suggests choledocholithiasis.
- A: Gilbert causes mild indirect hyperbilirubinemia.
- B: Crigler-Najjar causes indirect hyperbilirubinemia.
- C: Rotor causes isolated direct hyperbilirubinemia without pain or ALP rise.
- D: Hemolysis raises indirect, not direct, bilirubin.
Source: Ch 0052, Unconjugated Hyperbilirubinemia

**13. A** — In Crigler-Najjar type II (UDPGT ≤10%, bilirubin 6–25 mg/dL, survival into adulthood), phenobarbital induces UDPGT and lowers serum bilirubin.
- B: Rifampin decreases hepatic bilirubin uptake.
- C: Probenecid also decreases uptake.
- D: Ursodeoxycholic acid isn't the source's therapy.
- E: Prednisone isn't the source's therapy.
Source: Ch 0052, Unconjugated Hyperbilirubinemia
