# Ch 24 — Dizziness and Vertigo: practice questions

**1.** A 63-year-old woman has had 2 weeks of brief spinning sensations, each lasting about 15 seconds. They occur when she rolls over in bed, lies down, or looks up at a high shelf. She has no hearing loss, tinnitus, or other neurologic symptoms, and orthostatic vital signs are normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Brain MRI
B. Start meclizine daily
C. Dix-Hallpike maneuver
D. Audiometry
E. Start a diuretic and sodium restriction

**2.** A 58-year-old man has brief positional vertigo. With his head turned 45 degrees to the right and lowered into the head-hanging position, he develops transient upbeating-torsional nystagmus with the upper poles of the eyes beating toward the right ear, along with vertigo. Which of the following is the most appropriate treatment?

A. Oral glucocorticoids
B. Daily vestibular suppressant medication
C. Valacyclovir
D. Referral for vestibular nerve section
E. Epley repositioning maneuver for the right posterior canal

**3.** A 71-year-old man with hypertension, diabetes and atrial fibrillation presents with 10 hours of continuous vertigo, nausea and vomiting. He has spontaneous horizontal nystagmus. The head impulse test is normal in both directions. He has no hearing loss. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Brain MRI
B. Discharge with meclizine
C. Epley maneuver
D. Oral glucocorticoids for vestibular neuritis
E. Outpatient audiometry

**4.** A 42-year-old woman was diagnosed with vestibular neuritis 9 days ago. The acute vertigo has resolved, but she still feels unsteady with head movement. She has stayed in bed and continues taking a vestibular suppressant three times daily. Neurologic examination is otherwise normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?

A. Increase the suppressant dose
B. Add oral acyclovir
C. Order brain MRI
D. Stop the suppressant, resume normal activity and begin vestibular rehabilitation
E. Add a benzodiazepine at night

**5.** A 45-year-old woman has recurrent attacks of vertigo lasting several hours, with fullness and roaring tinnitus in the left ear. Audiometry during an attack shows asymmetric low-frequency sensorineural hearing loss on the left that improves between attacks. Neurologic examination is normal. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial treatment?

A. Intratympanic gentamicin
B. Diuretic therapy and sodium restriction
C. Labyrinthectomy
D. Epley maneuver
E. Migraine prophylaxis

**6.** A 54-year-old man has gradually worsening hearing in his right ear over 18 months. He has no vertigo. Audiometry shows right-sided sensorineural hearing loss. The head impulse test shows a catch-up saccade when the head is turned to the right. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

A. Reassurance and repeat audiometry in 1 year
B. Noncontrast head CT
C. MRI of the internal auditory canals with gadolinium
D. Vestibular suppressant therapy
E. Hearing aid fitting without further testing

**7.** A 50-year-old woman presents with acute vertigo. Which of the following examination findings most strongly indicates a central rather than peripheral cause?

A. Horizontal nystagmus that changes direction with the direction of gaze
B. Unidirectional horizontal nystagmus that is suppressed by visual fixation
C. Catch-up saccade after head rotation toward one side
D. Transient upbeat-torsional nystagmus on Dix-Hallpike testing
E. Unilateral hearing loss with ear fullness

**8.** A 67-year-old man treated with gentamicin for endocarditis 2 months ago now has unsteadiness, especially walking at night, and a sensation that the visual scene bounces when he walks or rides in a car. He has no vertigo. Head impulse testing shows catch-up saccades in both directions, dynamic visual acuity drops by three lines, and Romberg testing is positive. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?

A. Daily meclizine
B. Epley maneuver
C. Oral glucocorticoids
D. Diuretic and sodium restriction
E. Vestibular rehabilitation therapy

**9.** A 38-year-old woman had vestibular neuritis 8 months ago. Since then she has had fluctuating dizziness and unsteadiness that is present at rest, worse when standing, and especially bad in supermarkets and while watching action movies. Neuro-otologic examination and vestibular testing show only a compensated vestibular deficit. She is anxious about going out. Which of the following is the most appropriate treatment?

A. Long-term vestibular suppressant
B. Repeat Epley maneuvers
C. Intratympanic glucocorticoids
D. An SSRI with cognitive-behavioral therapy and vestibular rehabilitation
E. Vestibular nerve section

**10.** A 36-year-old woman with a long history of migraine headaches has recurrent episodes of vertigo lasting 30 minutes to several hours, usually without headache. During episodes she has photophobia and sensitivity to visual motion. Hearing and audiometry are normal, and neurologic examination between episodes is normal. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Ménière disease
B. Vestibular migraine
C. Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo
D. Vestibular schwannoma
E. Bilateral vestibular hypofunction

**11.** A 60-year-old man has 1 day of severe continuous vertigo with nausea. Examination shows unidirectional horizontal nystagmus suppressed by fixation, an abnormal head impulse test to the left, vesicles in the left external auditory canal, and left facial weakness. Which of the following is the most appropriate addition to his treatment?

A. No antiviral; antivirals have no proven benefit in acute vestibular syndromes
B. Long-term vestibular suppressant therapy
C. Antiviral therapy
D. Epley maneuver
E. Diuretic and sodium restriction

**12.** A 49-year-old man has a slowly progressive left-sided sensorineural hearing loss due to a cerebellopontine angle tumor. Which of the following best explains why he doesn't have vertigo?

A. The gradual vestibular deficit is compensated centrally as it develops
B. The tumor affects only the cochlear nerve
C. Vertigo occurs only with bilateral lesions
D. The tumor causes bilateral symmetric vestibular loss
E. Visual fixation permanently suppresses vertigo in all unilateral lesions

## Answers

**1. C** — Brief positional vertigo triggered by rolling over in bed is typical of BPPV. All patients with positionally provoked episodic dizziness should have the Dix-Hallpike maneuver.
- A: Nothing suggests a central cause.
- B: Suppressants don't treat BPPV, and treatment should target the diagnosis.
- D: She has no aural symptoms.
- E: Diuretics and sodium restriction are for Ménière disease.
Source: Ch 0024, Positioning Maneuvers; Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo

**2. E** — Upbeat-torsional nystagmus toward the lower (right) ear on Dix-Hallpike confirms right posterior canal BPPV, which is treated with the Epley maneuver.
- A: Glucocorticoids have no role in BPPV.
- B: Suppressants don't remove otoconia.
- C: Antivirals are for herpes zoster oticus.
- D: Ablative surgery is seldom needed even in Ménière disease and isn't BPPV therapy.
Source: Ch 0024, Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo; Fig 24-1

**3. A** — In acute prolonged vertigo, a normal head impulse test argues against a peripheral lesion and suggests a central cause. An older patient with vascular risk factors needs evaluation for stroke, and suspected central lesions need brain MRI.
- B: Discharging a possible cerebellar stroke is dangerous.
- C: Epley treats BPPV, not continuous vertigo.
- D: A normal head impulse test makes vestibular neuritis unlikely.
- E: Outpatient audiometry would delay evaluation of a possible stroke.
Source: Ch 0024, Acute Prolonged Vertigo; Table 24-1; Central Vestibular Disorders

**4. D** — Vestibular suppressants should be avoided after the first several days because they impede central compensation. Patients should resume activity as soon as possible, and vestibular rehabilitation may speed improvement.
- A: Higher doses further delay compensation.
- B: Antivirals have no proven benefit without herpes zoster oticus.
- C: The course and normal neurologic exam fit a resolving peripheral lesion.
- E: Benzodiazepines are suppressants with the same drawback.
Source: Ch 0024, Acute Prolonged Vertigo: Treatment

**5. B** — Vertigo, aural fullness and fluctuating low-frequency hearing loss indicate Ménière disease. Diuretics and sodium restriction are the usual initial treatment, with ENT referral.
- A: Intratympanic gentamicin is considered if attacks persist.
- C: Ablative procedures are seldom required.
- D: Epley treats BPPV.
- E: Migraine prophylaxis treats vestibular migraine, which lacks low-frequency hearing loss.
Source: Ch 0024, Ménière's Disease

**6. C** — Unexplained unilateral sensorineural hearing loss or vestibular hypofunction requires MRI of the internal auditory canals with gadolinium to exclude vestibular schwannoma.
- A: Watchful waiting would miss a tumor.
- B: Noncontrast CT doesn't adequately image the internal auditory canal.
- D: He has no vertigo to suppress.
- E: The cause must be found first.
Source: Ch 0024, Ancillary Testing; Vestibular Schwannoma

**7. A** — Nystagmus that changes direction with gaze is due to a central lesion, characteristically of the cerebellar pathways.
- B: Unidirectional, fixation-suppressed nystagmus is peripheral.
- C: A catch-up saccade indicates a peripheral vestibular deficit.
- D: Transient mixed vertical-torsional nystagmus on positioning is BPPV.
- E: Unilateral hearing loss suggests a peripheral cause.
Source: Ch 0024, Table 24-1; Ocular Motility

**8. E** — Gentamicin is the most common iatrogenic cause of bilateral vestibular hypofunction. It causes imbalance worse in the dark, oscillopsia, bilateral head-impulse abnormalities, reduced dynamic visual acuity and a Romberg sign. Treatment is vestibular rehabilitation.
- A: Suppressants increase the imbalance and shouldn't be used.
- B: Epley treats BPPV.
- C: Glucocorticoids don't reverse ototoxicity.
- D: Diuretics and sodium restriction treat Ménière disease.
Source: Ch 0024, Bilateral Vestibular Hypofunction

**9. D** — Persistent postural-perceptual dizziness (≥3 months, worse standing and in complex visual environments, normal or compensated testing) is treated with SSRIs, cognitive-behavioral therapy and vestibular rehabilitation.
- A: Suppressants should generally be avoided.
- B: Nothing suggests BPPV.
- C: Intratympanic therapy is for refractory Ménière disease.
- E: Vestibular testing shows no ongoing primary vestibular disorder.
Source: Ch 0024, Functional Dizziness

**10. B** — Vestibular migraine causes episodic vertigo lasting minutes to hours, often without headache, with photophobia and visual-motion sensitivity in someone with a migraine history.
- A: Ménière disease features low-frequency hearing loss and aural symptoms.
- C: BPPV episodes last under a minute and are positional.
- D: Schwannoma causes progressive unilateral hearing loss, usually without vertigo.
- E: Bilateral loss causes imbalance and oscillopsia without vertigo.
Source: Ch 0024, Vestibular Migraine

**11. C** — Antivirals aren't routinely given for vestibular neuritis but are indicated with evidence of herpes zoster oticus (Ramsay Hunt syndrome), suggested here by ear-canal vesicles and facial weakness.
- A: The "no proven benefit" statement applies to vestibular neuritis without zoster.
- B: Suppressants should be limited to the first few days.
- D: Epley treats BPPV.
- E: Diuretics and sodium restriction are for Ménière disease.
Source: Ch 0024, Acute Prolonged Vertigo: Treatment

**12. A** — Vestibular schwannomas cause slowly progressive unilateral hypofunction that is compensated centrally as it develops, so vertigo is typically absent.
- B: The vestibular examination shows a deficient head impulse response toward the affected side.
- C: Vertigo comes from acute asymmetry; bilateral symmetric loss causes imbalance without vertigo.
- D: A unilateral tumor causes unilateral loss (bilateral tumors occur in NF2).
- E: Fixation suppresses peripheral nystagmus; it doesn't explain the lack of vertigo.
Source: Ch 0024, Vestibular Schwannoma
