# Ch 13 — Palliative and End-of-Life Care (high-yield)

## Framework
- Palliative care fits **any stage** of serious illness and can run alongside curative care. Hospice is palliative care for roughly the last 6 months.
- Four domains: **physical, psychological, social, existential/spiritual**.
- Avoid invasive tests at end of life; skip uncomfortable exam steps that won't change management.
- Screening tools: PHQ-9/PHQ-2 (depression), GAD-7 (anxiety), Edmonton Symptom Assessment Scale.
- Prognosis heuristic: **"Would I be surprised if this patient died in the next 12 months?"** Physicians overestimate survival about 5×, and more so the longer they've known the patient.

## Breaking bad news: P-SPIKES (Table 13-2)
**P**reparation → **S**etting (private, family present, tissues, no beeper) → **P**erception ("What do you understand about your illness?") → **I**nvitation ("How much detail do you want?") → **K**nowledge (warning shot, no information dumping, check understanding) → **E**mpathy ("I imagine this is very hard to hear") → **S**ummary and plan.

## Advance directives
- **Living will** (instructional) vs **health care proxy / durable power of attorney** (a person). A combined form should state which takes precedence if they conflict.
- **POLST** = portable medical orders that follow the patient across settings.
- A directive applies **only once the patient loses capacity**. Review it periodically and with major health changes (Table 13-3).
- The Supreme Court requires "clear and convincing evidence" of an incompetent patient's wishes. A clear directive should be honored even if it isn't on an official form.
- Surrogate standards: **substituted judgment** (what the patient would choose; proxies are often inaccurate) > **best interests** (weigh benefits against burdens). The ordinary/extraordinary distinction is rejected.

## Hospice (Medicare Part A) [verify: policy details may change]
- **Two physicians certify prognosis ≤6 months** if the disease runs its usual course. Patients who outlive it aren't penalized.
- **Curative therapy for the terminal diagnosis isn't covered**. Treatment of other conditions is. Patients can revoke and re-enroll.
- Per-diem payment covers nursing, aides, chaplain, social work, **bereavement**, equipment and medications.
- Median stay is only **17 days**, so refer early.

## Pain (Fig 13-2 flowchart)
| Severity | Options (source doses) |
|---|---|
| Mild | Acetaminophen (≤4 g/d; **≤2 g/d in cirrhosis**); ibuprofen up to 2400 mg/d (NSAIDs have a ceiling effect; avoid with prior severe GI bleed; add a PPI if mild GERD) |
| Moderate | Codeine 30–60 mg q4–8h; tramadol; **combine with acetaminophen/NSAID** |
| Severe | Morphine 2.5–5 mg PO q3–6h; hydromorphone 1–2 mg; hydrocodone 5–10 mg, plus nonopioids |
| Neuropathic (burning, shock-like: brachial plexus invasion, post-herpetic, post-stroke) | **Gabapentin first-line** 100–300 mg bid–tid, titrated (900–3600 mg/d); ± nortriptyline; pregabalin, duloxetine, venlafaxine; 2nd line topical lidocaine or capsaicin |
| Difficult to control | Specialist consult: nerve block, epidural |

**Opioid rules**
- Give doses **around the clock**, not PRN, plus a **rescue dose ≈20% of the baseline dose** for breakthrough pain.
- **Start short-acting** to learn the 24–48 h requirement, **then convert to extended-release**. Never start with extended-release.
- If pain is still uncontrolled at 24 h: raise the daily dose by **the total rescue doses used**, or by **50% (moderate) / 100% (severe)**.
- Switching opioids → start at **50–75% of the equianalgesic dose** (incomplete cross-tolerance).
- **No ceiling dose**. Addiction and respiratory depression are extremely unlikely in the terminally ill, so don't withhold opioids or give antagonists.
- Rising requirement usually means **disease progression**, not tolerance. Physical dependence ≠ addiction.
- End-of-dose failure on a q12h drug → give it q8h. Predictable incident pain → pre-dose with short-acting.
- Buprenorphine (partial agonist) is **not** for chronic end-of-life pain.
- **Bone pain:** single metastasis → **radiation**; multiple → strontium-89/samarium-153; dexamethasone; bisphosphonates.
- Side effects: **constipation in nearly all, so prevent it**. Nausea and drowsiness usually fade within about 1 week (a prophylactic antiemetic can be stopped at a week).

## Constipation (Table 13-5)
- Opioids → start **senna + docusate** (stimulant + softener) preemptively.
- No BM after several days → **rectal exam** to disimpact and place a suppository.
- Refractory opioid-induced constipation → **methylnaltrexone** (peripheral antagonist; analgesia preserved).
- Impending obstruction → octreotide. Dysmotility → metoclopramide.
- Onset: lactulose 1 day; magnesium 6 h; senna/prune 6–12 h; docusate 1–3 days.

| Anticipatory | Lorazepam; biofeedback |

## Dyspnea (Table 13-6)
- **Oxygen is not better than air** (possibly an "expensive placebo"). Pulse oximetry rarely guides therapy.
- **Low-dose opioids are first-line**. Already on opioids → 30–50% of the baseline dose q4h. Nebulized opioids aren't supported.
- **Benzodiazepines only if anxiety is present**, never first-line.
- Specific causes: diuretic for pulmonary edema; bronchodilators/steroids for COPD; scopolamine for secretions. Sit upright, fan or fresh air.

## Fatigue
Treat reversible causes (anemia, infection, hypothyroid, drugs). Dexamethasone 8 mg/d; megestrol if anorexia; methylphenidate. ECOG 0–4 performance status: 2 = up >50% of the day, 3 = in bed >50%, 4 = bedbound.

## Depression
- About 25% of terminally ill patients have major depression; it's **undertreated**. Diagnose by **mood, hopelessness, anhedonia**, not vegetative symptoms.
- Rule out hypothyroidism, Cushing's, hypercalcemia, and **akathisia** (metoclopramide, prochlorperazine).
- **Prognosis of weeks → psychostimulant** (methylphenidate 2.5–5 mg AM and noon; works in days).
- **Prognosis of months → SSRI at half the usual starting dose** (fluoxetine 10 mg; takes up to 4 weeks). Fluoxetine if fatigued, paroxetine if anxious or sleepless.
- Mirtazapine: sedating, antiemetic, weight gain. **Avoid TCAs and MAOIs** (except TCAs as pain adjuvants). Bupropion lowers the seizure threshold.

## Terminal delirium (Table 13-7)
- Up to 85% of dying cancer patients. Fluctuating consciousness distinguishes it from dementia, depression and anxiety.
- At the first sign, **tell the family to say what they want to say now**.
- Stop unnecessary drugs; treat constipation and urinary retention; keep the environment familiar.
- **Haloperidol first-line** (PO/SC/IV; avoid IM). Olanzapine if longer prognosis. Chlorpromazine if sedation wanted. Add lorazepam only for alcohol or sedative withdrawal.
- Refractory → second neuroleptic → midazolam/propofol sedation. Restraints are a last resort. No LP or neuroimaging near death.

## Last hours (Table 13-9)
| Change | Tell the family | Do |
|---|---|---|
| Anorexia | Dying causes not eating, not the reverse | **No forced feeding** (doesn't prolong life or ease symptoms) |
| Dehydration | No suffering | **IV fluids can worsen dyspnea and edema** |
| Death rattle | Not choking | **Scopolamine**, reposition, **don't suction** |
| Cheyne-Stokes/apnea | Unconscious patients don't feel air hunger | Opioids; O2 unlikely to help |
| Agitation | Doesn't necessarily mean pain | Haloperidol, chlorpromazine, midazolam |
| Dry mouth/eyes | — | Mouth swabs, lip lubricant, artificial tears, nystatin for thrush |
- Home death plan: call **hospice or the on-call doctor, not 911**. The medical examiner isn't needed unless state law requires it or foul play is suspected.

## Withdrawing treatment
- Withholding ≡ withdrawing; both are ethical and legal. Opioids for symptoms ≠ euthanasia.
- Ventilator withdrawal: **stop neuromuscular blockers**, bolus midazolam or lorazepam 2–4 mg + morphine 5–10 mg, then morphine infusion; scopolamine for secretions. Median time to death is 1 h; about 10% survive ≥1 day.
- Futility disputes → negotiation, ethics consultation, process-based policies.
- **Palliative sedation:** last resort for refractory symptoms, by the patient's wishes, not to hasten death.

## PAS/euthanasia request (Table 13-8)
- Passive euthanasia (withdrawing treatment) is legal everywhere. PAS is legal in some US states (≤6-month prognosis + waiting period). Active euthanasia is legal in no US state. [verify: legal status changes]
- Drivers are **depression, hopelessness, loss of autonomy and dignity**, not usually pain.
- Response: **open-ended exploration** ("What makes you want to consider this?"), screen for and treat depression and symptoms, reassure non-abandonment, offer alternatives. Don't voice moral approval or disapproval.

## Classic traps
- O2 for dyspnea in a dying patient with normal oximetry: wrong (opioid).
- PRN-only opioids, or starting a fentanyl patch or ER morphine in an opioid-naive patient: wrong.
- Suctioning a death rattle, or IV fluids or a feeding tube "so she won't starve": wrong.
- Benzodiazepine as first-line for delirium or dyspnea: wrong (haloperidol / opioid).
