# Ch 43 — Edema (high-yield)

## Physiology
- **Body water:** 2/3 intracellular, 1/3 extracellular. Of the extracellular fluid, 1/4 is plasma and 3/4 interstitial.
- **Edema** is clinically evident excess interstitial fluid.
- **Five mechanisms:**
  1. ↑ **capillary hydrostatic pressure**
  2. **Inadequate lymphatic drainage**
  3. ↓ **plasma oncotic pressure**
  4. **Endothelial damage** or ↑ permeability
  5. ↑ **interstitial oncotic pressure**
- **Reduced effective arterial blood volume** drives most generalized edema. It comes from low cardiac output, low SVR, splanchnic pooling (cirrhosis) or low albumin, and triggers **RAAS, SNS and nonosmotic AVP release → renal Na⁺ and H₂O retention** (Fig 43-1).
  - **Low output:** low-output HF, tamponade, constriction.
  - **Low SVR:** high-output HF, sepsis, **cirrhosis**, AV fistula, **pregnancy**, arterial vasodilators.
- **ANP and BNP** promote natriuresis and vasodilation. They are **raised in HF and cirrhosis but too weak to prevent edema**, and resistance develops.

## Clinical recognition
- **Several kg of weight gain** come before overt edema. **Anasarca** = gross generalized edema; ascites and hydrothorax are special forms.
- **Signs:**
  - **pitting**;
  - an imprint of the stethoscope bell on the chest;
  - tight rings or shoes (worse in the evening);
  - **periorbital puffiness on waking**.

## Specific causes
- **Heart failure:**
  - Venous congestion plus RAAS and SNS activation → Na⁺ retention.
  - **Dependent edema** (legs, worse in the evening; **presacral if bedbound**).
  - **Diagnosis:** ECG, echo, **BNP or NT-proBNP**.
- **Acute glomerulonephritis:**
  - **Hematuria, proteinuria, hypertension**.
  - **Primary renal Na⁺ retention** with **normal or high cardiac output**, unlike most HF.
- **Nephrotic syndrome:**
  - Protein loss ≥3.5 g/d → albumin <3.0 → ↓ oncotic pressure → ↓ effective volume → RAAS.
  - Diffuse, symmetric edema; **periorbital edema worst in the morning**.
  - Causes: GN, diabetic glomerulosclerosis, hypersensitivity.
- **Cirrhosis:**
  - Hepatic outflow obstruction → splanchnic pooling, ↑ hepatic lymph, peripheral vasodilation, ↓ albumin.
  - **↑ Aldosterone** (not metabolized by the failing liver).
  - **Ascites first.** Peripheral edema comes later (low albumin, and tense ascites impedes leg venous return).
- **Nutritional:**
  - Protein-calorie deficiency.
  - **Beriberi heart disease** (peripheral AV shunting → low SVR).
  - **Refeeding edema** (↑ Na intake + **insulin increases tubular Na reabsorption**).
- **Localized edema:**
  - **Venous:** thrombophlebitis, varicose veins, valve failure.
  - **Lymphatic:** chronic lymphangitis, node resection, **filariasis**, primary lymphedema. Lymphedema is intractable (↑ capillary pressure + ↑ interstitial protein).
- **Other:**
  - **Hypothyroid myxedema** (hyaluronic acid deposition).
  - **Graves pretibial myxedema: nonpitting.**
  - Cushing syndrome, pregnancy, estrogens, vasodilators (especially **CCBs**).

## Distribution
- **Legs, evening, presacral if bedbound:** HF.
- **Face and eyelids in the morning:** hypoproteinemia, nephrotic syndrome. Less often trichinosis, allergy, myxedema.
- **One leg or arm(s):** venous or lymphatic obstruction; **paralysis** of that limb.
- **Face, neck and arms only:** **SVC obstruction**.

## Approach
1. **Localized or generalized?** Localized → look for a local venous or lymphatic cause.
2. **Generalized → is albumin <3.0 g/dL?** If yes → cirrhosis, malnutrition or nephrotic syndrome (history, exam, **urinalysis**).
3. **No hypoalbuminemia → is there heart failure** severe enough to explain it?
4. **Check urine output** (oliguria or anuria → renal failure).
