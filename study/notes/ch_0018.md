# Ch 18 — Low Back Pain (high-yield)

## Basics
- A leading cause of disability. **Acute <3 months, chronic >3 months**; duration is the main prognostic factor. About a quarter of acute cases become chronic. Most acute LBP resolves within **6 weeks**.
- Risk factors for chronic LBP: female sex, older age, unemployment, obesity, sedentary lifestyle. Poor outcomes: high disease burden, older age, psychopathology, poor job satisfaction, secondary gain.
- **Imaging correlates poorly with symptoms**: asymptomatic disk herniation is found in 29–43%. Treat the patient, not the MRI.

## Pain types (Table 18-1)
| | Nociceptive | Neuropathic | Nociplastic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Examples | Degenerative spondylosis, myofascial | **Herniated disk, spinal stenosis** | "Nonspecific LBP," central sensitization |
| Quality | Aching, deep | Sharp, shooting, lancinating | Diffuse, often neuropathic descriptors |
| Pattern | Referred into the leg, proximal, **nondermatomal** | **Dermatomal** | Outside anatomic patterns, diffuse tenderness |
| Deficits | Uncommon | Sensory common, motor frequent | Nondermatomal; pain-induced weakness |
| Other | Worse with activities that stress the structure | Stenosis eased by **forward flexion** | Very high distress, sleep problems, POTS, other pain syndromes |

## Etiologies (Table 18-3)
| Source | Clues | Diagnosis | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Myofascial** | Tenderness ± trigger points, normal neuro exam | Clinical | NSAIDs, muscle relaxants, duloxetine, topicals; trigger point injection (injectate doesn't matter) |
| **Discogenic** (26–42% of axial) | **Worse sitting or bending forward**, Valsalva; more often bilateral; midline tenderness | Discography (high false-positive rate) | NSAIDs, duloxetine; IDET or biacuplasty (mixed evidence) |
| **Facet** (10–15%, rises with age) | Unilateral paraspinal pain, morning stiffness, **better sitting** | **Medial branch blocks** (only way to establish it) | **RFA of medial branches** after a positive block (≥50% relief) |
| **SI joint** | **Below L5**, unilateral, may refer to the **groin** (mimics hip); ≥3 positive provocation tests | **Low-volume intraarticular injection** = reference standard | Injection; lateral branch RFA (extraarticular); fusion if refractory |
| **Vertebrogenic** | Compression fracture (T11–L2 most common); endplate (Modic 1/2) changes | MRI (acuity), plain films | Acute (<6 wk) severe fracture → **vertebroplasty or kyphoplasty can be considered** (conflicting evidence); basivertebral nerve RFA for Modic changes |
| **Herniated disk** | Dermatomal leg pain; peak age 30–50; inciting event in 38–56%; L5–S1 most common | MRI or CT; EMG or SNRB to confirm | NSAIDs, PT, **ESI (best in the acute phase)**; most improve within 3 mo; >2/3 retract within 2 y |
| **Spinal stenosis** (~11%) | **Neurogenic claudication** (sens 88%, low specificity), relief with sitting or leaning forward (**shopping cart sign**), wide-based gait, worse with extension; **SLR usually negative**; L4–5 most often | MRI or CT | NSAIDs, PT, ESI (short-term); pathology **progresses** |

Most compression fractures improve in 6–8 weeks, but up to 40% become chronic. Fractures with nerve root or cord involvement need surgical consultation.

## Exam
| Test | Use |
|---|---|
| **Straight leg raise** | ~80% **sensitive** for L5/S1; poor for roots above L4 and for stenosis |
| **Crossed SLR** | **>85% specific**, low sensitivity → best for ruling herniation IN |
| Femoral stretch (prone, hip extension) | ~50% sensitive for L2–L4 |
| SI provocation: FABER/Patrick, Gaenslen, thigh thrust, compression, distraction | **≥3 positive**: sensitivity potentially ≥90%, specificity ≥80% |
| Freiberg, FAIR, Pace, Beatty | Piriformis / deep gluteal syndrome |
| Schober (<5 cm increase) | Reduced flexion; **nonspecific** for ankylosing spondylitis |
| Hoover, tripod sign, **Waddell signs** | Nonorganic; predict treatment failure |

- **Reflexes:** patellar = **L4** (sometimes L2–3); Achilles = **S1**. These are the most objective sign, but about a third of older adults have absent reflexes.
- **Stenosis predictors:** older age, positive treadmill test, positive Romberg, pain gone with sitting, perineal numbness.
- **Suspected cauda equina:** perianal sensation + rectal tone → **urgent MRI**.

## Red flags (Table 18-4)
| Flag | Think |
|---|---|
| Age ≤18 | Congenital defect, tumor, spondylolysis or spondylolisthesis |
| Age >50 | Tumor, fracture, **AAA** |
| IV drug use, immunocompromise | Infection |
| Anticoagulation, recent spine procedure | Hematoma (± infection after a procedure) |
| Trauma | Fracture, hematoma |
| Cancer history, weight loss | Tumor |
| Fever, night sweats; pain at rest or night | Infection or tumor |
| **Saddle anesthesia, incontinence, sexual dysfunction, rapidly progressive deficit, low rectal tone** | **Cauda equina syndrome** |

Red flags are very common (92.6% of LBP patients have at least one; night pain 58%). Having flags predicts emergencies, but their **absence doesn't meaningfully lower the risk**.

## Imaging and tests
- **Acute LBP:** image only for red flags or serious or progressive neuro deficits.
- **Chronic:** MRI case by case, mainly before invasive treatment. MRI before ESI didn't improve outcomes.
- **MRI** = gold standard for soft tissue and herniation. **CT** is better for bone (>90% sensitive for most lumbar pathology). **Plain films** for scoliosis, spondylolisthesis and fractures (MRI for acuity).
- **EMG:** sensitivity 36–64% without focal signs, 51–86% with them; specificity 50–60%.
- **Transitional anatomy** (sacralization or lumbarization, 15–35%) is associated with more back pain.

## Treatment
- **First line:** nonpharmacologic: PT, exercise, education, heat, massage, CBT. **Acute LBP: OTC analgesics + PT and education usually suffice; no imaging needed.** Early return to activity (within 2 wk) is recommended, though long-term data are mixed.
- **Drugs:** **NSAIDs, nonbenzodiazepine muscle relaxants, duloxetine or TCAs.**
  - **Acetaminophen is no longer first line** (unlikely to help).
  - **Gabapentinoids:** insufficient evidence (harms: dizziness, somnolence, gait problems, esp. in the elderly).
  - **Opioids:** no long-term benefit. Short course only for severe acute pain, at the lowest dose. **Never combine with benzodiazepines** (respiratory depression).
- **Psychological** (depression in 33–67% of chronic LBP): CBT, mindfulness, biofeedback; no single therapy is superior; short-term benefit is stronger than long-term.
- **Integrative:** acupuncture, manipulation and massage give modest, short-lived benefit.
- **Interdisciplinary programs** give the best outcomes.

## Surgery
- **Decompression** (diskectomy or laminectomy): for **severe or progressive neuro deficits** or refractory radicular pain.
  - **Herniation:** faster short-term relief; small benefit after 1 y.
  - **Stenosis:** small benefit that fades after 2 y.
- Adding fusion to decompression without instability → more complications, no added benefit.
- **Fusion:** strongest evidence in **spondylolisthesis or instability**.
- Disk arthroplasty is an alternative for 1–2 level discogenic pain.
- Adjacent segment disease is more likely after fusion.

## Next best step
| Scenario | Next step |
|---|---|
| Acute LBP, no red flags | NSAID/OTC analgesic, activity, PT; **no imaging** |
| Saddle anesthesia, retention, low rectal tone | **Urgent MRI** + surgical consult |
| Radicular pain from herniation failing conservative care | ESI |
| Progressive motor deficit | Surgical decompression |
| Positive medial branch block | Medial branch RFA |
| Suspected SI pain, ≥3 positive tests | Intraarticular SI injection (diagnostic + therapeutic) |
| Acute osteoporotic fracture, severe pain at <6 wk | Consider vertebroplasty or kyphoplasty [verify: guideline may have changed since this edition] |
| On opioid + benzodiazepine | Stop the combination |
