# Ch 24 — Dizziness and Vertigo (high-yield)

## Framework
- **Dizziness** is imprecise. Patients' words don't reliably indicate the cause, so focus on **timing, triggers and associated symptoms**.
- **Three questions:**
  1. Is it **dangerous** (arrhythmia, TIA, stroke)?
  2. Is it **vestibular**?
  3. If vestibular, is it **peripheral or central**?
- **Vertigo** means an acute *asymmetry* of vestibular input.
  - **Symmetric bilateral loss** causes imbalance and **oscillopsia**, not vertigo.
  - Slowly progressive unilateral loss is **compensated**, so there is no vertigo (schwannoma).
- **Presyncopal dizziness** (arrhythmia, orthostasis, drugs): consider syncope (Ch 23) for brief or upright-provoked episodes.

## Timing
| Duration | Think |
|---|---|
| Seconds, positional | **BPPV**, orthostatic hypotension |
| Minutes | **Posterior circulation TIA** (also migraine) |
| Hours | **Vestibular migraine, Ménière disease** |
| Days, continuous | **Vestibular neuritis** vs. **cerebellar or brainstem stroke** |

## Table 24-1 — peripheral vs. central
| Finding | Peripheral | Central |
|---|---|---|
| Nystagmus direction | **Unidirectional horizontal**, fast phase *away* from the lesion | **Changes direction with gaze**; **downbeat** |
| Pure vertical or pure torsional nystagmus | No (mixed vertical-torsional only in BPPV) | **Yes** |
| Visual fixation | **Suppresses** nystagmus | Doesn't suppress |
| Head impulse test (acute prolonged vertigo) | **Abnormal** (catch-up saccade) | **Normal → suspect central** |
| Hearing | **Unilateral loss**, aural fullness, tinnitus | Rarely unilateral loss |
| Other signs | None | **Diplopia, dysarthria, limb ataxia**, numbness, vertical skew on cover test, dysmetric saccades, poor pursuit |

- **Nystagmus easily seen in room light** is probably central, unless the patient has acute vestibular neuritis.
- **Frenzel goggles** remove fixation and unmask peripheral nystagmus.

## Bedside tests
- **Head impulse test:** quick ~20° head turn while the patient fixates.
  - A **catch-up saccade** after rotation *toward* the weak side means a deficient VOR.
  - Saccades in both directions mean bilateral loss.
- **Dix-Hallpike:**
  - Sit with the head turned 45°, then lie back with the head extended ~20°.
  - **Transient upbeat-torsional nystagmus** = posterior canal BPPV (upper poles beat toward the lower, affected ear).
  - Repeat on the other side if no nystagmus after 15–20 s.
- **Dynamic visual acuity:** losing more than 1 line with 1–2 Hz head movement = vestibular dysfunction.

## Ancillary testing
- **Audiometry** whenever a vestibular disorder is suspected.
  - **Low-frequency loss = Ménière disease.**
  - Unilateral sensorineural loss = peripheral cause (schwannoma).
- **Calorics** compare the horizontal canals; **video head-impulse testing** tests all 6 canals; VEMPs test the otoliths.
- **Unexplained unilateral sensorineural hearing loss or vestibular hypofunction → MRI of the internal auditory canals with gadolinium** (schwannoma).
- **Suspected central cause → brain MRI** and full neurologic evaluation.

## Disorders
**Vestibular neuritis (acute prolonged vertigo)**
- Constant vertigo, nausea and vomiting, oscillopsia and imbalance, **even with the head still**.
- Key question: is it central (stroke or hemorrhage)? Look for diplopia, weakness, numbness, dysarthria.
- **A normal head impulse test makes a peripheral lesion unlikely.**
- **Older patients with vascular risk factors: evaluate for stroke even with no central signs.**
- **Treatment:**
  - Most recover spontaneously.
  - Glucocorticoids: benefit uncertain.
  - **Antivirals: no proven benefit** unless Ramsay Hunt.
  - **Vestibular suppressants for the first few days only;** later they impede compensation.
  - Resume activity early; **vestibular rehabilitation**.

**BPPV**
- Episodes **under 1 min (typically 15–20 s)**, always triggered by a change in head position: lying down, rising, looking up, **rolling over in bed** (separates it from orthostatic hypotension).
- **Mechanism:** otoconia from the utricle migrate, usually into the **posterior canal**. The horizontal canal is less common; the superior canal is rare.
- **Treatment:** **Epley maneuver** (Fig 24-1).
  1. Turn the head 45° toward the affected ear.
  2. Lie back to head-hanging for ≥30 s, until the nystagmus stops.
  3. Turn the head 90° to the other side for 30 s.
  4. Roll onto that side with the nose 45° down for 30 s.
  5. Sit up.
  - Repeat to confirm. Teach the home variant for refractory cases.

**Vestibular migraine**
- Common and **underdiagnosed**.
- Vertigo lasting minutes to hours, **often without headache**.
- Motion and visual-motion sensitivity; photophobia, phonophobia, aura.
- **Treatment:** migraine prophylaxis drugs; antiemetics for attacks.

**Ménière disease**
- Attacks of vertigo + **low-frequency hearing loss** + ear fullness, pressure or pain.
- Hearing improves between attacks but may become permanent. Due to **endolymphatic hydrops**.
- **Treatment:** refer to ENT.
  1. **Diuretics + sodium restriction**.
  2. Then intratympanic **glucocorticoid or gentamicin**.
  3. Then endolymphatic sac surgery.
  4. Ablation (nerve section, labyrinthectomy) is seldom needed.

**Vestibular schwannoma**
- **Slowly progressive unilateral sensorineural hearing loss**, usually **no vertigo** (compensated).
- Head impulse is abnormal toward the affected side; nystagmus isn't prominent.
- **Diagnosis:** MRI of the internal auditory canals with gadolinium.

**Bilateral vestibular hypofunction**
- No vertigo. **Imbalance worse in the dark** and **oscillopsia with head movement**.
- **Causes:**
  - **aminoglycosides (gentamicin), the most common iatrogenic cause**;
  - idiopathic, neurodegenerative;
  - NF2 (bilateral schwannomas);
  - autoimmune, superficial siderosis, meningeal infection or tumor;
  - polyneuropathy;
  - sequential neuritis or Ménière.
- **Exam:** ↓ dynamic visual acuity, abnormal head impulse in **both** directions, **Romberg sign**, reduced calorics.
- **Treatment:** **vestibular rehabilitation. Avoid suppressants** (they worsen imbalance).

**Central vestibular disorders**
- Stroke or hemorrhage, MS, tumor, neurodegeneration, paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration.
- **Acute central vertigo is an emergency → brain MRI** + neurology.

**Persistent postural-perceptual dizziness (PPPD)**
- **≥3 months** of fluctuating dizziness, worse standing, provoked by complex visual environments (supermarkets).
- Often follows an acute vestibular event; exam and tests are normal or show a compensated deficit.
- **Treatment:** **SSRIs, CBT, vestibular rehabilitation**; avoid suppressants.

## Vestibular suppressants (Table 24-2)
- Reserve for **short-term** active vertigo: the first days of neuritis, acute Ménière attacks.
- They hinder central compensation.
- Benzodiazepines may help psychosomatic dizziness with anxiety, but **SSRIs are preferred**.
