# Ch 12 — Ethical Issues in Clinical Medicine (high-yield)

## The principles in exam form
- **Respect for patients / autonomy:** honest information, informed and voluntary consent, respect informed refusals, protect confidentiality.
- **Beneficence:** fiduciary duty — patient's interests come before the physician's or third parties' (hospital, insurer). "First do no harm" alone gives limited guidance, because most useful treatments carry risk.
- **Justice:** fairness; no discrimination (race, religion, gender, sexual orientation, disability, age); fair allocation of scarce resources.

## Disclosure and consent
- Don't withhold or blur a serious diagnosis out of fear of upsetting the patient; honesty builds autonomy and trust.
- Deliver bad news at the patient's pace, with empathy, hope and support (spiritual care, social work).
- A patient **may choose** not to hear details or may delegate decisions to a surrogate. Ask the patient, not the family.
- Informed consent = understandable disclosure plus understanding, not a signature on a form.
- **Emergency exception:** treat without consent if the patient can't consent and waiting for a surrogate would jeopardize life or health. Patients are presumed to want emergency care unless they previously said otherwise (e.g., a **POLST**).
- Patients **cannot demand** interventions with no physiologic rationale, that already failed, or that contradict evidence-based practice.

## Decision-making capacity
- Capacity = can express a choice and appreciate the situation, the nature, risks and benefits of the options, and the consequences, with the choice consistent with their values (not driven by delusions or misinformation).
- **Physicians determine capacity** for a specific decision; **courts determine legal competence**.
- **Disagreeing or refusing ≠ lacking capacity.** Probe the reasoning for misunderstandings.
- **Fluctuating or reversible impairment** (e.g., delirium) → postpone non-urgent decisions until capacity returns.
- Tools: validated assessment instruments, psychiatry or ethics consultation.

## Surrogates
1. A **designated health care proxy** (advance directive or POLST) is respected first.
2. If none: family, in a priority order set by **state statute**.
3. **Unrepresented patient** (no proxy, no family or friends) → a fair, careful **institutional or legal process**.
- Surrogates use the patient's values, goals and prior wishes. Current best interest can sometimes override earlier statements: when the benefit is large, the prior statement doesn't fit the situation, or the patient gave leeway.

## When refusal collides with best interest
- Example: a young asthmatic refusing intubation for readily reversible respiratory failure. Just accepting it is "morally constricted." **Elicit concerns, correct misunderstandings, try to persuade**, use institutional resources. If he is informed and still refuses, **his informed choice prevails**.

## Confidentiality
- Can be overridden to prevent serious harm to the patient or third parties when the risk is **serious and probable**, nothing less restrictive works, and the harms of disclosure are minimized.
- Reporting laws cited: **TB, STIs, child or elder abuse, domestic violence**. [verify: adult IPV reporting mandates vary by state]

## Cost and access
- Patients may not fill prescriptions they can't afford → **ask about cost** at follow-up, choose affordable effective drugs, enlist pharmacists or social workers, and appeal for coverage of non-formulary drugs when justified.
- Guidelines serve most patients; advocate for justified exceptions.

## Allocation of scarce resources
- Policies: fair, transparent, accountable, proportionate. Prioritize people facing large irreversible harm without treatment; save the most lives; reduce disparities.
- **First-come, first-served is often unfair.** Never weigh race, gender, disability, insurance or wealth.
- **Avoid ad hoc bedside rationing** by individual physicians; follow institutional or public policy.

## Professional issues
| Situation | Correct action |
|---|---|
| Conscientious objection (contraception, abortion, aid in dying) | Don't obstruct care; arrange another clinician or a referral; notify patients **in advance** |
| Refusing patients for sexual orientation, gender identity, immigration status, race, disability | Never acceptable |
| Request for a stimulant for an exam, an unjustified disability form, excess opioids | Explore the reasons; if it's deceptive, unfair, or against the patient's interest → **decline and explain** |
| Medical error | **Disclose, explain, apologize**, offer compensation, improve the system (non-punitive) |
| Impaired colleague | Protect patients; **report to the clinical supervisor or director** |
| Trainee doing exams or procedures | Get the patient's **consent for trainee participation**, especially intimate exams (pelvic, rectal, breast, testicular), even under anesthesia; introduce trainees honestly |
| Drug-company gifts | Many centers ban them, even pens and meals; Open Payments makes payments public |
| Fee-for-service or quality incentives | Recommend what's in the patient's interest, "no more and no less" |
| Request for an unproven stem cell or gene "therapy" | Explain the lack of evidence and the risks; refer to a well-designed trial |
| Research | IRB approval plus informed, voluntary consent; extra safeguards for vulnerable participants |

## Most likely right answer (exam heuristics)
| Stem | Pick |
|---|---|
| Competent, informed patient refuses | Respect the refusal |
| Refusal based on a misconception | Explore and correct it, then re-discuss |
| Delirious patient, non-urgent decision | Defer until capacity returns |
| Unconscious patient, emergency, no surrogate, no known objection | Treat |
| Family asks you to hide the diagnosis | Ask the patient what they want to know |
| Proxy and relatives disagree | The designated proxy decides, guided by the patient's wishes |
| No one to decide | Institutional or legal process (not the physician alone) |

## Classic traps
- Calling psychiatry "to declare incompetent" just because the patient disagrees.
- Seeking a court order as the first step. Physicians assess capacity; courts are rarely needed.
- Letting the family override a capable patient or a designated proxy.
- Hiding an error to "avoid liability."
