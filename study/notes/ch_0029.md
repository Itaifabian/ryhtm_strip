# Ch 29 — Confusion and Delirium (high-yield)

## Definition and features
- **Delirium** is an acute decline in cognition that **fluctuates over hours to days**.
- **Hallmark: a deficit of attention.** All other domains (memory, executive, visuospatial, language) may be involved.
- **Sometimes present:** altered sleep-wake cycle, hallucinations or delusions, affect changes, autonomic instability. Worse at night (**sundowning**) is typical but not required.
- **It is a clinical, bedside diagnosis.**
- **Subtypes:**
  - **Hyperactive:** e.g., delirium tremens, with agitation, hallucinations and autonomic instability.
  - **Hypoactive:** e.g., benzodiazepine intoxication, with apathy and psychomotor slowing. **Most often missed.**
  - Many patients are mixed or fluctuate between the two.
- **Assume any acutely confused patient is delirious.**

## Epidemiology and prognosis
| Setting | Rate |
|---|---|
| Hospitalized (age >65 or baseline cognitive impairment) | Approaches 50% |
| Hospitalized overall | 10% to >50% (higher after hip surgery) |
| Older ICU patients | Approaches 75% |
| Nursing homes | ~25% |
| End of life | 50–80% |

- **Unrecognized in up to one-third** of delirious inpatients.
- **In-hospital mortality 25–33%**, similar to sepsis.
- **5-fold higher mortality in the following months**, plus longer stays, more nursing home placement, readmission, recurrence and cognitive decline.
- In the ICU, delirium should be treated as **organ dysfunction**.

## Risk factors
- **Strongest:** **older age** and **baseline cognitive dysfunction**. At least two-thirds of delirium occurs in patients with dementia.
- **Predisposing:** hearing or vision impairment, immobility, malnutrition, underlying illness.
- **In hospital:**
  - **physical restraints, bladder catheter**;
  - sleep or sensory deprivation;
  - **≥3 new medications**.
- **Surgical:** cardiopulmonary bypass, too little or too much pain control, possibly inhaled anesthetics and benzodiazepines.
- **Dementia with Lewy bodies** mimics delirium (fluctuation, visual hallucinations, parkinsonism) and is especially vulnerable to it.
- **"Stress test for the brain":** delirium after a minor insult in a previously healthy person → look for an unrecognized neurodegenerative or diffuse brain disease. **Screen as an outpatient after recovery.**

## Pathogenesis
- Diffuse cortical and subcortical dysfunction. **EEG shows symmetric slowing** (nonspecific).
- Rare focal causes: **right (nondominant) parietal** and **medial dorsal thalamic** strokes.
- **Acetylcholine deficiency** (anticholinergic drugs; AD, DLB and PD dementia have cholinergic loss).
- **Dopamine excess** (dopaminergic drugs in PD cause hallucinations and confusion).

## Diagnosis
- **Confusion Assessment Method (CAM):** (1) acute onset and fluctuating course **AND** (2) inattention, **plus either** (3) disorganized thinking **or** (4) altered level of consciousness. Use **CAM-ICU** in the ICU.
- **Three most important history items:**
  1. **Baseline cognition** (from a collateral source)
  2. **Time course**
  3. **Medications**, including OTC, herbal, and dose or generic changes
- **Medications cause ~one-third of delirium.**
- **Exam:**
  - Look for infection (fever, consolidation, murmur, meningismus) and fluid status.
  - Skin: jaundice, cyanosis, track marks.
  - **Asterixis or multifocal myoclonus → metabolic or toxic cause.**
- **Attention test: digit span forward.** Normal is 5–7 digits. **≤4 indicates inattention** (barring hearing or language barriers); many delirious patients manage ≤3.
- **The MMSE is unreliable in delirium** because inattention spoils serial subtraction and spelling "world" backward.
- **A single morning assessment can miss sundowning.**

## Causes (Table 29-1)
- **Toxins and drugs:**
  - **Prescription:** **anticholinergics, narcotics, benzodiazepines**.
  - **Abuse:** alcohol intoxication and **withdrawal**, opiates and fentanyl, MDMA, LSD, GHB, PCP and ketamine, cocaine, bath salts, cannabis and synthetic cannabis.
  - **Poisons:** inhalants, **CO**, ethylene glycol, pesticides.
- **Metabolic:**
  - glucose (hypo or hyper);
  - **sodium, calcium, magnesium** derangements;
  - liver or renal failure, hypercarbia, hypoxemia;
  - **thiamine or B12** deficiency;
  - thyroid or adrenal disorders;
  - CNS vasculitis.
- **Infection:** **UTI**, pneumonia, cellulitis, sepsis (septic encephalopathy is cytokine-mediated). CNS infection and autoimmune or paraneoplastic encephalitis are less common but must not be missed.
- **Vascular:** usually global hypoperfusion (heart failure, shock, dehydration, anemia). Focal strokes rarely.
- **Seizures:** **nonconvulsive status** or postictal states. **EEG is essential whenever the cause stays unclear.**
- **Hospital environment:** a diagnosis of exclusion, usually part of a multifactorial picture.
- **Alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal:** consider in **everyone**, including the elderly. Even a few drinks a day can cause significant withdrawal on admission.

## Stepwise workup (Table 29-2)
1. **Everyone:** history (medications), exam, **CBC, electrolytes with Ca/Mg/phosphorus, LFTs with albumin, renal function**.
2. **First tier, guided by findings:**
   - **UA and culture, CXR and respiratory pathogen testing, blood cultures** (infection screen, especially in the elderly);
   - ECG, ABG;
   - **toxicology screen (earlier in young patients)**;
   - **brain MRI with diffusion and gadolinium** (preferred) or CT.
3. **Second tier:**
   - **LP after imaging** if CNS infection or inflammation is suspected;
   - **EEG** (immediately if seizure suspicion is high);
   - TSH and free T4, cortisol;
   - ANA, complement, ANCA, autoimmune or paraneoplastic encephalitis panels (serum and CSF);
   - RPR, HIV, fungal and viral serologies.
- **A clear precipitant (e.g., an offending drug) → further testing may not be needed.**
- Imaging is often unhelpful. CT finds big masses and bleeds; **MRI is the test of choice**.
- **Consider LP in any delirium with a negative workup.**

## Treatment
- **Treat the cause** (antibiotics, careful electrolyte correction). Treating symptoms blindly prolongs delirium and masks clues.
- **Supportive care (Fig 29-1 checklist):**
  - **Daytime:** shades up and lights on, date and staff names on a board, out of bed to a chair for all meals, walk 3 times a day, water within reach, **glasses and hearing aids on**, activities, involve family.
  - **Night:** dark and quiet, **cluster nighttime tasks**, eye mask and earplugs, less caffeine; **melatonin** may be considered.
  - **Remove "tethers"** (nasal cannula, Foley, telemetry, continuous pulse oximetry) when not truly needed.
  - Home bedding and objects, family visits; nutrition, volume, pain, continence, skin.
- **Agitation threatening safety:**
  - **Bed alarms and sitters are better than physical restraints.**
  - **Low-dose antipsychotic as needed** only as a last resort for severe agitation. They **don't prevent or treat delirium** and raise mortality in the elderly.
- **Benzodiazepines worsen confusion.** Use them only for **alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal**.

## Prevention
- Screen high-risk patients, then run protocols for sleep, mobility, vision, hearing, sleep deprivation and dehydration.
- **No drug proven:** cholinesterase inhibitors and antipsychotics failed; melatonin and ramelteon are promising.
- **ICU:** **dexmedetomidine** (less deliriogenic) and **daily sedation interruption** with reorientation.

## Next best step
| Scenario | Next step |
|---|---|
| Acute confusion, unknown baseline | Collateral history (baseline, time course, drugs) |
| Elderly delirium, no obvious cause | CBC, CMP with Ca/Mg/phosphorus, LFT; UA and culture, CXR, ± blood cultures |
| Young delirious patient | Toxicology screen early |
| Delirium + fever + meningismus | Imaging, then immediate LP |
| Fluctuating delirium, workup negative | EEG (nonconvulsive status) |
| Agitated delirium pulling lines | Sitter or bed alarm; antipsychotic only if dangerous |
| Hospital day 2–3, tremor, hallucinations, autonomic instability | Treat alcohol withdrawal: benzodiazepine |
| Delirium on a new anticholinergic | Stop it; further tests may be unnecessary |
| Recovered delirium after a minor insult | Outpatient screening for underlying neurodegeneration |
| ICU sedation | Dexmedetomidine; daily awakening |
