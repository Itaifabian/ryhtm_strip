# Ch 16 — Abdominal Pain (high-yield)

## Core rules
- How severe the pain is doesn't track how severe the disease is. A catastrophe can present with subtle findings, especially in the obtunded, elderly, immunosuppressed, or psychotic patient.
- Most common causes on admission: **nonspecific abdominal pain, appendicitis, urologic pain, intestinal obstruction**.
- **The time course of symptoms matters more than where the pain is.**
- **Don't withhold analgesia** while waiting for a diagnosis. Adequate analgesia rarely hides the diagnosis.
- **Consider intrathoracic disease in every abdominal pain**, especially upper abdominal (MI, pneumonia, PE, pericarditis, esophageal disease).
- Pelvic and rectal exams are mandatory: pelvic peritonitis can have no abdominal signs.

## Pain patterns by mechanism
| Mechanism | Character | Clues |
|---|---|---|
| Parietal peritonitis | Steady, localized | Lies still; worse with cough or movement; reflex spasm. Acid/pancreatic juice hurts more than blood, urine or feces |
| Hollow-viscus obstruction | Colicky, poorly localized | SBO: periumbilical, colic fades as bowel dilates. Colon: infraumbilical, may radiate to the lumbar region |
| Biliary distension | **Steady** ("colic" is a misnomer) | Gallbladder: RUQ → right scapula tip. CBD: epigastric → upper lumbar |
| Pancreatic duct | Like CBD | Worse lying flat, relieved by sitting up |
| Ureter | Severe colic, restless | UPJ → CVA. Distal (intravesical) ureter → penis, scrotum, inner thigh |
| Bladder distension | Dull suprapubic | In the obtunded patient, restlessness may be the only sign |
| **SMA occlusion** | Mild diffuse or crampy pain for 2–3 days, then collapse | **Pain out of proportion to the exam** (hyperperistalsis, no peritonitis yet) |
| Rupturing AAA | Pain → **sacrum, flank, genitalia** | May precede rupture by days |
| Abdominal wall | Constant, worse with movement or standing | Anticoagulated patient + lower-quadrant mass → **rectus sheath hematoma** |

## Referred and other causes
- **Thoracic referral:** splinting with respiratory lag. Apparent spasm **eases on inspiration**. Palpation doesn't worsen the pain (it may relieve it).
- **Spinal root / zoster:** lancinating, dermatomal, hyperesthesia, worse with spine movement, unrelated to food.
- **Metabolic:** DKA, uremia, porphyria, lead (mimic obstruction through hyperperistalsis), hyperlipidemia (via pancreatitis), C1-esterase deficiency, FMF, adrenal crisis.
  - **DKA trap:** appendicitis or obstruction can trigger DKA. If the pain doesn't resolve once the metabolic derangement is corrected, look for an organic cause.
- **Black widow bite:** abdominal *and back* rigidity.
- **Immunocompromised:** blunted signs. CMV, mycobacteria, fungi; splenic abscess (Candida, Salmonella) → LUQ pain; acalculous cholecystitis (CMV, cryptosporidium in AIDS); **typhlitis** after chemo; GVHD.
- **IBS:** a clinical diagnosis after excluding structural disease. Stress-triggered, variable location, nausea and vomiting rare.

## Location → differential (Table 16-3)
| RUQ | Epigastric | LUQ |
|---|---|---|
| Cholecystitis, cholangitis, pancreatitis, pneumonia/empyema, pleurisy, subdiaphragmatic abscess, hepatitis, Budd-Chiari | PUD, gastritis, GERD, pancreatitis, **MI**, pericarditis, ruptured AAA, esophagitis | Splenic infarct, rupture or abscess; gastritis, gastric ulcer, pancreatitis, subdiaphragmatic abscess |

| RLQ | Periumbilical | LLQ |
|---|---|---|
| Appendicitis, salpingitis, inguinal hernia, **ectopic**, stone, IBD, mesenteric adenitis, typhlitis | Early appendicitis, gastroenteritis, obstruction, ruptured AAA | Diverticulitis, salpingitis, hernia, ectopic, stone, IBS, IBD |

**Diffuse:** gastroenteritis, **mesenteric ischemia**, obstruction, IBS, peritonitis, diabetes, malaria, FMF, metabolic, psychiatric.

**Extra-abdominal causes (Table 16-2):** MI, myocarditis, pericarditis, CHF, lower-lobe pneumonia, PE, pneumothorax, esophageal disease, **testicular torsion**, herpes zoster, tabes, lead, black widow, narcotic withdrawal, heat stroke.

## Exam
- Test for rebound with **gentle percussion or by asking the patient to cough**, not deep palpation with sudden release.
- Auscultation is one of the **least useful** parts of the exam: strangulation or perforation can occur with normal bowel sounds. A truly silent abdomen = sudden severe chemical peritonitis.

## Tests
- **WBC never decides surgery.** WBC >20,000 can occur with perforation, but also with pancreatitis, cholecystitis, PID or infarction. A normal WBC doesn't exclude perforation. Anemia is often more informative.
- **Amylase** also rises with perforated ulcer, strangulation and cholecystitis. It doesn't rule surgery in or out.
- **Nontraumatic acute pain with unclear indication → low-dose CT**, preferred over plain films.
- **Suspected colonic obstruction:** avoid oral barium; a contrast enema can be diagnostic (if no perforation).
- **Ultrasound:** gallstones, enlarged gallbladder or pancreas, ovary, tubal pregnancy.
- **HIDA:** separates cholecystitis or biliary colic from pancreatitis.
- **Laparoscopy:** diagnoses and treats pelvic disease, ectopic pregnancy, appendicitis.
- Peritoneal lavage has been replaced by CT and laparoscopy (outside trauma).

## Next best step
| Scenario | Next step |
|---|---|
| Exsanguinating intraabdominal hemorrhage (ruptured AAA, hypotensive) | IV access → **straight to OR**; don't wait for imaging |
| Unclear acute nontraumatic pain | Low-dose CT |
| Upper abdominal pain + cardiac risk | ECG / evaluate for MI |
| Suspected colonic obstruction, no perforation | Contrast enema (not oral barium) |
| DKA corrected, pain persists | Look for an organic cause (appendicitis, obstruction) |
| Diagnosis uncertain, stable | Watchful waiting with repeated exams |
| Severe pain pending surgical evaluation | Give analgesia now |

## Classic traps
- "Acute abdomen" isn't a diagnosis.
- Pain with a benign exam in an elderly AF patient = mesenteric ischemia until proven otherwise.
- A pregnancy-related WBC rise can be physiologic; a gravid uterus displaces anatomy.
