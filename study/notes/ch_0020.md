# Ch 20 — Fever (high-yield)

## Definitions
- Normal core temperature: 36.5–37.5°C. Mean oral 36.6°C. **Fever = oral temp >37.7°C (>99.9°F)**, the 99th percentile in healthy adults.
- Lowest around 8 a.m. and highest around 4 p.m.
- **Rectal reads ~0.4°C higher than oral.** Unadjusted tympanic reads ~0.8°C lower than rectal. Lower-esophageal temp ≈ core temp.
- Use the **same site** when following a fever over time.
- Ovulation raises the morning temp by ~0.6°C until menses.
- **Hyperpyrexia >41.5°C:** severe infection, but **most often CNS hemorrhage** (CNS cytokines).
- Hypothalamic damage usually causes **low**, not high, temperatures.

## Fever vs hyperthermia
| | Fever | Hyperthermia (heat stroke) |
|---|---|---|
| Set point | **Raised** (e.g., 37 → 39°C) | **Unchanged** |
| Mechanism | Pyrogens → cytokines → PGE2 | Heat gain exceeds heat loss (environment, exertion, drugs) |
| Skin | Can be **cold** (vasoconstriction) | **Hot and dry** (esp. heat stroke or anticholinergic drugs) |
| Antipyretics | Usually lower the temp, even in hyperpyrexia | **Don't work** |
| Clue | Infection or inflammation | Preceding heat exposure or a drug that impairs thermoregulation |

Hyperthermia can kill quickly, so it's worth telling apart from fever, although this is hard in an emergency (sepsis can exceed 40.5°C).

## Pathogenesis (Fig 20-1)
1. **Exogenous pyrogens:** gram-negative endotoxin (LPS, febrile at 2–3 ng/kg) and gram-positive superantigens (TSST-1, enterotoxins, streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins).
2. Monocytes, macrophages and endothelium release **pyrogenic cytokines: IL-1, IL-6, TNF, IFN** (also CNTF). IL-1 and TNF are febrile at 10–100 ng/kg; IL-6 needs μg/kg. IFN-α therapy commonly causes fever.
3. Cytokines act on the **OVLT** (circumventricular organ) endothelium → COX → **PGE2** → **EP-3 receptor** (essential) on glia → **cAMP** (the neurotransmitter) → higher set point.
   - Peripheral PGE2 → myalgias and arthralgias.
   - Toll-like and IL-1 receptors share a signaling pathway.
4. **CNS-made cytokines** bypass the OVLT → hyperpyrexia of CNS hemorrhage, trauma or infection.

Noninfectious triggers include pericarditis, trauma, stroke and vaccines. Disease categories that cause fever (Table 20-1): infection, autoimmune/inflammatory, cancer, medications (vaccines, drug fever), endocrine (hyperthyroidism), hypothalamic.

## Evaluation
- **History:** timeline, sick contacts, vectors.
- **Blunted fever:** newborns, elderly, chronic liver or kidney failure, glucocorticoids, anticytokine drugs, NSAIDs. **Septic shock can cause hypothermia.**
- **Labs:** CBC with a manual (or band-sensitive) differential. Bands, toxic granulation and **Döhle bodies** → bacterial. Neutropenia → some viral infections.
- **CRP and ESR** are the most useful tests for low-grade fever or occult disease. CRP stays elevated while IL-6 fluctuates. Measuring circulating cytokines is not helpful.
- **Anticytokine therapy** (TNF, IL-1, IL-6, IL-12/23, IL-17 blockers): ↑ infection risk (latent TB disseminates on anti-TNF; anti-IL-17 → candidiasis), and fever may be blunted. **Even low-grade fever → early, rigorous workup.**

## Fever patterns
| Pattern | Think |
|---|---|
| **Relative bradycardia** (temp–pulse dissociation) | **Typhoid, brucellosis, leptospirosis**, drug fever, factitious fever |
| Every 3rd day | *P. vivax* |
| Every 4th day | *P. malariae* |
| Days of fever / several days afebrile / relapse | *Borrelia* (relapsing fever) |
| **Pel-Ebstein:** 3–10 days of fever, then 3–10 days afebrile | **Hodgkin** and other lymphomas |
| Every **21 days** with neutropenia | **Cyclic neutropenia** |
| Recurrent fever + neutrophilia + serositis | Autoinflammatory (FMF, Still, HIDS, TRAPS, idiopathic pericarditis, gout) → respond dramatically to **IL-1 blockade (anakinra, canakinumab)** and also to antipyretics |

## Treatment
- **Treating fever does no harm** and doesn't slow recovery from common infections.
- **Withholding antipyretics** can help judge antibiotic response (esp. culture-negative infections) and help diagnose an unusual fever. Routine antipyretics can mask an undertreated infection.
- **Drug choice:**
  - **Acetaminophen preferred.** Aspirin and NSAIDs work equally well but affect platelets and the GI tract.
  - **Children:** acetaminophen or ibuprofen; **no aspirin (Reye syndrome).**
  - Can't take oral drugs: parenteral NSAIDs or rectal suppositories.
- **Mechanisms:**
  - **COX inhibitors** lower brain PGE2. Acetaminophen is oxidized in the brain (P450) and inhibits COX there (± COX-3).
  - **Glucocorticoids** block phospholipase A2 *and* transcription of cytokine mRNA.
  - Antipyretics don't lower normal temperature (PGE2 plays no role in normal thermoregulation).
- **Who must be treated:**
  - Cardiac, pulmonary or CNS impairment: **each 1°C above 37°C raises O2 consumption ~13%.**
  - Children with a history of seizures (febrile or not). The absolute temperature doesn't predict febrile seizures.
- **Hyperpyrexia:** cooling blankets **plus** oral antipyretics, never blankets alone. Cooling protects the brain in CNS hemorrhage or trauma.

## Next best step
| Scenario | Next step |
|---|---|
| Low-grade fever on a TNF inhibitor | Early, rigorous workup (incl. TB) |
| Child with a viral illness and fever | Acetaminophen or ibuprofen; no aspirin |
| Adult needing an antipyretic, GI or bleeding risk | Acetaminophen |
| Culture-negative infection on antibiotics | Consider withholding antipyretics to track response |
| Fever + heart failure or lung disease | Treat the fever |
| Hyperpyrexia | Antipyretic + cooling blanket |
| Heat exposure, hot dry skin, no response to acetaminophen | Hyperthermia (see Ch 478) |
