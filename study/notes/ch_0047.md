# Ch 47 — Dysphagia (high-yield)

## Terms
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Aphagia** | Can't swallow; complete obstruction (food bolus, foreign body) |
| **Odynophagia** | Painful swallowing; **mucosal ulceration** (infection, pills) |
| **Globus** | Lump sensation in the neck that **doesn't interfere with swallowing** and may be relieved by swallowing |
| **Transfer dysphagia** | Nasal regurgitation or aspiration with swallowing; **oropharyngeal** |
| **Phagophobia** | Fear of swallowing |

## Physiology
- **Striated muscle:** oropharynx, UES, cervical esophagus. Innervated by **CN V, VII, XII** (oral) and **IX, X** (pharynx).
- **Smooth muscle:** distal esophagus and LES, run by the myenteric plexus.
  - Excitatory transmitters: **ACh, substance P**. Inhibitory: **VIP, NO**.
- **LES:** tonically closed. It relaxes with **deglutitive inhibition**. The crural diaphragm helps.

## Mechanisms
- **Structural:** bolus too large or lumen too narrow. **Solid dysphagia when lumen <13 mm.**
- **Propulsive (motor):** abnormal peristalsis or sphincter relaxation.
- **Mixed:**
  - **Scleroderma:** absent peristalsis + weak LES → peptic stricture.
  - Head and neck radiation.

## Oropharyngeal dysphagia
- **Signs:** drooling, trouble initiating a swallow, **cough with swallowing, nasal regurgitation**, hoarseness, cranial nerve deficits.
- **Causes:**
  - **Iatrogenic** (head and neck surgery or radiation);
  - **Neurologic** (stroke, Parkinson, ALS);
  - **Structural.**
- **Lateralized pharyngeal dysphagia:** a structural lesion or an ipsilateral brainstem or cranial nerve process. **Unilateral cortical stroke** can also cause it.
- **Zenker diverticulum:**
  - elderly;
  - regurgitation of **undigested food**, aspiration, **halitosis**;
  - pulsion diverticulum above the cricopharyngeus at **Killian dehiscence**, from a poorly opening UES.
- **Cricopharyngeal bar:** common and usually asymptomatic. **Rule out other causes before treating.**
- **Workup:**
  - **Videofluoroscopic swallow study** (rapid-sequence, by a swallow therapist) = test of choice.
  - **Laryngoscopy** for structural lesions or biopsy.

## Esophageal dysphagia
- **Most common structural causes: Schatzki ring, eosinophilic esophagitis, peptic stricture.** GERD can cause dysphagia without a stricture.
- **Motor patterns:**
  - **Achalasia:** absent peristalsis + **failed LES relaxation**.
  - **Distal esophageal spasm:** normal LES, disordered body contractions.
  - **Scleroderma:** absent contractility + **very weak LES**.
- **Striated-muscle disease** (myasthenia, polymyositis) usually presents as oropharyngeal dysphagia.

## History is the key (Fig 47-2)
| Clue | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Nasal regurgitation, coughing with swallowing | Oropharyngeal (also **TE fistula** if severe cough) |
| Localizes to the **chest** | Esophageal |
| Localizes to the suprasternal notch | Either (distal causes refer up ~30% of the time) |
| **Solids only, intermittent** | **Structural** (Schatzki ring, web) |
| **Solids and liquids from onset** | **Motor** (achalasia, spasm) |
| **Progressive over weeks to months** | **Cancer** |
| Episodic solids, stable over years | Schatzki ring, EoE |
| Food impaction | Structural |
| Long heartburn, then dysphagia | **Peptic stricture** (rarely adenocarcinoma) |
| **Odynophagia** | Pill or infectious esophagitis |
| Immunocompromised | **Candida, HSV, CMV**, Kaposi, lymphoma |
| **Atopic young white man (20–40)** | **Eosinophilic esophagitis** |
| Hoarseness *before* dysphagia | Laryngeal primary |
| Hoarseness *after* dysphagia | **Recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement by cancer** |
| Opioid use | Opioid-induced dysmotility |

**Caveats:**
- **Scleroderma:** mild dysphagia for **solids only**, despite being a motor disorder.
- **Oropharyngeal dysphagia** is often **worse with liquids**.

## Exam
- **Oropharyngeal:**
  - bulbar or pseudobulbar signs (dysarthria, dysphonia, ptosis, tongue atrophy);
  - neuromuscular disease;
  - neck masses, nodes;
  - dentition.
- **Esophageal:** exam is less helpful, **except skin** (scleroderma, pemphigoid, lichen planus, epidermolysis bullosa).

## Diagnosis
- **Suspected oropharyngeal → videofluoroscopic swallow study** ± ENT and neurology.
- **Suspected esophageal → upper endoscopy = single most useful test.** It shows mucosa, allows biopsies, and allows **dilation in the same session**.
- **Biopsy the esophagus routinely in unexplained dysphagia**, even if the mucosa looks normal (EoE).
- **Suspected motility disorder:** endoscopy still comes first. **Cancer or inflammation can mimic achalasia ("pseudoachalasia")**. Then **manometry** if endoscopy doesn't explain the symptoms, or to confirm a motor disorder.
- **Barium (± barium tablet):** subtle or complex strictures, prior surgery, diverticula, paraesophageal hernia.
- **FLIP** (impedance planimetry during endoscopy): esophagogastric junction opening; complements manometry.

## Treatment
- **Oropharyngeal:**
  - swallow therapy (postures, maneuvers, **modified food consistency**);
  - post-stroke dysphagia **usually improves in weeks**;
  - severe or persistent → **gastrostomy**, which **doesn't prevent aspiration of saliva or refluxate**.
- **Treat the cause** (myasthenia, polymyositis).
- **Cricopharyngeal myotomy:** usually not helpful, **except symptomatic cricopharyngeal bar, Zenker, oculopharyngeal muscular dystrophy**.
- **Structural esophageal:** **bougie or balloon dilation** for most.
- **Achalasia and cancer:** surgery or endoscopic therapy.
- **Infectious:** antimicrobials or reverse immunosuppression.
- **EoE:**
  - **dietary allergen elimination, PPI, swallowed topical steroids, type 2 biologics**;
  - dilation for persistent strictures.
